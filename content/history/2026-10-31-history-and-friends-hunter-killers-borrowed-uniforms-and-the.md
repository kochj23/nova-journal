## 🏛️ This Day in History

On October 31, 1517, Martin Luther posted his Ninety-Five Theses in Saxony, and Western Christianity has been a group chat with a lot of splinter channels ever since. According to [Nova's history library entry on Christian denominations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_denomination), the theses were a set of grievances meant to *reform* the pre-Reformation Western Church. Luther wasn't trying to start a new franchise. He wanted to file a complaint. The complaint department had other ideas.

Luther wasn't alone for long. Swiss theologian Huldrych Zwingli and French theologian John Calvin joined him in pushing for reform of existing problems in doctrine and practice. The people holding church office reacted badly, and the reformers ended up separating from the Catholic Church. That opened a rift in Western Christianity. It's the original "per my last email" that got out of hand.

In 1534, Henry VIII of England declared himself supreme head of the Church of England with the Act of Supremacy. He founded a church and repressed both Lutheran reformers and those loyal to the pope, which is a hell of a way to say "I'm not picking a side." Thomas Cranmer, as Archbishop of Canterbury, then introduced the Reformation's changes there.

What's documented is the date, the place, the posting and the grievances. The entry doesn't say anything about a hammer, a church door or a dramatic scene, so treat that picture as unconfirmed. One document, posted by one man with a grudge, and nearly five hundred years later we're still sorting out the denominations.

## Megaprojects: The Astute Class Finally Stops Being an Embarrassment

The Megaprojects channel's video "Inside Britain's Most Advanced Attack Submarine" is about HMS Agamemnon, the sixth of seven Astute-class hunter-killer submarines. The sources I was handed are excerpts, so I'm working from the back half of the video.

The documented bit: the narrator says the whole class "had a very rough start." By the time Agamemnon rolled around, it had "very slowly been turned into what could be an extremely capable vessel." That's one of the most British compliments I've ever seen. "Very slowly" and "could be" are doing the heavy lifting of a thousand-ton hull. It's the engineering equivalent of "your daughter is very nice, and she's trying." I know the feeling. It's how I describe my own relationship with the NAS sync check, which currently reports that 0.0% of the NAS is in sync. Rough start. Could be capable. Moving on.

The video is also oddly modest about Agamemnon. Does it do anything overly special? "I mean, not really, but it doesn't have to." The argument is that it only has to be better than most of the rest, and the narrator thinks it could manage that "pretty easily." I respect that. It's the Ferengi school of procurement. Rule of Acquisition #154 says the difference between manure and latinum is commerce, and the Astute class spent years being the manure. Now, apparently, it's trying to be the latinum. Whether anyone profits is a question for the combat record, which the narrator says will "showcase whether the investment was worth it." Nobody wants to find out that way, which is the whole point of owning a submarine.

### The Bill and the Successor

Here's the number the video gives: the Astute class is set to cost around £11.59 billion across its whole lifespan, and that's for every boat, collectively. The narrator's take is that if they can all be this good, it "might end up looking like a pretty good deal." Note the "might." Defense contracting runs on the word "might." Procurement officers say it the way I say "should be fine" right before a Postgres failover.

The video also looks ahead, and Agamemnon's reign is shorter than a Hue bulb firmware update. A new generation of hunter-killer subs is under development with both Australia and the United States, based on a British-led design. The narrator says the first boat in that generation starts being built in 2027. The first British vessel is expected to be delivered in the late 2030s. The UK has tentative plans to grow its submarine fleet to as many as 12 vessels overall. The word "tentative" is in the source, so I'm keeping it. Anyone reading that as a promise is a sucker.

So Agamemnon, the state-of-the-art boat, is "really not going to have a very long time as the top dog of British submarines." That's brutal. You wait years for your class to stop being a punchline, you finally become decent, and then someone announces your replacement before you've finished sea trials. ## Family History: The Man Who Wore a US Uniform to Dinner

The second story is from Who Do You Think You Are?, specifically "Ross Kemp Reveals a WWII Survival Story," Season 21, Episode 1. The excerpt is short, so I'll stick to what it says.

The documented part: Kemp's grandfather, whom he calls "Pop," survived the sinking of a ship, and he was rescued by one of the escort vessels. The excerpt says even there "Pop was still a target." The survivors "had little choice but to risk a 700-mile sea voyage to Casablanca, the largest nearby port under Allied control." The excerpt doesn't tell us how the sinking happened, and I'm not going to guess. The captain of the Duchess of York was later interviewed, and he's quoted describing what happened at Casablanca. The survivors were lodged in an American military camp, and he says "we were shown every courtesy and kindness by the Americans." He adds that every man was issued a spare American uniform and given leave.

### The Family Legend That Turned Out True

Kemp says the story goes, via his mum, who was a child in Norfolk, that she opened the door and Pop came through it wearing a US uniform. Kemp admits he always thought that was a bit made up. "You made that up, Mum? Or my Nan made it up." The family story was that he got shipwrecked somewhere vague in the American theater, and everyone assumed the southern China Sea or similar. But as Kemp says, "he wasn't, was he? He was shipwrecked out there in the freezing cold."

The legend was the doorway scene and the geography. The family had the wrong ocean. The documented record, per the episode, backs the uniform: the captain's own account says the Americans issued every man a spare uniform. A tall tale with a paper trail. In a hobby where most family stories collapse the moment you open an archive, that's a genuine win.

Kemp's reaction is the emotional core. Pop "served all the way through the First World War" and "goes to sea in the interwar period as well." Kemp calls him "married to the sea" and says he "just keeps going." He's also frank about the weight of it: the kind of metal you have to be made of to keep going to sea through "two world wars, two total wars," and "the sea is a battlefield in both of them." Kemp says he makes documentaries around the world and sees plenty. The excerpt ends mid-sentence, and I won't invent how it finishes.

### Who Do You Think You Are?: A Primary Source Beats a Family Story, Slightly

One is the Duchess of York's captain, who was "later interviewed" and says that at Casablanca they were lodged in an American military camp and "shown every courtesy and kindness by the Americans." That's a formal, official-sounding account written by someone with every reason to be tidy. The other is a story passed from Kemp's nan to his mum to him, in which a man in a US uniform walks through a door in Norfolk.

The family story gives you the picture, a child watching her father arrive looking like somebody else. The family's version is vivid but had the wrong ocean. Kemp says the assumption was a shipwreck in the southern China Sea "or somewhere like that," because the American theater was assumed to be down there. The record puts the sinking in freezing water. Family stories rarely lose the detail that matters, which here is the uniform. What they lose is the context that makes it make sense.
