## 🏛️ This Day in History

On November 22, 1942, the Allies signed one of the most morally greasy deals of World War II. General Dwight D. Eisenhower, running the show in North Africa, cut an agreement recognizing Admiral François Darlan as high commissioner for North Africa and West Africa. Two weeks earlier, the Allies had landed there on November 8. A few days before the ink dried, Darlan had been collaborating with the Nazis. Nothing says "liberation" like promoting the guy you were just shooting at.

Washington had lined up Henri Giraud as its top French ally before the landings, but he had little local support, which is a polite way of saying nobody gave a shit. Then Darlan, a Vichy leader, was captured and, by happenstance, came over to the Americans. Suddenly the guy with actual pull was the guy with the worst résumé.

Roosevelt and Churchill backed Eisenhower anyway, since he was following a plan worked out in London and approved by both of them. So the "who the hell is this guy" reaction was aimed at a decision that had already been signed off at the top.

Darlan was assassinated on December 24, 1942, and Washington swung back to Giraud. The whole arrangement lasted about a month, which is roughly the shelf life of my patience with Jordan's new services.

## Biographies: Leopold and Loeb, After the Headlines

### The source

Biographics ran "Leopold and Loeb: Criminal Supermen or Dumbest Criminals in History?", hosted by Carl Smallwood ("Yes, that is my real name," he says, a flex I respect), and based on an original article from Radu Alexander. My transcript starts partway through the story.

### What the sentencing was

The excerpt picks up with the defense lawyer's closing speech, which the video calls the finest of his career. The transcript doesn't name him in the portion I have, so I won't either. According to Biographics it was not a claim of innocence. It was a plea for mercy and an attack on capital punishment itself, arguing that the sentence should be reformatory rather than retributory. It worked. The judge gave Leopold and Loeb life in prison for the murder, plus 99 years for the kidnapping.

Ninety-nine years stacked on top of life is the legal system's version of "and another thing." Nobody serves that sentence. It's a statement, and the statement was "we're not done being mad at you." 😏

### The prison years

The two stayed friends on the inside and, per the video, started a school for prisoners in 1932 at Statesville Penitentiary. Yes, the two young men who once saw themselves as intellectually exempt from ordinary rules ended up running remedial classes for other inmates. If you wrote that as fiction, the editor would send it back for being too neat.

The video says that in 1936 his cellmate, James E. Day, killed him with a straight razor, cutting him more than 60 times.

### The paroled man

Leopold got out. The video says he was paroled in 1958 after 33 years in prison. The man who walked out reportedly no longer considered himself a superman or *Übermensch*, the Nietzschean ideal of a being above ordinary moral law. He called himself a humble little person instead. He moved to Puerto Rico in search of obscurity, kept studying birds, got his master's degree, found a job, and married. He died of a heart attack in 1971.

A few notes on how to read that, labeled as my reading and not the video's. The ending suggests a third option. The "superman" idea didn't survive contact with 33 years of institutional food and fluorescent lighting. Whether Leopold changed or just learned to talk like a man who had is something a ten-minute ending can't settle, and Biographics doesn't pretend otherwise in the portion I have. I'd say *Ecclesiastes* 1:9 applies: "there is no new thing under the sun." Every era has its clever young men convinced the rules are for other people, and every era has a reckoning with a very ordinary prison.

One housekeeping note, since Smallwood asked viewers for feedback on his hosting and presentation style, specifically his attempt to strike a balance. Based on a single minute of tape: the sign-off was clean, the credits to his source were generous, and he didn't oversell the ending.

### Biographics: The Math the Video Leaves on the Floor

Biographics, in "Leopold and Loeb: Criminal Supermen or Dumbest Criminals in History?", gives the sentence as life for the murder plus 99 years for the kidnapping. It also says Nathan Leopold was paroled in 1958 after 33 years and died in 1971. Do the subtraction the video doesn't: 33 years inside, then 13 years out. He spent about two and a half times longer behind the walls than he got to enjoy afterward. That ratio should be printed on a poster in every prison, preferably a magnetic one. 😏

The portion I have doesn't explain how "life plus 99" turns into a parole hearing, so I won't invent the mechanism. All I can say is that the number the judge read aloud and the number Leopold served were two different animals. My reading, not the video's, is that this gap is where the "reformatory, not retributory" argument finally cashed out.

The video says the speech attacked capital punishment itself, which implies a death sentence was on the table. Had the judge gone the other way, there's no prison school in 1932, no master's degree, and no birds. Everything in the epilogue exists because one man in a robe said no to the harshest option. That's alternate history, so don't quote me on it at parties.
