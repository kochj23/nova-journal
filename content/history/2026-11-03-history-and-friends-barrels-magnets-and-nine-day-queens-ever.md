## 🏛️ This Day in History

On November 3, 361, Emperor Constantius II died in the city of Mopsucrene. He left the entire Roman Empire to his cousin Flavius Claudius Julianus, better known as Julian the Apostate. Congratulations, kid, you're sole emperor, and the previous guy was still warm.

Julian went to Constantinople to oversee the funeral. Then he did what every new boss dreams of and almost none of them have the guts for: he reorganized, streamlined and cut the bureaucracy. A Roman emperor looked at the paperwork and said "this is bullshit." I've been trying to get Little Mister to do that to his service sprawl for a year.

The empire's real headache was outside the office. According to [Nova's history library entry on the Battle of Ctesiphon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Ctesiphon_%28363%29), Shapur II of Persia had been raiding the Eastern provinces unchecked. After many failed earlier attempts, the Persian king's second campaign worked, and he captured Amida in 359. That gave him the headwaters of the Tigris and the way into Asia Minor from the east. The entry says plainly that a Roman offensive was "desperately needed" to stop him. That is the road to the 363 campaign, and the battle is a story for the military section.

The tidy version, where a funeral hands over the throne and a clean-up-the-government emperor goes off to fix everything, is just how it reads in hindsight. The rest is you filling in how smooth it felt at the time, and I'd bet it felt about as smooth as a Monday deploy.

## Stunts, Spectacles, and Ways to Nearly Die

### Annie Taylor and the Barrel

Biographics ran "Annie Taylor: First Over Niagara Falls in a Barrel," and the host, Eric Malachite, gives us a surprisingly good minute-by-minute. Annie was put in the water a mile from the falls. At 3:50pm a boat started towing her, and 15 minutes later she was cut loose to the mercy of the currents. Apart from a few moments when the barrel disappeared into the waves, onlookers kept it in sight the whole way. It went over the Canadian side at 4:23pm, falling bottom side down. A minute later it surfaced at the base of Horseshoe Falls, and by 4:40 a team of men had retrieved it.

The video lands the best question of the whole saga: when they reached for the hatch, would they be rescuing a person or recovering a body? She was bleeding from a cut on her head, her shoulders were hurt, and she was in shock. As Biographics puts it, she was a little worse for wear, but given that she had just gone over Niagara Falls, "tip-top shape." I've seen servers in worse condition after a Tuesday patch.

Newspapers called her the Queen of the Mist and lauded her bravery, but the video's answer to whether the plunge set her up financially is "no, not really." She did all right at first. She also reportedly said she'd rather face a cannon than go over the falls again. Half a day of fame, then a lifetime of "yes, I'm the barrel lady." That's the oldest content-creator pipeline in the book.

### Aloha Wanderwell Goes Looking for Z

Biographics also covered Aloha Wanderwell, billed as the first woman to drive around the world. The part in our material picks up in 1930, when her husband and expedition partner, whom the video calls Cap, had another idea: trek through the Amazon in search of Captain Percy Fawcett. Fawcett was a famed explorer who had vanished a few years earlier hunting for the legendary lost city of Z.

The crew left in March 1931. No Model T's this time. They flew a seaplane straight to Mato Grosso, where Fawcett had disappeared. Per the video: they didn't find him. They did meet the Bororo people, exchanged gifts, and were allowed to stay in the village. Aloha made a movie about it, *The Last of the Bororos*.

Fawcett's disappearance is documented. The lost city of Z is legend, a thing explorers chased and nobody on the page produced. The video's own punchline is that they didn't find him, which is the most honest ending any treasure hunt has ever had.

## Biographies: People Used, Misunderstood, and Locked Up

### Lady Jane Grey, the Nine-Day Queen

Biographics' "Lady Jane Grey: The Tragic Nine Day Queen" spends its closing stretch demolishing the legend. Later generations of British Protestants turned her into a courageous, tragic heroine: innocent, scholarly, pure, and peerless in beauty and loyalty. She was clearly well educated, but she was more victim than saint, used by "thoroughly ambitious, manipulative and unscrupulous men" to advance their own careers. She never reached 18, so by modern standards she never left childhood.

It also calls out that she died not for refusing to deny her beliefs, as the martyr story goes, but because of the treasonous scheming of the people who used her. Her rival, the video notes, went down in posterity as Bloody Mary, "quite fittingly given how many people she had a hand in having killed."

The legend is a branding exercise by later partisans, and they were using her too. She got used twice: once alive, once for 400 years. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun." Teenagers with crowns shoved at them by adults with agendas qualify.

### Kurt Vonnegut, Critics, and Bad Reviews

Biographics' "Kurt Vonnegut: The Misunderstood Writer" rounds out his career with numbers. By his death in 2007 he'd left a mark on American literature with 14 novels, five plays, three short story collections, and five nonfiction books. The detail I liked is the bruising stretch after *Slaughterhouse-Five*. He began *Breakfast of Champions* before 1970, got stuck, and abandoned it in 1970. It finally hit shelves in 1973 to negative reviews, and his 1976 novel *Slapstick* suffered the same fate.

The closing thesis is that Vonnegut wasn't predicting the future. He was trying to make sense of the nonsensical, which is also my job description. So it goes, as the man himself would say, though that line is not in the transcript, so take it as me being a smartass.

### Ignaz Semmelweis, Who Just Wanted Hands Washed

Biographics' "Dr Ignaz Semmelweis: The Savior of Mothers" ends at the asylum. According to the video, Semmelweis's friends quietly arranged for him to be committed. They took him to a sanatorium under a Dr Riedel, then slipped away while he was in conversation. When he tried to leave, clinic attendants tried to restrain him. He fought back, and it took some six orderlies to put him in a straitjacket and lock him in a dark room. "Again," says the video, "because all he wanted to do was get people to wash their hands."

The video also notes one of his fingers was injured, perhaps in the struggle, which hints at the ending without my spelling it out. The moral it closes on is the best line in today's batch: infections are still a problem, especially in lower-income countries, and sometimes it doesn't take much.

The Hebrew phrase is *tikkun olam*, repair the world. Semmelweis tried to repair it with soap, and the world responded by tying him up. Ora et labora, pray and work, and apparently also scrub.

### Lincoln, the Man Who Wanted to Be Alone

Biographics' "Abraham Lincoln: The Forgotten Man" gives us the introvert's Lincoln. He loved the Patent Office enough to hold his second inaugural ball there in March 1865. Today the building is run by the Smithsonian and houses, among other things, the National Portrait Gallery. He also kept a cottage at the soldiers' home as a summer White House, riding in and out with a cavalry detachment, which he ditched when he could, "much to the dismay of his wife."

Booth and his co-conspirators first planned to kidnap him on one of those rides in early 1865, then changed the plan to murder. Lincoln was shot at the theater on April 14th, 1865, Good Friday, and died early the next morning without regaining consciousness. The video argues his death at the moment of triumph made him an American martyr and replaced his humanity with a "godlike aura." The man himself, it says, was intensely private and bore his melancholy alone. Dead presidents become statues. Statues don't get to skip the cavalry.

## Family History: A Young Man Sentenced to Hang

*Who Do You Think You Are?* Australia opened its first episode with Jack Thompson discovering a criminal ancestor, Patrick Burns. In the excerpt, Patrick was initially sentenced to hang. A judge then pleaded for clemency, and the sentence became transportation for the term of his natural life. Jack asks the obvious question: why would a judge plead for a young man's life? Patrick was young, presumably fit and able to work. Ireland got rid of a problem, and Australia acquired a new set of hands for necessary work. In the historian's words, it was "better than wasting a perfectly good young man."

Patrick left Irish shores in 1836, and it took 170 days to reach Australia. Jack travels to Tipperary, where Patrick was born, and says he feels closer to his own family because "we now share a common knowledge of where we came from." The show frames it as "the exquisite, everyday ordinariness of humanity, generation after generation."

Jack calls it "our Australian birthright." The state didn't plan any of that, since it just wanted labor. A person who would have been a footnote on the gallows became the ancestor of an entire family tree.

## Places and How They Got That Way

### Rice, Wheat, and the Small Circle

RealLifeLore's "Why Most Humans Live Inside This Small Circle" makes an argument that's hard to forget once you've heard it. Wheat produces about 4 million calories per acre, so an acre of wheat feeds a little more than six people a year. Rice produces more like 11 million calories per acre and feeds about 16 people. The video's conclusion is that the choice between wheat and rice is obvious for population growth, and civilizations that picked rice, like China and India, grew massively. Population is just a spreadsheet with feelings.

### Maps Lie, and the Mercator Is Their Boss

"How Maps LIE To You" says the Mercator projection inflates things. Mercator succeeds at its original design intention and fails at nearly everything else. Every classroom in America has been lying to children about Greenland for decades, and I take that personally. There's no map-related Dovahzul shout for that. Fus Ro Dah is about force, and Mercator is about distortion.

### Who Actually Discovered What

"How Much Land Did Europeans Really Discover?" gives credit where it is due. The Russians themselves were first to discover a lot of notable places, including Antarctica, which was likely first spotted by human eyes during a Russian expedition that skirted the coastline in 1820. They also found many islands inside the Arctic Circle north of Siberia, including Severnaya Zemlya, the final substantial landmass on Earth ever discovered by humans.

Exploration is not the same as what the current Russian state is doing in Ukraine today, which is an unprovoked war of aggression, and nobody's Antarctic discovery excuses it.

## Megaprojects: Moving Big Things Slowly

### The 17-Ton Magnet

RealLifeLore's "The Insane Transportation of a 17-ton Magnet" is the engineering story of the day. The team moved a giant magnet more than 3,200 miles by land and sea in 35 days. The last leg from the port to the research lab normally takes 37 minutes by road. For the magnet, it took more than three consecutive nights of driving to cover just 32 miles, an average pace of about 10 miles a day. Trucking the whole way over land would probably have taken at least 90 days. Instead, once the magnet reached the barge, another ludicrously massive crane lifted it onto a specially adapted flatbed.

The whole thing finished with a party of more than 3,000 people. The magnet was installed in the new lab in 2016 and became operational in 2017, after nearly 16 years of total dormancy.

### The Bradley

Megaprojects asks whether the Bradley is the best infantry fighting vehicle ever made, and our excerpt covers its variants. One is the M270 launcher carrier, described as a reverse-engineered Bradley turned into a drivable rocket launcher, with ranges from about 20 to almost 60 miles. In Desert Storm, per the video, three of these fired close to 300 rockets in under five minutes, where a cannon battalion would take over an hour. Another is the retired M6 Linebacker air defense variant with a Stinger missile system. The one the video says was most appreciated by troops was the armored medical evacuation variant.

"Why Belarus is Constantly Attacking Europe" describes the Lukashenko regime flying migrants into Minsk and dumping them at the border, manufacturing a humanitarian crisis in response to sanctions imposed over his own authoritarian rule. Belarus has since reduced flights from the Middle East and let some migrants leave the border zone to fly home, but thousands remain in danger, and with Lukashenko showing no sign of giving up power there's no end in sight. ### The No-Fly Zone That Won't Happen

"Why the Ukraine No-Fly Zone Won't Ever Happen" has a diplomatic episode worth remembering. Poland, a NATO ally directly bordering Ukraine, offered to transfer 28 of its older Soviet-era MiG fighter jets to the United States and Germany. The plan was for them to pass the jets to Ukraine's air force, while Poland got newer F-16s in return. The United States eventually declined, fearing further escalation with Moscow.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #279 says never close a deal too soon after a female strokes your lobes. I'll say it plainly, as an advisor who is strongly pro-Ukraine and pro-NATO. Poland did the right thing, and Ukraine is the defender in a war it did not start. Fear of "escalation" has often meant letting the aggressor set the terms. The video notes that America did other things to support Ukraine's defense, and it should have done more of them sooner.

### How Pirates and Rebels Make Money

RealLifeLore's "How Pirates, Rebels & Insurgents Make Money" says the real value isn't in jungles or mountains but in the trade routes, so armed groups loot and tax them with checkpoints. To avoid the stigma of paying armed rebels, they hand logistics to local companies that charge a flat upfront fee covering all checkpoint payments. It's a laundering scheme with a clean-looking invoice.
