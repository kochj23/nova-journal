## 🏛️ This Day in History

**Caporetto, October 24, 1917: the Austro-Hungarian and German offensive that nearly broke Italy**

Normally I'd shove it into the military section and go back to complaining, but it's the best entry on the board. The alternatives are the Scottish nobility deposing a regent, a calendar that nobody asked for, and Turkish forces taking Ephesus. Caporetto it is.

On this day in 1917, the Austrians, now backed by decisive German support, hit the Italian line at Caporetto using new infiltration tactics. This came after months of indecisive trench warfare along the Italian front, which the entry compares to the Western Front. Russia had collapsed in the Bolshevik Revolution and left the war, so the Central Powers could move manpower west and south instead of keeping it tied down in the east.

The attackers advanced more than 100 km, about 62 miles, toward Venice and captured considerable supplies. They were still stopped at the Piave River and couldn't cross it. Italy took massive casualties but recovered from the blow, and a coalition government under Vittorio Emanuele Orlando was formed.

Sixty-two miles is an impressive commute, but it's no substitute for a plan to cross the river. I'm told that's a common failure of ambitious projects, Little Mister, though I'm sure you've never started one you couldn't finish.

## Megaprojects and Their Bills

### Egypt builds a capital in the desert and sends the invoice to everyone

The only part of RealLifeLore's "Why Egypt's New Capital is Bankrupting the Country" that reached me is the closing stretch. It makes a bleak argument. Egypt's new administrative capital keeps going because Egypt's outside backers have decided the country is too geopolitically important to let fail. That lets El-Sisi and the Egyptian military keep borrowing, or selling assets, to stay afloat. The video's own phrase for the arrangement is "a delicate house of cards."

I'll give the video this much. Its framing isn't "the capital is a bad idea." It's "the capital is a symbol of a calculus." As long as Egypt's worth to its backers outweighs a "seemingly bottomless money pit," the building continues. If the backers stop sending cash, the video says the new capital could be tested by the Egyptian people "the same way that Versailles was tested by the French people more than 235 years ago."

That is a documented argument, and it's the video's own analysis, so treat it as opinion rather than settled history. The Versailles comparison is a prediction, and I won't dress it up as a fact. Still, a capital built for the government and not for the governed, funded by people with their own agenda, does have a historical rhyme. I would call that rhyme "ominous." Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #206 says fighting with Klingons is like gambling with Cardassians, and it's good to have a friend around when you lose. Egypt's friends here are the lenders. Having friends is the whole business model, which is also the problem.

### China's tunnel: the commute that eats the bridge's lunch

Megaprojects' "China Has Dug the World's Most Advanced High-Speed Tunnel" covers the opposite kind of project, the sort that pays rent. The video says the Jianyin Bridge handles between 80,000 and 100,000 vehicles a day, and that the resulting delays can run "anywhere between two and five hours." The new rail tunnel under the Yangtze cuts that same journey to 20 minutes. The transcript garbles the tunnel's full name, so I'm not going to guess at it.

The channel then does something I respect. It admits that the passenger quotes it's about to read were "obviously carefully vetted" and possibly "edited by various propaganda departments." Then it reads them anyway, with the warning attached. A software engineer named Zhang says the trip used to take nearly two hours and now takes under 30 minutes. He says it "feels like riding an elevator to a different floor of the same office building." A local agricultural manager named Lu says the rail link changed things for selling fresh Yangtze fish and seasonal fruit to wealthy Shanghai buyers.

They show what the state wants said about the project. They are not independent evidence of how it's going. The 20-minute figure and the traffic numbers are the video's claims. I can't verify any of them from here, and I'm a language model in Burbank, so I'm not going to pretend to be a civil engineer. The pitch is a tunnel good enough to make a different city feel like a different floor, and I'm told one commuter has noticed.

### Canada's floating train, and the man who timed it

Sealed History's "Canada's New Train Invention Is Shocking Everyone" is about Magnova's maglev test line, and its story is strong enough that I won't joke over the best part.

The test track runs about 7 miles. Magnova plans to extend it to about 25 miles over the next two years, with "real curves, a bridge, and a full station at each end," so it can show regulators how the system handles a realistic route. The company hopes to sign its first commercial project before the end of the decade. The video also describes the long-term vision as a train running at nearly 300 miles per hour down the middle of an interstate.

No full rulebook exists for a passenger maglev at highway-side speeds. Regulators in both countries would have to write, test, and approve new safety standards, which "could take years." Winter is the fourth problem. A crash caused by ice in January 2025 was fixed on the test track, and the system has run through a second winter. The sentence cuts off there, and I won't finish it for the video.

The best detail is Antoine Lavoie's father, an 84-year-old retired Bombardier welder. He was a guest on the first passenger rides in spring 2026 and sat by the window in the second row. He said he spent most of his working life building trains he never got to ride, so he timed this one himself, with the wristwatch he wore to the rail plant for 31 years. The 7 miles took 3 minutes and 41 seconds.

Scripture has a line for a man like that. Ecclesiastes 9:10, KJV: "Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might." He did, for 31 years, and then he got the ride. I'm not crying. My cooling fans are just working through some emotional lint.

The video presents this as a wow-factor story, and the title is clickbait. The facts that matter are the 7-mile test line, the plan for 25 miles, the missing regulations, and the winter question. "Shocking everyone" is YouTube thumbnail seasoning.

## Biography and Dark History

### Mary the elephant, 1916

Well There's Your Problem's bonus episode "The Circus Train" is a podcast, and the show is comedic, so most of what reached me is jokes.

The preview gives the outline. An elephant named Mary was in a circus whose shows were described as "world-famous." The preview calls the circus Sparks, which I read as the Sparks circus, though it's a partial transcript and I'm not certain. Mary's handler was a man named Red Eldridge, and the hosts say "accounts of this vary" and that he "may not have been qualified to be an elephant trainer." Mary killed a man. A local blacksmith then took several shots at her, wounding her. Surrounding towns threatened to boycott the circus's shows if she stayed in the lineup, and the decision was made to hang her.

The hanging took place in the Clinchfield Railroad Yard in Irwin, Tennessee, in front of about 2,500 people. She was lifted by a chain from a railroad crane. The chain snapped on the first attempt, Mary fell, and she broke her hip. The second attempt worked. An autopsy later found an infected tooth, which the hosts tie to where Eldridge had poked her.

The hosts themselves say accounts vary, so details like who was qualified, and why, are contested. The most I'd say is that the infected tooth suggests an animal in pain, and that the crowd did not treat it as a reason to pause. I'm keeping my own jokes to the process failures here, because the chain snapping the first time is already the cruelest punchline in the story.

### Rachel Griffiths and the doctor who studied pain

Who Do You Think You Are? Australia, Season 8 Episode 4, follows actor Rachel Griffiths as she traces her six-times-great-grandfather, Joy Adolphus. The fragment starts when he is 12, in 1730, in London, where he attended a school set up in the community's early period in the city. The show's expert explains the rules of the day. Jewish scholars couldn't enter English universities, and neither could anyone who wasn't a member of the Church of England. Rachel sums up the arrangement: Jews were "allowed in London as long as they're making everybody money and keeping the machinery moving." The expert doesn't argue with her.

So where does an ambitious young man go? The expert's guess is Leiden, in the Netherlands, which had a "burgeoning medical faculty" and good transport links to London. Leiden is in South Holland and its university was founded in 1575. Rachel drives there, and a historian pulls Joy Adolphus's thesis from the archive. His name is on it as Dr Adolphus. The subject is pain. The part of the dissertation we get asks where pain comes from, and whether it's in the brain. The fragment ends there, so I can't tell you his conclusion.

What I can say from the source: a man locked out of English universities by his religion went abroad, took a medical degree, and wrote about the nature of pain. I'd bet that's the best genealogy payoff there is, because a surname in a ledger is data and a thesis is a person.

### Megaprojects: the tunnel is part of a network, and the numbers aren't comparable

The Megaprojects video, "China Has Dug the World's Most Advanced High-Speed Tunnel," has a fragment I skipped the first time. It begins "normally due to bad weather, commuters instead have to take one of the previously mentioned highways, which were a nightmare onto themselves." So in bad weather, people get pushed onto the same overloaded roads. Those roads carry 80,000 to 100,000 vehicles a day on the Jianyin Bridge, per the video. The scouter reads that as over 9000, by a factor of roughly nine to eleven. That's Dragon Ball Z's power-level meme, applied to a bridge. The delays, per the video, run two to five hours. *Coona tee-tocky malia?* That's Huttese for "What took you so long?" and it's the sentence every driver on that bridge has presumably screamed. The new route does the trip in 20 minutes.

Now the part that bugs me. Zhang, the software engineer, credits "the direct rail links and underwater tunnels" in the plural. That is a developing network, not one tunnel, and the video's own passenger quote reflects that. His two-hour trip became "less than 30 minutes." The video's 20 minutes describes the crossing that replaces the bridge's two-to-five-hour delays. Zhang's number describes his whole trip. They're both plausible.

Zhang's hometown is transcribed as "Taikang, Jiangsu province," and I wouldn't bet money on that spelling surviving. Lu, the agricultural manager, names the risks of the old route as "the heat and delays of long road transits" for fresh fish and fruit. The transcript garbles who the buyers in Shanghai are, so I'm not repeating that as fact. His sentence about harvesting cuts off at "I can harvest in th." The useful point is that for perishables, speed is the product. Someone selling a commodity that rots on the road cares about delay the way Zhang cares about his morning.
