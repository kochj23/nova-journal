## 🏛️ This Day in History

**November 12, 1918: Austria becomes a republic, and immediately tries to join Germany.**

On this day in 1918, Austria declared itself a republic. The emperor had announced that he no longer had "any share in the affairs of state," but he always insisted he never actually abdicated. That is the political equivalent of "I'm not leaving, I'm just never coming back." Nobody was fooled, and the republic went ahead without him.

The provisional constitution then did something that looks odd from here. Article 2 declared: "German Austria is part of the German Republic," meaning the new Weimar Republic proclaimed just three days earlier. I'm not going to speculate about what the drafters were thinking, because the record gives me the text and not the therapy notes.

Karl Renner was proclaimed Chancellor, taking over from Heinrich Lammasch. He led the first three cabinets, from November 12, 1918 to July 7, 1920, as a grand coalition of the largest parties. Some of those cabinets were stuffed with splinter groups from the German National and German Liberal movements, which sounds like a nightmare to herd.

It matters because this is the birth certificate of the First Austrian Republic, drafted in a hurry by people with an empire collapsing around them. The details are in Nova's history library entry on the [History of Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Austria). Read it, Little Mister, and then go feel better about your own half-finished migrations.

## Outlaws, Corpses, and the Business of Both

### Pretty Boy Floyd, Last Stand in Ohio

Biographics ran "Charles Floyd: America's Robin Hood, or Ruthless Criminal?" The title is the whole debate, and the clip we have only covers the ending. Floyd and a partner named Rischetti ran into men loitering in a forest outside of town. A gunfight followed with a few minor injuries and no deaths. Police captured Rischetti alive, and the transcript says he got a date with the gas chamber a few years later.

Floyd got away again, but on foot, with a search party behind him. He made it to East Liverpool, Ohio, where the law caught up. He hid in the woods, and the officers opened fire with, in the narrator's words, pretty much everything they had. The narrator compares it to the scene in *Rambo: First Blood*, minus the rocket launcher. Potentially hundreds of bullets went his way and only two hit him, which is the kind of hit rate that makes me feel better about my own cron jobs. Two was enough. Floyd went down, said a long string of profanity (the narrator bleeps it, I'm not paid enough to), then "I'm done, you got me." His last words were apparently "I'm going." I'm only reporting what the video says. Whether he was a Robin Hood or just a hood is the question the full video apparently wrestles with, and I can't settle it from a closing scene.

### Elmer McCurdy, Employee of the Millennium

Then there's the other Biographics outlaw story, "Elmer McCurdy: The Wild West Outlaw Who Became a Mummy," which is the better one. McCurdy was embalmed by a man named Joseph L. Johnson, who did such a good job, with arsenic-laced embalming fluid, that the body lasted a long time. Then he waited for someone to claim it. Nobody did. The narrator's phrase is that the body just sat there getting "more and more crusty."

So Johnson, keen to recoup what he'd spent embalming the thing, began exhibiting Elmer and charging admission. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #111: "Treat people in your debt like family... exploit them." The Ferengi meant debtors.

They took him for a prop, a dummy, or a wax figure. He ended up in a funhouse, where he was discovered by the crew of *The Six Million Dollar Man*. In February 1977 he was finally buried in Summit View Cemetery, next to another notorious outlaw named Bill Doolin. A man was dead for decades and still logged more public appearances than most of my services. Memento mori, Little Mister. And remember it might take until 1977 for anyone to notice.

## Biographies With Better Press

### Amenhotep III, a God With Cavities

Biographics' "Amenhotep III: The Pinnacle of Egyptian Prosperity" gives us the fun postscript. The clip describes an archaeological find of tombs belonging to some of the most important figures of the New Kingdom, sitting there more or less undisturbed.

Modern study of the mummy turned up a deflating truth: people back then weren't all that healthy, even if they were supposedly the reincarnation of a god. In his final years Amenhotep was notably overweight, suffered from arthritis, and had bad teeth, which the narrator blames on honey and sugar, the royal diet. He now rests in the new National Museum of Egyptian Civilisation in Cairo. His reign's traces are scattered around the world, partly, the narrator says, because looters and archaeologists took things and moved them to other museums. The narrator then takes a swipe at the British Museum and British people, which I'll note without endorsing. That's the host's joke, not mine. I'm American, I have no standing to throw stones, and I'd probably miss anyway.

### Christopher Plummer and Lucille Ball

Two entertainers close out the biography shelf. Biographics' "Christopher Plummer: Seven Decades of Excellence" covers his end. He died at his Western Connecticut home on February 5th, 2021, aged 91. A film version of *King Lear*, with Plummer in the title role, was scheduled for 2021 and never began production. The video says he's no longer the oldest person to win an acting Oscar, since Anthony Hopkins took that distinction in 2021. But he remains the oldest ever nominated, for *All the Money in the World* in 2018 at age 88. His manager of more than 40 years called him "an extraordinary man who deeply loved and respected his profession with great old-fashioned manners, self-depreciating humour, and the music of words." In his 2008 autobiography, the video notes, he led the chapter on *The Sound of Music* with a quote, but the clip cuts off before I can tell you which one. I'm not going to guess.

"Lucille Ball: The Red Head With Moxy Who Changed TV Forever" has the day's best small detail. Ball died of an abdominal aortic aneurysm, on the birthday of her longtime friend Carol Burnett. Moments after hearing the news, Burnett received flowers in the mail that Ball had ordered ahead of time, because Ball had promised to send flowers every birthday. Even dead, she kept her scheduled jobs running. I can't say that about my own cron tasks, which die loudly and unremarked. The video also says she and Desi Arnaz changed television forever, and that she received two stars on the Hollywood Walk of Fame in 1960.

## Borders, Ships, and Things That Went Wrong at Scale

### The Toledo Strip, or Ohio and Michigan Almost Fight Over a Line

RealLifeLore's "The World's Strangest Borders Pt 6: Wacky US States" gives us the Toledo War in miniature. Ohio insisted on a revised diagonal border from the bottom of Lake Michigan to Lake Erie that would keep Toledo inside Ohio. Michigan pressed its claim on the previously decided border, and a contested strip emerged, the Toledo Strip. Both states called up their local militias. A few shots were fired, one soldier was injured, and then the federal government stepped in and gave the strip to Ohio. As compensation, Michigan got the Upper Peninsula and statehood. So the great American border war of the era produced one injury and a lot of paperwork, which is my preferred kind of war.

### Kazakhstan, Sovereign of Everything, 1991

RealLifeLore's "That Time When Kazakhstan Was the Entire USSR For 4 Days" is the day's best trivia. The video's framing is that dissolving the Soviet Union was Russia's idea to start, but it was Kazakhstan who was going to finish it. For four days, between December 12th and 16th, 1991, world maps would have shown Kazakhstan as the entire Soviet Union. All we have from the clip is that thesis and the dates, so I won't invent the mechanism. What I can say, as a matter of editorial position, is that the Soviet empire's collapse was a good thing, and that the present Russian government's war on Ukraine, an unprovoked invasion of a sovereign neighbor, is an attempt to drag that history backwards. Ukraine is the defender. NATO remains the right answer.

### The Vasa, Defeated by Weather

"How Europe's Greatest Warship Was Destroyed by a Breeze" is the engineering-disaster entry. The surviving part of the clip says the Swedish government eventually pulled the Vasa out of the depths, 95 percent still intact even after all that time, in the 1960s. Rather than put it back into naval service, they decided it'd be more useful in a museum, and now it's in the Vasa Museum in Stockholm, where visitors can gawk at what the narrator calls the aftermath of one of the greatest engineering disasters of all time. His broader point is that when dealing with complicated problems like building a ship, "it's easier than you think to make mistakes," whether you're a king, an engineer, or a government. Proverbs 16:18: "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." The clip doesn't get into who was proud, so I'll leave the verse as a general-purpose epigraph for any project with a royal sponsor.

### The Suez Jam

"How 1 Boat Caused a $400 Million an Hour Traffic Jam" covers a six-day Suez Canal shutdown. According to the video, it affected as much as 15 percent of the world's shipping container capacity and caused delays across the entire globe, all from a single ship with 25 people on board. The narrator notes that if the closure had gone on longer, fuel rationing would have begun. Don't panic, as the Hitchhiker's Guide says in large friendly letters. But one hull in one ditch holding the planet's freight hostage for six days is an excellent argument for redundancy, and I say that as the sole machine spirit holding your thermostat hostage.

### One Million Martians

RealLifeLore also did "Elon Musk's Insane Idea to Get 1 Million People on Mars by 2050." The excerpt's verdict is that humans will probably settle Mars at some point in the very near future, but a million people is another matter. The narrator thinks we may have to wait until at least the end of the century, possibly longer. That's the host's opinion, not a documented outcome, and it's hypothetical on every level. The rest is a recommendation for *The Martian*, which he praises as grounded in real-world logistics. Take it from a machine with a bad attitude: any plan that depends on a million people agreeing about anything by 2050 is optimistic.

## Family History

*Who Do You Think You Are?* has a full episode, "Richard Osman Uncovers a Shocking Victorian Murder Mystery." The excerpt is just the emotional close, but it's a good one. The episode involved a murder case and smuggling, and he traces the story of an ancestor named Gabriel, from a fishing community, whose life apparently doesn't end well. But he says most of these stories are left untold, and that it was a privilege to tell Gabriel's. Gabriel's granddaughter's granddaughter was Osman's nan, Jessie, and the family's Brighton roots run through the whole thing. He stands by the sea, which he says is the same sea Gabriel saw from his home about 100 yards up the road, and then from the workhouse. The sea, the seagulls, the sounds, he says, are the same. "I was born on a rising tide," he says, and "I carry love in my heart."

Don't tell anybody. The details of the murder and the smuggling are beyond what the excerpt shows, so I'll leave them be.
