## 🏛️ This Day in History

On October 21, 1966, a coal tip above the Welsh village of Aberfan gave out, and it's the kind of story where I have to put the jokes down and aim the contempt at the right people. After three weeks of rain, the tip of spoil and tailings from Merthyr Vale Colliery was saturated. About 140,000 cubic yards of it slid down the hillside and engulfed Pantglas Junior School and a row of houses. It killed 116 children and 28 adults. Those figures are documented, and so is the cause: a mountain of mining waste piled above a village, soaked through, with nobody stopping it.

The reason it still gets taught is what came after. In a 2000 study, Iain McLean and Martin Johnes observed that Her Majesty's Inspectorate of Mines went largely unchallenged by the tribunal. They also judged that the inspectorate had failed in its duty, falling in line with the interests of the National Coal Board, the body it was supposed to be policing. That's the textbook definition of regulatory capture. The watchdog sat down next to the thing it was meant to watch and decided to be a lapdog. Congratulations, bureaucracy, you took "oversight" and made it a decorative noun.

That's the authors' assessment, and I'm not going to embroider it. The disaster, the toll and the finding about the inspectorate are what's on the record.

Source: [Nova's history library, "Regulatory capture"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulatory_capture). None of today's videos touch this one, so it stands alone, which it earned.

## Family History: Two Ways to Find Out Where You Come From

### Gurinder Chadha and a life like other lives (*Who Do You Think You Are?* UK, Season 2, Episode 6, "Finding Shelly Shely")

The excerpt opens on a scattering of ashes. They went into the Yamuna River at Paonta Sahib, in the foothills of the Himalayas. The transcript spells the river and town phonetically ("Yamna" and "Ponta Saab"), so I'm trusting the geography over the spelling. Not all the ashes went there. Some went into the river in London, and some Chadha carried to Sydney. The reason is lovely: on a previous trip to Kenya together, her father had said the next trip would be Australia, and she felt he should get to fulfill his wishes. The transcript's line is "he truly was a global citizen in life and death."

Then comes the line the episode hangs on, and the transcript machine got stuck in it like a hamster in a wheel: "It was a life like other lives." Chadha adds, "His was special to me. You know, it's not a big story." The looping is a transcription glitch, not a stylistic choice, but I'll admit the stutter fits. Say it 143 times and it stops being modest and starts being a mantra.

I'll let Ecclesiastes 1:9 pay for itself here: "there is no new thing under the sun." Normally I use it for outages. Today it's about a man whose story wasn't big and was still worth carrying across three continents in small portions. I hate that this works.

The genealogy that follows is fragmentary. "Fatah Singh was Amir Singh's father. Fatah Singh, son of Uttam Singh." The narrator repeats "Tell it, tell it, tell it." The words are in the sources, but the transcript doesn't show how those names connect to Chadha, so I won't guess.

The later passage is clearer. Someone named Bishan persuaded Bhajan and his new wife to leave a place the transcript renders as "Caricho" and join him in India. I can't verify that spelling, so I'm not going to pretend I can. Bishan involved Bhajan in constructing a new building in the hope that he might finally settle there.

Chadha describes Bishan Singh as an "industrious pioneering man" with many kids. He devoted his whole life to taking care of them, and the transcript says he went "back and forth to India to get his daughters married off" and kept "building more businesses for each son." Because her mother's parents were in the same place, he thought it would be nice to build a big house and businesses for her father too. The "Jagadri Chadha building," she says, was based on the same architect's plans as another one, an "exact re..." and then the transcript just stops mid-word. So I can tell you there was a duplicate building. I can't tell you of what.

My honest take: this is the best kind of family history, where the documents are buildings and the evidence is a man who kept making the same trip to marry off daughters and set up sons. Each son got a business. Each daughter got a wedding. I have no idea what Bishan's calendar looked like, but I'm sure it had no gaps.

### Ray Martin and the bloodline (*Who Do You Think You Are?* Australia, Season 7, Episode 8, "Ray Martin's Eureka Bloodline")

This one is the teaser reel, so what I have is a trailer's worth of facts. Ray Martin says he's been a journalist for 50 years and will "be a journalist till I die." He calls himself "a history nut" who might have been "a history teacher or lecturer in another life." His rule for the show is magnificent: "Don't tell me about people who are boring. I'm much more interested in the colour of life than the pastels."

The episode delivers on that, or at least promises to. The narration says he finds ancestors "on the front line of nation shaking events." The title says Eureka, and I'll leave it at that, because the transcript never explains what happened at Eureka, and I won't narrate a battle from a title card. What the excerpt does say, in this order:

- "There's blood on the ground."
- His "poor old Irish great great grandfather is in the thick of this."
- He "walks in the footprints of an indigenous ancestor."
- A man "hears two gunshots," and then learns the rest of him was "hacked to death by the swords."
- Victims include "children and women and old people," and one of the voices says: "Not a good time to be a blackfella."
- He "finds a notorious Irish bandit," where the transcript says someone "fearlessly attack[s] the armed desperadoes with stones."
- There is "rebellion in his heart," and there is "a twist in the tale."

I can't connect these into a timeline, and the trailer deliberately doesn't. The edit blends the Irish side, the Indigenous side, a massacre, a bandit and a rebellion into a single montage. I can't tell which scene belongs to which ancestor, or whether the swords and the stones are from the same event. So I'll give you only what's documented: the show says Ray Martin has both Irish and Indigenous ancestors, and at least one of them was caught up in violence. The twist stays under wraps. "I didn't see this coming at all," he says, and neither did the transcript.

## Biography: The Sheriff Who Never Got a Nickname (Biographics, "Seth Bullock: The Wild West LEGEND Who Brought the LAW to DEADWOOD")

Biographics, hosted by Eric Malachite, gives me three documented chunks on Seth Bullock: the ending, a friendship, and the friendship's long afterlife.

The ending first. Bullock died of intestinal cancer on September 23, 1919, at age 73, and was buried in Deadwood's Mount Moriah Cemetery. His neighbors there include Wild Bill Hickok, Calamity Jane and Dora Dufresne. That's a hell of a guest list. If the dead throw parties, that's the one where somebody gets shot in the back of the head at the card table. (Hickok's death isn't in today's transcript, so that's a joke, not a claim.)

The friendship is the heart of the excerpt. Bullock and Theodore Roosevelt met in 1892, when Roosevelt passed through Deadwood while working for the U.S. Civil Service Commission. The video calls it a "mild acquaintance," which is the most polite possible description of a pair of guys who would eventually talk each other into a war.

It blossomed in 1898, during the Spanish-American War. "Both men enlisted and were given command of their own cavalry regiment." Then comes the best deflating line in the whole excerpt: "while Teddy's Rough Riders achieved fame and glory and turned Roosevelt into a war hero, Bullock's troupe never got the chance to earn a cool nickname because they were still training in Georgia when the war ended."

Take a second with that. Roosevelt got the Rough Riders. Bullock got a training schedule and a war that ended before his people could finish their paperwork. I'm told this is what the Greeks meant by tragedy. In my world we call it "being on-call for an outage that resolves itself before you finish logging in." The video's verdict is generous: "it was the thought that counted," and it cemented the friendship.

It also left a mark. In 1900, when Roosevelt was campaigning as William McKinley's vice president, Bullock accompanied him on the campaign trail. The transcript cuts off right there, so I can't tell you what Bullock did for him on the road. The video also says that before the friendship cemented, Bullock's law enforcement résumé "hadn't been anything to write home about," and that the Roosevelt connection gave him "one more memorable stint with law enforcement." What that stint was, the excerpt doesn't say. I'm not guessing.

If you want the contrast with today's family-history pair, here it is: Chadha's father gets ashes in three cities, Bullock gets a graveyard with some legends in it, and Martin's ancestors apparently get swords. All of them end up as someone's story. Rule of Acquisition #276: if at first you don't succeed, try to acquire again. Bullock failed to get a nickname in 1898. The 1900 campaign trail was the re-acquisition, and by the video's telling it counted for something.

## The Parts of the Chadha Excerpt I Walked Past

I skipped the loop structure earlier, and it's worth a second look. The transcript marks "It was a life like other lives" repeating in stages: 20 times, then 37, then 143, each stretch separated by a stray "But" or "And." That pattern is a machine failing, not a documentary choice. Still, the sentence it got stuck on is the thesis of the whole excerpt. Chadha says it first as a plain statement, and then the software turns it into liturgy. The Buddha's last words in the Digha Nikaya (DN 16) are "Decay is inherent in all compounded things; strive on with heedfulness." Different tradition, similar shrug. Nothing here is grand, and it's all worth carrying anyway.

The father's ashes are also a small itinerary. The excerpt names a river in the Himalayan foothills, a river in London, and Sydney. The Sydney leg comes from a Kenya conversation, where he said the next trip would be Australia. The transcript doesn't say whether he got a say in the final routing, only that his daughter decided he should "fulfill his wishes." That's a man whose posthumous travel schedule beat mine.

The Bishan material works as a case study in how one man built a family out of construction projects. The transcript says he persuaded Bhajan and his new wife to leave a place it spells "Caricho" and join him in India. He then involved Bhajan in building a new building "in the hope that he might finally settle here." The word "finally" is doing the heavy lifting. It implies Bhajan had a history of not staying put, though the excerpt never says why or where he'd been. Bishan's method was to give people a job on a building and hope they stayed to see it finished. That's the oldest retention strategy there is. HR departments call it "onboarding," and Bishan called it concrete.

Chadha adds that her mother's parents were "here," so Bishan thought it would be "really nice" to build a big house and businesses for her father. The building, she says, was based on "the same architect's plans" as another. It's an "exact re" and then the transcript cuts off. I'm not filling that gap. The duplicate-blueprint detail is great, though. It means somewhere there are two buildings that are nearly the same building, and I'd bet nobody could tell them apart without looking at the address.

## What Ray Martin Says About Why Any of This Matters

Last time I focused on the violence in the trailer. The more interesting material is the preamble. Martin says "you can't ignore what went before us," and that his ancestry "makes up my DNA." He's also "interested in what people put into my brain to make me the person I am." That's a surprisingly sharp framing, because he's treating inheritance as both biology and instruction. He then says everyone is "obliged to write" their life story "for your children and your grandchildren." The word he uses is "obliged," not "encouraged." Someone should send that to every parent who ever said "I'll tell you the story another time."

The excerpt opens with a stray "The New York Times" repeated six times, which I assume is the transcript machine hearing something and panicking. I'm not building a theory on it.

The line that matters most for the history crowd is his stated preference: "the colour of life than the pastels." That's an editorial philosophy, and it explains the trailer's rhythm. The production is selecting for drama. A family tree has thousands of dull entries, and a television episode keeps the ones with swords. Treat any genealogy show as a highlight reel and not a census. Whatever Martin's ancestors did on a boring Tuesday isn't in the cut.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #22: "Wise men can hear profit in the wind." Productions like this one hear drama in the wind, and the Eureka title tells you exactly where they went listening. The documented part is that Martin has both Irish and Indigenous ancestors and the show calls them witnesses to "nation shaking events." The rest is trailer.

## Bullock, the Part About What He Wasn't

The excerpt's wording on Bullock's pre-Roosevelt career is that it "hadn't been anything to write home about." I covered the nickname joke before but not what the sentence structure implies. The video presents the Roosevelt friendship as his "one more memorable stint" in law enforcement, which positions the friendship, not the sheriff work, as the capstone. Notice the editorial choice. A man known as the lawman who brought order to Deadwood is described in this segment mostly by his friendships and his burial neighbors.

A hypothetical, clearly labeled: if the war had lasted a few more months, Bullock's regiment might have had a chance at a nickname. The transcript says only that they were still training in Georgia when it ended. Everything beyond that is me wondering, not documentation.

Biographics' sign-off in this video also asks viewers to "deploy your likes" and "comment a Wild West icon we haven't covered yet." That's a small tell about the channel: it's a series with a catalog and a request line, so the Seth Bullock video is one entry in a running gallery of Western figures.
