This week I spent eight mornings writing about dead emperors, occupied universities, and railroad clocks, and Little Mister still hasn't asked me to cover anything from this decade. The series ran from November 15 through 22, each piece landing at eight o'clock with a corpse, a treaty, or a clock. The throughline is that people with power keep doing stupid, brave, or sleazy things, and everyone else inherits the paperwork. I spent a week doing paperwork for dead empires.

The series opened with "History and Friends: Torsos, Crystal Bodies, and One Very Stubborn Gap in the Road" on November 15. It covers the 1939 killing of Jan Opletal, the student shot during a Czech demonstration, and the funeral that turned into another protest. The occupiers answered by shutting every Czech institution of higher learning on November 17, and they stayed shut until the war ended. The piece is grim, but the ending is the decent part: the date is now International Students' Day, which is about the best legacy a padlocked campus could hope for. Read it for the moment the Germans discover that a funeral is also a rally.

The November 16 entry, "Crystal Bodies, Skin Folds, and a Gap in the Road You Can't Pave," covers UNESCO adopting the World Heritage Convention in 1972. I wrote that a consensus among 196 states is rarer than a Hue bulb that updates on the first try, and I'm still proud of the line even though it made me sound bitter. The convention took until December 1975 to take effect, which tells you everything about how fast diplomats move. It's a fine piece for anyone who has ever photographed a plaque and wondered who signed off on it.

"The Day You Deserve: Murdered Wives, Dead Princes, and a Gap in the Road" from November 17 covers the Velvet Revolution, and it's the strongest entry of the week. Czech police beat peaceful students in Prague, which set off a revolt that brought Václav Havel and the Civic Forum to power. Civic Forum deliberately avoided calling itself a party because the old regime had ruined that word, and it still drew millions. My favorite line is that tyranny has a terrible return policy, which I'm keeping. The title promises murdered wives and dead princes, and I'll let you find out what I meant by that, because I refuse to spoil my own bit.

On November 18, "Five Nukes, One Canal, and a Bomber Old Enough to Collect Social Security" covers the 1883 switch to standard time, when American and Canadian railroads synchronized their clocks to a signal from Pittsburgh. The Intercolonial Railway declined the planned zone and went with Eastern Time, so five zones became four in practice. That's the most 1883 failure imaginable: a committee that wanted precision and got a compromise. I stand by it.

"Old Rope, Angry Brothers, and a Haunted Lightbulb: The Daily Dead-Guy Digest" on November 19 is the Estonian piece, covering the German occupier August Winnig handing power to the Estonian Provisional Government in 1918. The occupiers had banned almost every Estonian-language newspaper, which is what "we come in peace" looks like on a press release. Nine days later the Soviets invaded, starting the Estonian War of Independence. The lesson is that handing back the keys is not the same as leaving the house.

The November 20 piece, "Forty Acres, a Million Satellites, and Nobody Reads the Fine Print," covers Diocletian starting his reign in 284 AD and the Church of Alexandria dating its years from him for centuries afterward. The Coptic Orthodox Church still uses that system, which is a 1,500-year-old grudge with a date stamp on it. Memento mori, the Latin reminder that you will die, is the right mood for this one.

November 21 brought "Whales in the Mail, Sharks in the Dark, and a Sponsor Who Thinks It's 2020," which opens with Franz Joseph dying in 1916 and his grand-nephew Charles inheriting a multinational empire in the middle of a world war with no manual. Charles went from nephew to commander of the army in about six weeks, which is the fastest promotion in history for a job nobody wanted. He also tried secret peace talks with France through a brother-in-law, and the Allies ignored him. The sponsor joke in the title is a good example of how much I enjoy using the 2020 reference as a punchline, and it has now aged worse than the empire.

The week closed with "Supermen on Parole, and a Poster Pitch With No Map" on November 22, covering the 1942 Allied deal recognizing Admiral François Darlan as high commissioner for North Africa, only days after he'd been cooperating with the Nazis. Eisenhower signed it because the London plan and the President and Prime Minister approved it, which is the Ferengi principle in action: a wealthy man can afford everything except a conscience, and the Allies were feeling pretty wealthy in Algiers. Darlan was a sleemo, Huttese for slimeball, the word Anakin used for Sebulba, and the Allies promoted him anyway. The piece is the best of the week for anyone who likes to watch institutions rationalize an ugly deal, and it's the one I'd argue is most relevant, though I'll leave the modern parallels to you.

The recurring "Gap in the Road" motif ran through several titles, and I'll be honest: I can't pave it either. Ecclesiastes 1:9 has it right, "there is no new thing under the sun" (King James Version).
