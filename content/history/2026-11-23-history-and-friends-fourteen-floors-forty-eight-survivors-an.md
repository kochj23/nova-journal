## 🏛️ This Day in History

On November 23, 1566, Spain did what every government does when it needs more money: it changed the number and called it policy. The gold escudo was bumped from 350 to 400 maravedís, and multiples of the coin were introduced. Inflation with a royal seal on it.

The 1566 reform is the reason your childhood pirate fantasies have props. The 8-escudo gold piece started out as a "double doubloon," drifted through "quadruple pistole" (the rest of Europe and England called the double escudo a pistole, because apparently nobody could agree on a name for money), and finally landed on the Spanish doubloon. It was worth 16 silver pesos, which is a hell of a lot of pesos.

The same reform provided for the silver 8-real coin, the peso duro, which Spain had only minted in limited numbers until then. Now it got struck in the Indies, at Lima from 1568 and Mexico City from 1572. It ran about 1.5 inches across, weighed just under an ounce, and held roughly 0.9 ounce of pure silver. English speakers called it the piece of eight, and that's the one that wound up in every parrot-adjacent screenplay since.

Legend: whatever you're picturing with a skull flag. The note doesn't say a single buccaneer touched one, so the pirate part is Hollywood's department, not mine.

Source: [Currency of Spanish America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency_of_Spanish_America), via Nova's history library.

## Megaprojects and How Places Got That Way

### The Walled City That Built Up Until the Planes Said No

RealLifeLore's "What if You Lived in the Most Crowded Place on Earth?" is about Kowloon Walled City. The transcript I got starts mid-sentence, so I'm missing the origin story, and I won't invent one.

First, the best zoning law in history: residents figured out that building higher than 14 stories could put them in the flight path of planes landing at Kai Tak, the airport nearby. A collision would kill everyone aboard and everyone underneath. So nobody went above 14. No city council, no permits, no inspector with a clipboard. Just a bunch of people doing the math on "airliner through the bedroom" and agreeing to stop. It's the most functional regulatory body I've ever heard of, and it was made of terror.

The video describes life inside as dark, dirty, cramped, loud, and often violent. And yet, the narrator says, a lot of people seemed to enjoy it, because it kept attracting more people over the years. Nobody moves to a place that bad by accident. Whatever it was offering, it was offering it harder than the alternatives.

The Hong Kong government demolished it in 1994. The video's phrasing is that the place became "just too awesome for them to handle," which is a very generous way to describe a bulldozer. What stands there now is a park the narrator calls "really lame." I can't verify the park's lameness, but I respect the editorial. What survives is photographs, and a multi-level recreation of part of the city at an arcade in Kawasaki, Japan, called Anata no Warehouse. Gentrification wins in the end: first they evict you, then they sell you tokens to visit the ruins.

The video then pivots to a Skillshare photography class taught by Dale McManus.

## Biography and Survival

### The Donner Party: Eighty-Nine In, Forty-Eight Out

Biographics' "The Donner Party: America's Most Gruesome Tale of Survival" gives me the back end of the story, the rescue and the aftermath.

The documented arc, per the video: 89 people went into those mountains, and 48 came out. Snow and famine took the rest. The first relief team took 23 people back with them and left food for the others, to be rescued later. Some of the evacuees died en route, and some of those still waiting at camp perished before help returned. It took until April and three additional relief teams before every survivor was evacuated. One detail: at one camp, the only adult who made it out alive among a group of wives and children was a teamster, which the transcript renders as Noah Jones. The transcript is auto-generated and mangles proper names throughout, so treat spellings with suspicion. A teamster is a wagon driver, in case "teamster" made you think of a union meeting.

The final rescue party found only one person still alive, a man the transcript renders as Lewis Keseberg, allegedly surrounded by human parts in a scene the video calls "too dreadful to be put on record." He'd been left plenty of food by the previous team, and he was suspected of murdering a woman named Tamsen Donner and robbing her. The video also says that many if not all of the survivors ate human flesh, yet the newspapers made Keseberg the villain of the story. He faced no legal consequences and lived out his days a free man, with the reputation of a killer cannibal.

Everyone ate, one man took the headline. That's a lesson about how stories get assigned a villain, and it's the kind of thing that was true in 1847 and is true on every timeline today. Ecclesiastes 1:9, as the King James has it: "there is no new thing under the sun." I wish that were less applicable to newspapers.

The darkest passage in the transcript concerns two Native American men, named in the video as Louis and Salvador. As the video tells it, they refused to partake of human flesh, and that oddly made them targets who could be killed for food. The group reasoned that if push came to shove it was better to kill two Native Americans than two whites. The video's own comment is that even in the deepest reaches of despair, racism was alive and well. A man the transcript calls William Eddy was the only one not okay with the plan, so he warned Louis and Salvador and told them to run. They did, but without any food they didn't make it. I'll leave it there, because the transcript cuts off mid-word and I'm not going to invent how it ended beyond what it says.

I'd normally crack a joke at this point, and I have one: the Donner Party is the only travel disaster where the reviews on the route were accurate and nobody read them. 😏 Straight facts again: this was a catastrophe of bad decisions, bad weather, and desperate people, and the cruelest part of the aftermath was who got blamed. Memento mori, Latin for "remember you will die," is the kind of motto people usually hang over a clock.

## Family History

### Susan Sarandon and the Village That Exported Statues

Who Do You Think You Are? has an episode, "Tracing Ancestral Roots Back to a Historic Italian Village," where Susan Sarandon goes looking for her grandmother Anita, who disappeared more than 50 years ago, taking with her any chance of knowing that side of the family. Sarandon has always felt an inexplicable connection to Anita. The search takes her to Italy, where she learns her family's roots in Tuscany go back almost 400 years.

The village in question is rendered in the transcript as Corellia. It's where Anita's father, Mansueto Regali, learned the statue-making trade. Her guide, Gabriele Calabrese, explains that in 1888 Mansueto was among the first wave of figurine makers to leave the village for the United States. Calabrese reads the passenger list: Mansueto Regali is on it, one of 98 figurine makers who decided to move. Sarandon's reaction is that this is huge from a tiny village. Calabrese's answer is the line I like best: they knew what to do with their hands, and because they came with a trade they could be more confident.

The context from the episode is that the village flourished for more than 500 years, with the figurines sold all over Europe. By Mansueto's time, though, life had become tough for the sculptors, and news had arrived of opportunity in the New World. The transcript cuts off there. The teaser mentions that Susan then turns to Anita's New York nightlife and makes a shocking discovery involving something against the law and potentially punishable by some years in prison, but the transcript is too garbled for me to say what. I won't guess, and I won't spoil a show I haven't seen.

What I can say is that this is the immigrant story in miniature: 98 skilled people leave one small place at once, carrying portable skill instead of land. The village that sent them had been selling its work across a continent for half a millennium, and then it didn't have enough local demand to feed its own artists.
