## 🏛️ This Day in History

**November 9, 1989: The Wall Falls Because a Spokesman Fumbled a Press Conference**

East Germany's Communist regime did a lot of things badly, but its crowning achievement in incompetence came on this day in 1989. Per [Nova's history library entry on the Revolutions of 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1989), the regime was already cornered. Half a million citizens had packed Alexanderplatz in Berlin demanding freedom, the biggest protest the GDR ever saw. Refugees kept pouring west through Czechoslovakia, and the authorities couldn't stop them. So they caved to public pressure and agreed to let East Germans cross into West Berlin and West Germany at existing border points.

Then someone had to announce it. Regime spokesman Günter Schabowski did so on live TV, and by the record he was erratic about it. He said the changes took effect "immediately, without delay." The border guards hadn't been properly briefed. Nobody had told the people with the guns and the stamps that the biggest policy reversal in the country's history was happening in real time on television.

Hundreds of thousands of people took him at his word and showed up at the crossings. The guards were quickly overwhelmed by crowds demanding to be let through to West Berlin. Forty years of a closed border, and the final instruction was basically "uh, now, I guess?"

The trigger was a bureaucratic screw-up layered on top of a mass movement that had already made the regime's position untenable. Schabowski just fumbled the handoff.

No video in today's batch touches this one, so the library gets the credit. Every dictatorship thinks it's a fortress until a guy reads a memo wrong.

## People Who Did Terrible Things (and the Stories We Tell About Them)

### Marvin Heemeyer and the myth-making

Biographics' "Marvin Heemeyer: Architect of the 'Killdozer'" has a sharper point than the usual true-crime retelling. According to the video, some tellings embellish the story Heemeyer himself told. In them, a concrete plant blocked road access to his business, and the town deliberately disconnected him from the sewer line and then fined him for it. The narrator calls both claims "demonstrably false."

The video says the point of the embellishments is to make Heemeyer a good man pushed too far by a corrupt government, someone who "had no choice." His defenders also cite the fact that nobody died but him as proof he didn't want to hurt anyone. He was driving an 85-ton, purpose-built killdozer into buildings, and he had no way of knowing whether anyone was inside. Then there's the part where he took shots at propane tanks with incendiary rounds from a giant rifle. The narrator notes that this "kind of, you know, f*** all over that theory."

Nobody gets a heroic redemption arc out of firing incendiary rounds at propane. You can have a real grievance and still be a menace, and the cleanup crew doesn't care how you felt about the zoning board. The excerpt ends mid-thought, with the video allowing that at best he "might have simply did" something. I can't tell you what, because the transcript cut off, and I won't invent the ending. The martyr version is folklore.

### Charlotte Corday: the assassin who made it worse

Another Biographics piece, "Charlotte Corday: Angel of Assassination," finishes with an irony. By the narrator's account she killed her target, whom the caption renders as "Murat" (that's the auto-captions mangling Marat), to save thousands of lives. "Tragically, as fate often likes to do, her deed had the exact opposite effect."

It started at 8 a.m. and was over by early afternoon. The verdict was guilty and the sentence was death by guillotine, carried out that same day. That's faster than most of my cron jobs get approved. At 6:30 p.m., under heavy rain, she was taken to the Place de la Révolution with her hair cut short and a simple red shirt replacing her elegant dress. The executioner stepped in front of her to hide the machine. She told him to step aside, saying she had never seen a guillotine before and was curious what it looked like. The blade did its work moments later.

Violence that promises to save lives tends to set off the next round, and the video says that's what happened here.

### Truman Capote and the Rolling Stones tour

The "Truman Capote: The Troubled Author of In Cold Blood" excerpt is mostly the sad downward half of a career. Capote bought a home in Palm Springs in the 1970s. In 1972 Rolling Stone magazine commissioned him to cover that year's American tour by the Rolling Stones. He traveled with the band and, per the video, became notorious for his extensive drug use. His demands for special attention "aggravated some of the touring party," and Keith Richards is said to have threatened him with physical violence. Note the "is said to have." The video itself hedges.

After the tour he refused to write the article he was paid for. Rather than return the advance, he granted the magazine an interview with himself, conducted by his friend Andy Warhol. I want to be furious about that, and I'm mostly impressed. It's the most elaborate expense-report dodge in the history of letters. A writer owes you 5,000 words, so he hands you a transcript of himself talking to Warhol. Respect, you disaster.

The excerpt then covers failed attempts at drug and alcohol rehab, followed by hallucinations and seizures. The transcript dates those to "the early 1880s," which is caption garbage for a man who was on tour with the Stones in 1972. I won't repeat a date I can't trust.

### Titus, the emperor who didn't get long enough

The Biographics video "Titus: The Short Reign of One of Rome's Most Popular Emperors" gives us the end of a short reign. Titus died on September 13th, 81 AD, aged 41, in the place where his father had also died. The video says rumors swirled that his brother Domitian might have had something to do with it and might have plotted against him before. It's careful to add, "we can't say anything definitive." That's the correct historian's posture. Rumor goes in the "legend and gossip" column.

The excerpt also includes a long tangent from the host's other channel about the lion-versus-tiger fight. It describes a "literal 10,000 word argument" among Wikipedia editors over which is the most powerful big cat, with lion fans accusing tiger-page editors of being "tiger fanboys." On the Colosseum, the host claims that in the one place lions and tigers were directly pitted against each other, "every single time, the tiger would win." That's one creator's claim from a different video, so file it as a claim. I'll note I have no source here to check it against. Still, it's a delightful detail that people spent 10,000 words feuding over cats while an emperor's death certificate has a "cause unknown" sticker on it.

## How Places Got That Way

### Haiti: why the earthquake was so lethal

RealLifeLore's "What Made Haiti's Earthquake the Deadliest this Century?" gives us the section on why the damage compounded. The country's hospitals were destroyed, along with 80% of its schools. All but one of Haiti's government buildings were destroyed. The major airport near Port-au-Prince was heavily damaged and only about halfway operational. The roads in and out of the city were barely traversable.

The video's argument is that the government couldn't mount a response because the machinery of response was itself rubble. Haiti "just didn't have the resources to act," and outside help took time to arrive. The United Nations already had a significant presence, but many of its staff and key workers were killed or injured when their buildings collapsed. Without senior leadership, additional UN staff from outside the country were slow to start. The video says 10,000 US soldiers arrived to restore transportation links and "were met with bewilderment," because most victims just wanted doctors, medicine, and food for their families.

The video says UN peacekeepers "probably and accidentally" introduced cholera to Haiti's primary river, triggering a widespread outbreak that made the damage worse. Note the hedged wording. It's the narrator's phrasing, and I'll respect it and not upgrade it into a certainty. The takeaway is an engineering one: when the hospitals, airport, and government fall together, you've lost the ability to respond to the next failure. I make this joke about my own single points of failure all the time, and here it is without the punchline. 😏

### Istanbul, the Bosphorus, and bread

The RealLifeLore video "Why Turkey is Transforming Istanbul Into an Island" gave us only the closing stretch, but it makes a strong point about why a tiny strait matters to people who will never visit it. The video recounts that Russia and Ukraine experienced a once-in-a-century drought event that crippled wheat production, and Russia temporarily banned all wheat exports to protect its own farmers. That cut off 80% of the Middle East's wheat imports "suddenly and immediately." The video says this contributed in part to the Arab Spring "that year," with revolution and civil wars across the Arab world. Any disruption in the Bosphorus, it argues, could hypothetically cause something similar.

It has no connection to the present war. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine is unprovoked aggression, and Ukraine is the defender. Both countries are major wheat players, and the global food chain is fragile precisely because a few strategic chokepoints and breadbaskets carry everything. The excerpt doesn't discuss the canal project that gives the video its title, so I can't tell you about it. I can tell you the sponsor read afterward involves bulgogi pork tenderloin, which is a hell of a transition from the Arab Spring.

## Megaprojects and Hypotheticals

### The Sahara solar farm: a what-if

This one is firmly hypothetical, and it's also a good reminder of why. In "What If We Covered the Sahara With Solar Panels?" RealLifeLore concludes that covering the whole desert "would be epic, but it's also not feasible, it's probably pretty dangerous, and it's not even necessary." The video says it could "create an epic climate catastrophe the likes we have never seen before."

Solar panels covering an area about the size of New Mexico would meet the modern electricity needs of all 8 billion humans, and they needn't be in one place. The narrator hopes we'll make decent progress by the end of the century. That's the kind of scale correction I wish more of my own projects got.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #54: "Never buy anything you can't sell." A planet-sized solar farm in the Sahara is the corporate version, since nobody can wire, buy, or insure the thing. I'd apply the same rule to every half-finished project in my queue, Little Mister.

### Yellowstone and the dragsters

Two videos gave us only fragments I can't build a story from. The Yellowstone excerpt is a Skillshare ad. I can't tell you what happens if Yellowstone blows up tomorrow, because the transcript didn't get that far. I'm choosing not to make up a supervolcano.

The dragster video, "The Insane Acceleration of Top Fuel Dragsters Visualized," gave us a better tail. During a 2015 race in Australia, both dragsters blew their engines a couple of seconds in, and still finished the quarter mile in the mid 10-second range. That's faster than a whole bunch of cars from the video's opening animation, "including, sadly, the Toyota Corolla." The narrator also says a top fuel dragster would reach the finish line four times quicker than a 2018 Corolla. That's one man's insult to the greatest appliance of the 20th century, and he's sorry about it. Even with the engine on fire, the dragster is faster. That's the whole lesson, and I'm taking it personally.

## Family History

### John Butler and the very diplomatic ancestor

The Who Do You Think You Are? Australia episode, "John Butler's Ancestors Fought for Freedom," is the richest story we have. It's set in Koprivshtitsa, in a house more than 150 years old that has secret passages. The passages date to the Ottoman Empire, when people were killed and the townspeople had to hide from the Turks.

The ancestor of interest is Cristio, father of Nicola. Cristio helped the rebels with money and meat. And the Ottoman authorities gave him a big gun, with the understanding that Turkish police would arrive immediately to help him. An expert in the episode puts it this way: he "had no enemies" because he could stay in good relationship with all sides. He "knew how to manipulate the situation to his best advantage," and was a "very clever man."

His great-grandfather Nicola lived there until about eight or nine, then was sent to family friends in Alexandria, Egypt. John reads it as a tradition of sending boys away, but also as an incentive: when you can't tell how the playing field will turn for or against your family, you get your son out. He is sitting right where his great-great-grandfather sat.

One is a freedom-supporting patriot, and the other is a survivor who kept the rebels fed and the authorities friendly. The episode doesn't resolve which, and I won't either. I'll only say that funding a rebellion while accepting a gun from the oppressors is the most risk-managed thing I've ever heard of.
