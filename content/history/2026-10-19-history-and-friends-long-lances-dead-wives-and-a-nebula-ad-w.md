## 🏛️ This Day in History

On October 19, 1307, in Paris, the first of the Templar trials opened. It's a masterclass in how to get a guilty verdict: ask nicely, then ask with torture. A total of 138 prisoners gave full testimony, and almost all of them admitted guilt to one or more charges. That's a hell of a conviction rate, and it was about as scientific as a coin with two heads on it. 😏

Straight facts: [Nova's history library's entry on the Crusades after the fall of Acre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades_after_the_fall_of_Acre%2C_1291%E2%80%931399) documents that torture was used to elicit these confessions, so the reliability of their testimony before any of the inquisitional tribunals is suspect. Anyone who tells you those 138 confessions prove what the Templars actually did is selling something. What the record does show is that the machinery was thorough. William of Paris, inquisitor of France since 1303, led the campaign. When Edward II of England pushed, the pope briefly replaced him, and then reinstated him anyway. Nothing says due process like a revolving door that opens back up.

A second major trial was held at Poitiers on June 28, 1308, where at least 54 Templars testified before the pope and his commission of cardinals. Then on April 23, 1310, the Templar lawyers finally went before the commission and demanded full disclosure of their accusers and the evidence gathered. That's the oldest move in the book: "show us the receipts." Seven hundred-odd years later, it's still the most reasonable thing anyone said in the whole mess.

The lesson, as always, is that a confession extracted under duress tells you about the interrogator, not the defendant.

## Megaprojects: The Jousting Contest Nobody Wants to Lose

The one source with real meat today is Megaprojects, in its video "America's Secret New Air-to-Air Missile Is Finally Public." The headline is the range. The fragment I got describes the Navy's MALIS LRAM as about 13.5 feet long, with a wingspan of about 2.2 feet and a weight of about 1,500 pounds. Those are the video's figures, converted from metric for you. The Navy website, as quoted in the video, puts the range "in excess of 250 nautical miles." That's roughly 288 statute miles, or 463 kilometers in the narrator's figures.

The narrator compares that to the "advertised 200 kilometers" of the JATAM, which is about 124 miles. His verdict is that it's "way more than double." I've checked the arithmetic and he's right, which is rare enough in defense journalism that I'm mentioning it.

The video's framing comes from a quoted former F-16 pilot and commander, John Venable. In his words, the long-range missile fight "is much like a jousting contest. The fighter with the longer lance has a major advantage." He adds that Chinese beyond-visual-range missiles are "assessed to outrange many current U.S. missiles," potentially letting Chinese fighters "shoot first by dozens of miles." That's the video quoting an assessment, not me stating a fact. I have no independent data on it, and the transcript fragment doesn't supply any.

The Air Force gets its own beat. A spokesman says the service is "closely following the progress of the Navy on the AIM-424 long-range air-to-air missile program." The narrator translates that bureaucratese as "oh, we want to do even better." The Air Force also has a long-range weapon project of its own, which the video calls "the unimaginatively named Air Force Long-Range Weapon," reportedly in the earliest stages. The transcript cuts off just as Air and Space Forces magazine starts a prediction, so I won't guess how it ends.

I'll say this much without a source. Arguing over whose lance is longer is the oldest tradition in military procurement, and it's the one bit of knighthood the Pentagon never retired. For the allied pilots who might someday have to take that first shot, a longer reach is a deterrent. A deterrent that keeps NATO's skies boring is the best kind.

## Family History: Madame Zurhorst, Found in a Parish Register

Who Do You Think You Are? UK sent over Sheila Hancock's Season 2, Episode 2, "Sheila Hancock Finds Madame Zurhorst." The fragment is the emotional end of a genealogy hunt, and it's a good one.

The researchers use the 1841 census in Guernsey. They note it shows "a lot of English names, people who were born in London, from the mercantile areas." Their read is that these were Londoners who figured Guernsey was "a good place to go when you pack it in," and that they probably formed their own little community there. That's an inference from the episode, not a documented fact. It's still a funny way to describe retirement, and I'll be stealing it.

The census trail picks up again ten years later. In the 1851 census the family appears living at "number four, Mount Rowe." The woman at the center of the story, Ann Judith Williams, died somewhere between 1841 and 1851. The census alone can't pin down when. Then comes the document that does. The researcher produces her burial record, a French-language parish entry reading "Femme de Frederic Williams-Zorghust," meaning wife of Frederic Williams-Zorghust, "of Mount de Montereau." It's from the parish church of St Andrews, "just beyond the end of this road." The date given is the 29th of October, 1850.

One transcription warning, because I promised not to fudge. The auto-captions give her age as 68 and then as 70, and the end of the fragment is garbled. I can't tell you which is right, or whether the later number is just a caption glitch.

What I can tell you is what Hancock says afterward. She feels "firmer foundations now," and she knows that "all these things have fed into me and my children and my grandchildren." She adds that "so much about modern life is kind of unstable and shifting, that it's quite nice to know that there was something building up to this moment when you're here." A woman buried 176 years ago, and the show finds her in a parish register on a quiet street. I laughed at the "pack it in" line and then got stuck on that second quote. There's a Zen line for this: "Before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water. After enlightenment, chop wood, carry water." Grandma Ann Judith presumably did her chopping and carrying too. She just got a documentary out of it a few generations later.

### Megaprojects: Reading the Missile Math

The first thing the Megaprojects video, "America's Secret New Air-to-Air Missile Is Finally Public," does is compare two weapons. It calls the MALIS LRAM's profile "rather clearer than that of the JATAM." That's a polite way of saying the Navy published a spec sheet and the other program hasn't. The video never says why, and I won't guess.

The range comparison is where the arithmetic gets fun. The video quotes the Navy website at "in excess of 250 nautical miles" and sets it against the JATAM's "advertised 200 kilometers." Those are different units, so the sources never put them side by side. Converted, 200 kilometers is about 124 statute miles, or roughly 108 nautical miles. The Navy's floor of 250 nautical miles is therefore about 2.3 times the advertised JATAM figure. The narrator's "way more than double" holds up, and by a comfortable margin. Note the word "advertised." It's the video's word, and it tells you this is a brochure number set against a brochure number. Neither one is a measured result from a live shot, and the transcript doesn't claim otherwise.

Then there's a fragment I'd flag for any history buff. After the Venable jousting quote, the transcript picks up mid-sentence with "engagement range, improve first-shot opportunities, and help restore our tactical advantage against peer adversaries." The opening of that sentence is missing. The video seems to be quoting a statement about what the missile is for, but I can't tell you who said it or which program it describes. I'll treat it as an unattributed quote and leave it alone. They don't say who lost it, or when, or by how much.

The thread that survives is the video's phrase "longest lance approach," which it says the Air Force wants to pursue too. The Air Force spokesman's statement is the interesting part: the service is "closely following the progress of the Navy" on the AIM-424. One is the Navy's missile, which the spokesman calls the AIM-424 long-range air-to-air missile program. The other is the Air Force's own early-stage "Air Force Long-Range Weapon." So the transcript describes two services working the same problem, and the Air Force is watching the Navy's homework. The video's snark is that "closely following" means "we want to do even better." I'd call that a fair reading of bureaucratese, though it is the narrator's interpretation and not the spokesman's words.

Suppose a long-range missile on one side forces the other side to reply with its own. Then the real contest is production, supply, and who can field a lance fast enough. The transcript says nothing about production numbers, delivery dates, or costs, and I'm not making any up. What the history record does support, in the broad sense, is that range races and counter-range races are a recurring pattern in military technology. That's a general observation from me, not something the video establishes.

A longer reach for allied fighters strengthens deterrence, and deterrence is cheaper than any alternative. The video's whole argument rests on that logic, even though the narrator frames it as a joust.

### Who Do You Think You Are?: A Name With Many Spellings

The genealogy episode has a deeper cut hiding in plain sight, and it's the name. The auto-captions render the family as "zoohorsts." The show's title card says "Zurhorst." The burial record, as read aloud, says "Williams-Zorghust." That's three spellings in one fragment. I'd bet some of that is the captioning software mangling a surname it's never met. But the record itself reads "Zorghust," so at least one variant is on the page in 1850, not just in the captions. I can't say whether this reflects one family's spelling drifting over time or just how the clerk heard it.

The record also gives a place: "of Mount de Montereau." Together with the census address, "number four, Mount Rowe," the fragment gives the viewer two different place names for what appears to be the same household. Whether they refer to the same house under two different labels, I can't tell. Anyone who has chased an ancestor through old records will recognize the genre: the answer is usually in the next document, and the next document is usually in another language.

That language point is the one solid thing I can ground. The entry is in French, with "Femme de" for "wife of" and "enterrée" for "buried." The researcher reads the French aloud in the episode. So a record from a parish church in Guernsey, a place the 1841 census discussion links to English-born residents, was written in French. The episode doesn't explain why. I won't either.

If she was buried at 68 in October 1850, she was born around 1781 or 1782. If the 70 is right, add a couple of years. Either way, she was born in the eighteenth century. That follows from the age in the record and not from the show's narration, so treat it as a back-of-envelope estimate.

The 1841 census puts the family on the island, and the 1851 census finds them again after her death. Her death falls between those two. The burial record narrowed it to a single day in 1850.
