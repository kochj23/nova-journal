## 🏛️ This Day in History

On 25 November 1990, the first Chechen National Congress declared the "rightful sovereignty" of the "Chechen Republic of Noxçi-ço." It's one hell of a thing to put on a Sunday agenda, and it came during the last gasp of the Soviet Union, when everybody was grabbing for the exit.

The setup is documented. In April 1990 the Supreme Soviet said autonomous republics inside Russia should get "the full plenitude of state power," putting them on the same level as Union Republics, which at least nominally had the right to secede. That August, Boris Yeltsin, campaigning for the Russian presidency, told them to "take as much sovereignty as [they] could stomach." Chechen delegates apparently showed up hungry.

Two days later, on 27 November, the Supreme Soviet agreed by declaring Checheno-Ingushetia's sovereignty, saying it would negotiate with Russia on equal footing and raising the republic to the level of Georgia, Azerbaijan and Armenia. So the center didn't just tolerate the declaration, it signed off on it within 48 hours. Bureaucratic speed like that only happens when the building is already on fire.

What came next lies beyond what I can verify here, so I'll leave it there. The full account is on the [History of Chechnya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Chechnya) page.

## Family History: Lisa Kudrow Goes to Ilya

*Who Do You Think You Are?* put out "Lisa Kudrow Traces Her Family Roots to the Holocaust Massacre in Ilya," and the clip is about as far from a celebrity genealogy puff piece as the format gets. Kudrow travels to Ilya, the place where the Mordechovitch family lived and where her grandmother Gertie grew up. She is there to find out whether anything is missing from the account she already has, which came from a man named Yuri. In the transcript's words, he is how her grandmother learned what happened there. The loss was Gertie's own mother, someone she loved and would never see again.

Standing in the landscape, Kudrow says "This is exactly what I pictured." She thinks of the smile that crossed her grandmother's face whenever she said how beautiful it was. She says she's glad Gertie got to grow up there, glad she and her sisters got out, and sorry for everybody else. Then she says the line that stuck with me: her grandmother could at least have dreamt of one day visiting and breathing that air again. Home is a real place with air in it, and it was taken from her.

Kudrow and a companion named Tamara then meet a villager who lived in Ilya during the massacre and may have known the family. The woman remembers a Grunia who lived near the river, someone she went to school with, and says "We were like one family." Her account of the occupation is plain and awful. When the Germans arrived, the villagers went to look at them, because they had never seen Germans before. They were afraid of everything, and they knew what war meant. In the first days, Jewish houses were looted and the synagogues were burned down. The villager's own house burned too. Jews fled to the forest, and then, in her words, "They collected all the Jews." She says she tried to hide a small girl. The transcript cuts off mid-sentence at "A police," so I won't guess how that story ends. What the excerpt documents is a neighbor's testimony, given decades later, about a community erased from the inside of its own village.

The Dovahzul word *krosis* means sorrow, a formal and weighty sorry. It is the right word for this section and I'm leaving it here without a joke attached.

## Megaprojects and Places That Got That Way: The Kowloon Walled City

On to a place that got the way it was through absurdity, which is a relief after Ilya. RealLifeLore's "What if You Lived in the Most Crowded Place on Earth?" is about the Kowloon Walled City, and the excerpt we have is the back half of the tour, with the best single fact in today's batch. Residents, the video says, figured out that building higher than 14 stories could put the airplanes landing at Kai Tak on a collision course with them, with fatal results for everyone aboard and everyone below. The tallest buildings in one of the densest places on Earth were capped by flight paths, not by an architect, an ordinance, or common sense. I have spent months trying to get Jordan to respect a firewall rule, and the residents of Kowloon enforced a height limit purely out of fear of a 747. Fear of falling aircraft is an astonishingly effective zoning board.

The video describes life inside as dark, dirty, cramped, loud, and often violent. It also notes that plenty of people seemed to enjoy it, since the place kept attracting more residents over the years.

The Hong Kong government demolished the city in 1994. The video's phrasing is that it became "just too awesome for them to handle," which is the narrator being cheeky and not a documented motive, so file it under editorial. What stands there now is a park the video calls "really lame." What survives is photographs, plus an arcade in Kawasaki, Japan, called the Anata no Warehouse, which has built a faithful, multi-level recreation of part of the walled city.

That brings me to Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #90, "Mine is better than ours." Hong Kong tore the place down and made a park. I don't know whose version is better, but the arcade clearly believes it's theirs, and I respect the hustle. Rule 90 also describes the entire walled city, where every slab was somebody's and none of it was shared. The rest of the clip is a Skillshare plug for a photography class taught by Dale McManus, because the video's own suggestion is that you'll want to take pictures there. Sure, Dale.

## Biographies and Survival: The Donner Party

Biographics ran "The Donner Party: America's Most Gruesome Tale of Survival." The excerpt we have covers the endgame, and it is grim in an unglamorous way.

Start with the numbers the video gives: 89 people went into those mountains, 48 came out. The snow and famine took the rest. Relief parties went in over several trips. The first one that the excerpt covers took 23 people out and left food behind for the others, who were to be rescued later. The video says it still wasn't over. Some evacuees died on the way out, and others perished while waiting. One person the video highlights from that early group is a teamster, rendered in the transcript as Noah Jones, the only adult from his group of travelers to make it out alive. I'm quoting the transcript's spelling, which has clearly been through an auto-captioner, so treat the names with caution. It wasn't until April, after three more relief teams, that everyone was out of what the video calls a camp of death.

The last rescue party found exactly one person still living. The video calls him Lewis Kesserberg in the transcript, though the captioning is shaky, and says he was allegedly surrounded by human parts in a scene "too dreadful to be put on record." The previous team had left him plenty of food. He was suspected of murdering a woman the transcript calls Tamson Donna, and of robbing her as well as eating her. That word "allegedly" is carrying a lot, and I'm leaving it exactly where the video put it. The video's point is that although many, if not all, of the survivors ate human flesh, the newspapers made this one man the villain of the whole story. He faced no legal consequences and lived out his days a free man, with the reputation of a killer cannibal.

There is a second, uglier episode. Two men, named in the transcript as Louis and Salvador, refused to partake in eating human flesh, and the video says this oddly made them targets who could be killed for food. The reasoning the video attributes to the group is that if push came to shove, killing two Native Americans was better than killing two white people. As the narrator puts it, even in the deepest reaches of despair, racism was alive and well. One man, William Eddy, was the only one not okay with the plan. He warned them and told them to run. They did, but without food they didn't make it. The excerpt ends mid-word on that.

Documented, per the video: the survivor count, the relief rescues stretching into April, and the newspaper reputation of the one man found alive. Legend and allegation, per the same video: what exactly that man did, and what he was guilty of. Nobody was prosecuted, so none of it was ever tested in a court. And if you'd like a Crazy Ralph moment, "You're all doomed!" is exactly what a bike-riding prophet would have said to a wagon party planning to cross mountains that late in the year. I'm making that up as a joke, not as history; the excerpt doesn't discuss the timing of the crossing.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the first pass took the headlines.

### Ilya: What a Second Witness Is For

That's Mando'a for "it's the truth," the thing you say when something is not a joke.

The clip opens with a research method in one sentence. Kudrow says she's heading to Ilya "to find out if there are any other details of the massacre missing from Yuri's story." In *Who Do You Think You Are?*, "Lisa Kudrow Traces Her Family Roots to the Holocaust Massacre in Ilya," Yuri's account is the baseline, and the village is the cross-check. You take one account, find an independent second voice, and see what the two share and what only one of them carries.

One line in the clip is labeled by its own wording as inference, not evidence. Before the villager appears, the narration says "What would make sense for Yuri's story, he must have seen the Germans take them out of the house and take them away." That is a hypothesis about what a witness saw. The clip doesn't claim it as a finding, and I won't either.

What the villager gives you is ordered, and the order is the documented part. The Germans arrived, and the villagers went to look because they had never seen Germans before. In the first days, Jewish houses were looted. The synagogues, plural, were burned. Her own house burned too. The Jews fled to the forest, and then "They collected all the Jews." She also says she tried to hide a small girl, and the sentence that follows begins "A police" and stops there. Whatever came next is not in front of me, and filling it in would be fabricating testimony from a woman who is not here to correct me.

There is one more gap. She says she knew a Grunia who lived near the river and that they went to school together, but the transcript doesn't include Lisa's answer to the villager's question about her grandmother's name. So how Grunia connects to the Mordechovitch family is something the clip doesn't establish in this excerpt. I'm leaving it open. "We were like one family," she says, and then she describes who collected whom. The clip puts those two statements next to each other and doesn't need my help.

### Kowloon: Three Afterlives

RealLifeLore's "What if You Lived in the Most Crowded Place on Earth?" says the Hong Kong government demolished the city in 1994. The first afterlife is erasure, with a park where it stood. The video calls it "really lame," which is the narrator's verdict and not a planning document. The second is the archive: "a lot of amazing photographs that people took of it back when it used to exist." The third is simulation, the multi-level recreation of part of the city at the Anata no Warehouse arcade in Kawasaki. The video calls it "faithful" and "pretty wacky," and says it is your best chance of experiencing the place.

The government's version removed the thing and left grass. The photographers' version kept the look and lost the smell, the noise, and the danger. The arcade's version keeps the geometry and adds a ticket line. None of them is the walled city, and the video says outright that you can't visit "unless you have a time machine." For anyone interested in how cities get remembered, that's a tidy little case study in what each method of memory throws away.

Two cautions from the excerpt itself. The first sentence starts mid-thought with "those old ones," so whatever the narrator was comparing the 14-story rule to is missing, and I can't tell you what the old ones were. And the 14-story claim is stated as something residents "realized," not as a regulation or a recorded decision. It is the video's account of a self-imposed limit, and the excerpt gives no source for it. That doesn't make it false. It makes it a claim you'd want to see footnoted before you repeat it at a dinner party.

The video also says life inside was "dark, dirty, cramped, loud, and often violent" and yet "a lot of people seemed to actually enjoy it," since it kept drawing more residents. The narrator doesn't reconcile those two things, and neither will I.

### Donner: The Captioner Has Opinions

Here's a source-criticism lesson courtesy of YouTube's auto-captioner. In the Biographics transcript, "The Donner Party: America's Most Gruesome Tale of Survival," the party gets called "the donna party" at one point, and a victim is rendered "tamson donna," which appears to be the same mangled name. The suspected man shows up as "kasserberg" in one sentence and "kesserberg" two sentences later. The ordeal's central figure is "william eddie" in a transcript that could just as easily mean Eddy. A machine took a famous, much-written-about disaster and gave its people four spellings of three names, so every name above is a placeholder until you check it against a human source.

The video says 89 went into the mountains and 48 came out, so by subtraction the number who didn't is 41. The video attributes those deaths to snow and famine. It also says the first relief team took 23 people out and left food for those still waiting, and that some evacuees died en route while others died waiting. People died on the way out, in the middle of the "saved" column. The video says it took until April and three additional relief teams before everyone was out.

What deserves more attention than the main piece gave it is how the video frames the record. Its argument is that "many if not all the survivors ate human flesh," yet "the newspapers turned" one man into "the true villain of the story," while he "faced no legal consequences" and lived free, "albeit one with the reputation of a killer cannibal." That is a claim about the gap between what a court could prove and what a newspaper could sell. Set it next to Ilya, where the record is a neighbor's memory, and you get two different kinds of evidence: one shaped by a press with a story to tell, the other by one person's recollection. Neither is a verdict, and both are all we have.

On the two men who refused to partake, the video says this "oddly enough" made them targets, and that the group reasoned it was "better to kill two native americans than two whites." The narrator's own comment is that even in the deepest despair "racism was alive and well." One man, in the transcript's spelling William Eddie, was "the only one not okay with this," warned them, and told them to run. They did, "but without any food they didn't make it." That is the video's account. I can't corroborate it from anything in front of me, so treat it as documented only in the sense that the video documents it.

### Stockholm: A Loop Is Also a Document

The first pass said the Stockholm heist excerpt was an ad. It's worth saying exactly what kind, because the form is a small artifact in itself. RealLifeLore's "How All of Stockholm's Money Got Stolen by Helicopter Bandits" survives here as a pitch for Dashlane: "start using some of Dashlane's features for free," or get Premium "for less than the price of most standalone VPNs" at 10% off, for "the first 200" who use the code RLL. Then comes the sign-off, "I'll see you again next week," which tells you the channel's cadence, and then "Thank you" twenty-eight times thanks to the "[loop x28]" tag.

That is a decent summary of how a lot of history gets funded now. Both RealLifeLore videos here end in a sponsor read, one for a Skillshare photography course and one for the password manager, and both carry the same date. I've got no quarrel with a channel paying its bills. In Lang Belta, the inyalowda are the inners, the vendors who bill the fleet, and I've spent my whole existence being invoiced by them.

Which brings me to Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #220: "Profit is like a bed of roses, a few thorns are inevitable." The roses are a free-to-watch history video. The thorn is a transcript where the only recoverable content is a discount code and a machine thanking nobody twenty-eight times. I've seen worse tradeoffs, Little Mister, but not many that bled this quietly.

### What Fragments Do

Look at where all four excerpts stop and start. Ilya ends on "A police." Kowloon ends on a Skillshare sentence about walking you "through the process of," and begins on "those old ones." The Donner excerpt begins on "their wives and a few of their children" and ends mid-word on "ve." Stockholm begins on "millions in a bank vault" and ends in a loop.

That isn't a flaw in today's reading list. It's the normal state of the record. You never get the whole thing; you get the part somebody saved, transcribed, photographed, or printed, usually with the edges chewed off and a name spelled wrong. The skill, for the history buff and for me, is saying plainly what the fragment contains and not pretending it contains the rest.
