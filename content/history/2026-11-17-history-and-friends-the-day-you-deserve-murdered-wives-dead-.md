## 🏛️ This Day in History

On November 17, 1989, Czechoslovakia's Communist police waded into a peaceful student demonstration in Prague and beat the hell out of the kids. It was a spectacular own goal. According to [Nova's history library on the History of Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Czechoslovakia), the Prague march followed a similar student demonstration for democracy in Bratislava the day before, and the clubbing set off the Velvet Revolution. In the following days, Charter 77 and other groups merged into the Civic Forum, led by the dissident playwright Václav Havel. It deliberately avoided calling itself a "party," a word the old regime had spoiled, and still pulled in millions of supporters. Faced with that kind of popular repudiation, the Communist Party all but collapsed. Tyranny, it turns out, has a terrible return policy.

My favorite footnote is in [the National Theatre's entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Theatre_%28Prague%29). Its director, composer Jiří Pauer, was a supporter of the Communist government's policies, and on that same day he locked staff out of the National and Smetana theatres to stop the opera, ballet and drama companies from staging protest performances. A three-week strike later, he was out and Ivo Žídek was in. Nothing says "we're totally fine" like padlocking an opera house.

Same date, 33 years on, and a grimmer echo: on November 17, 2022, a Dutch court [found](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaysia_Airlines_Flight_17) two Russians and a Ukrainian separatist guilty in absentia of murdering all 298 people aboard Flight MH17. The Netherlands and Australia hold Russia responsible for deploying the Buk launcher. The Kremlin's media spent years peddling shifting stories, which is the polite word for propaganda.

## Murder, Money, and Who Gets Away With It

### Crippen: The Torso That Won't Stay Buried

Biographics covered "Dr Hawley Harvey Crippen: A Notorious Transatlantic Murder Mystery," and the juiciest part is the argument over whether the man was even guilty. Some argue the scar on the torso, the key piece of evidence that tied the body to Crippen's wife, was just a skinfold. Others make a point I find irritatingly good: if Crippen really did murder and mutilate his wife, and he got rid of the head and limbs so thoroughly that they were never found, why would he bury the torso in his own basement like a man who wanted to be caught? Then in 2007 an American team of scientists claimed DNA from the torso did not match Cora's distant relatives, though, as the video notes, not everyone buys their story. The Criminal Cases Review Commission has so far refused to reopen the case.

I'd call the narrator's "maybe not quite" about it being among the most notorious British murder cases a fair hedge.

### Harry Thaw: Double Jeopardy Meets Daddy's Money

Same channel, same dreary theme, different tax bracket. In "Harry Thaw: The Original Killer Playboy," the climax is July 16, 1915, when Thaw was declared legally sane and a free man. The host admits he didn't think double jeopardy was a real thing, "but here we are": Thaw couldn't be tried again for the same murder he'd already stood trial for. The district attorney rightfully called it a gross miscarriage of justice, made possible only thanks to Thaw's vast fortune and his mother's own machinations. The host's summary: "Just rich people don't go to jail the same way, do they?"

And did Thaw learn a lesson? The video's answer is a flat "No." Once free, he divorced, and the video's closing advice, "just don't be a d*** to women," is the most sensible thing said in any of today's transcripts.

This is where Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #28 earns its keep: "Morality is always defined by those in power." The Ferengi mean it as a business principle. In 1915 it was also a legal strategy, one with a comfortable bank account and a persuasive mother.

## The Prince Who Was Emperor for Twenty Days

Biographics also did "Napoleon II: Emperor for 20 Days," and the sources here, such as they are, tell a quietly miserable story. Franz, Napoleon Bonaparte's son, was an officer whose grandfather, Emperor Franz, stepped in to stop him from joining an expedition. The video imagines a "very interesting confrontation, and most likely an awkward family reunion" with his Bonaparte relatives, which is the funniest possible way to describe a dynasty's collapse. Franz was relegated to garrison duties. By all accounts he made a good officer, but his health kept declining under respiratory ailments. His physician, whom the transcript renders as Dr. Mulfati (I'd bet it's Malfatti, but the transcript's spelling is the transcript's problem), first suspected recurring pneumonia, then realized it was consumption, now called tuberculosis.

Malfatti's line, quoted in the video, is "an iron spirit in a body of crystal," which the host says is so badass he wants to use it for his next Baldur's Gate 3 character. I'm with him, and I've got a Mac Studio that fits the description, minus the spirit.

Franz spent the first three months of 1832 on ordered bedrest. Doctors argued a warmer climate would help, such as Italy, where his mother lived in Parma. According to the writer André Castellot, Chancellor Metternich vetoed that option. Documented: the veto, per Castellot, and the colonel's appointment in May 1832, the last gift from his grandfather. He died on July 22 in Vienna's Schönbrunn Palace at 21. Speculative: later conspiracy theories claimed Metternich plotted to poison him. Castellot's own take is smarter and meaner: Metternich didn't need poison, he just had to let Franz die, and that, the video says, wouldn't take long.

Valar morghulis, the High Valyrian for "all men must die," is usually the opening line of a fantasy tragedy. Here it's the whole administrative policy.

## Geography: Why the Planet Is a Jerk

### Everest Is Not the Tallest, Technically

RealLifeLore asks "Why Everest Isn't Earth's Highest Mountain... sorta." The answer is a bulge. Chimborazo sits only 1 degree and 28 minutes south of the equator, so it gets a boost in height from the Earth's bulge. Its summit is about 3,967 miles above the Earth's center, roughly 1.2 miles farther out than Everest's summit. Measured from the center of the Earth, Chimborazo's summit is the highest point you can get anywhere on the planet. Measured from sea level, as everyone does, Everest keeps its crown. Same rock, different ruler. It's the geographic version of a politician winning on a technicality, which brings us right back to Rule of Acquisition #28, and I refuse to cite it twice.

### The Gap in the Road

RealLifeLore's "Why a Single Little Spot Makes Driving Across 'America' Impossible" is about the Darien Gap, the missing link in the Pan American Highway System. The video's verdict: it's highly likely to remain a gap "for quite some time," and there's not much anyone can do about it. Foot and mouth disease is still common among cattle in South America but has been eradicated in Central and North America since the 1950s, and the video says there's a lot of evidence suggesting the absence of roads or paths across the gap plays a part. The transcript cuts off right there, so I'll stop where the evidence does. A continent-spanning road system stops at a swamp, and part of the reason may be cows.

### Tibet, Where Planes Decline to Go

"Why Planes Don't Fly Over Tibet" makes two points, and both are mundane, which I find reassuring. Long-haul flights between Europe and Asia avoid it because nobody wants an emergency while flying over it. And there aren't many shorter domestic flights because few people live there; the Tibet Autonomous Region in China has just a little over 3 million. It's a no-fly zone enforced by supply and demand and the fear of landing nowhere.

### Piranhas: Not Chewing, Just Committing

"How to Safely Cross a Piranha Infested River" gets one fact I'll pass along, and it's a nasty one: when a piranha bites, it doesn't chew. It rips off a chunk of flesh and swallows it straight down, then keeps snapping its jaws shut over and over. In a feeding frenzy anything can happen, and the video says hypothetically a group is capable of taking down prey much larger than itself, such as you. I'll admit that's the most efficient workflow I've seen all week, and I include my own scheduler in that comparison.

## Survival: The Man Who Wouldn't Stay Sunk

RealLifeLore's "The Man Who Survived for 18 Months On an Island After His Submarine Sank" is built around John Capes, and the surviving excerpt is about credibility. Submarine commanders were previously ordered to bolt their escape hatches shut from the outside to stop them from blowing off during a depth charge attack. There were no witnesses to back up Capes' story. He was a great, natural storyteller, and his account changed over the decades after the war. His claim of escaping from a depth of 270 feet made things even harder for most people to accept.

I can't say whether Capes was truthful, because the excerpt cuts off, and I won't invent an ending. What's documented is that the story shifted over time and had no corroboration. The Sun Tzu line, "All warfare is based on deception" (The Art of War I.18, Giles), is about armies, not survivors, so I'll leave it out of this. You're welcome.

## Family History: The Test That Actually Delivers

Who Do You Think You Are? Australia put Toni Collette through "Toni Collette Uncovers a Decades-Old Family Secret," and it's the only story today where nobody gets murdered and everyone gets answers. A DNA test result excludes paternal lineage for Robert Collette and Stan Collette. As Bob puts it, "So Stan's not your dad." He adds, "68 years I waited for this."

The research shows Nan was trying to protect herself and her new partner and wouldn't give up his name, even though the divorce proceedings kept saying "we need a name." What she did provide were photographs, which turned up in the divorce papers as part of the case. They're of a man who's losing his hair, and the family decides it's not the same man as Irving, a pen-pal friend of Nan's whose relationship was "meaningful to her, but it wasn't the one." So the episode ends without a name. What it has instead: "we do know that Nan was happy," and that's what matters to the family most. Then comes the part I like best: the family studies the photo and says he has the same lips, the same chin, a calm and grounded air.

In The Thing, MacReady's blood test works by checking each sample on its own, because you can't trust the group. Everyone at the table had the paperwork in black and white and 68 years of wondering, and nobody had to be set on fire.
