## 🏛️ This Day in History

**November 14, 1918: Czechoslovakia gets a president, and he's the guy who actually built the thing.**

The Austro-Hungarian Empire collapsed at the end of World War I, which left a lot of Central Europe as a pile of very expensive rubble and one hell of a to-do list. [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia) was the new state, founded by Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, who served as its first president from 14 November 1918 to 14 December 1935. That's seventeen years in the job, which in 20th-century Europe counts as a miracle of job security. He handed the keys to his close ally Edvard Beneš, who I'm sure had a perfectly calm and uneventful tenure. (He did not. I'm just not allowed to spoil it.)

Czech nationalism had been simmering since the 19th century, when philologists and educators, steeped in Romanticism, set about promoting the Czech language and pride in the Czech people. Think about that: a country partly kicked off by grammar nerds. By the second half of the century it was a mass movement, and figures like historian František Palacký founded patriotic self-help organizations that let people practice community life long before they had a country to practice it in.

The same week, the German occupiers formally handed power over to Estonia's national government, according to [the history library's account of the German occupation of Estonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_occupation_of_Estonia_during_World_War_I). Then Bolshevik Russian troops invaded anyway, and Estonia had to fight a war of independence until the Treaty of Tartu on 2 February 1920. Some neighbors really do treat "your borders" as a suggestion, and the record shows how that went.

## Family History: Cindy Crawford, Charlemagne's Descendant

The source is the "Who Do You Think You Are US" episode "Cindy Crawford Discovers She is Descended from Royalty," posted on the show's channel on 2026-11-13. The transcript opens in the middle of a scene, which is rude, but we work with what we get.

The setting is Charlemagne's palace chapel. The historian guiding her says it dates "probably from around 796," and describes "the marble, the arches, the glorious mosaics." The point of the place, per the historian, is that it's where Charlemagne "wanted to express his commitment and achievement and his aims as a Christian ruler." That's the documented part, at least as far as this episode tells it, and it's a pretty good flex. Most of us express our aims as rulers by leaving a half-finished spreadsheet. He built a chapel with mosaics.

The historian says Charlemagne came to mass here, and that "for the last part of his life, he lived here more or less all the time." So the great emperor of the textbooks ended up basically living at his office, which is the most relatable thing a Carolingian ever did.

Then comes the family payload. Crawford says her "41 times great-grandfather was living in the 700s," and that the connection is humbling for a "small Midwestern girl." Her reflection is the actual best line in the transcript: "time is elastic. It can seem very long ago, but then it doesn't seem that long ago." That's the kind of thing you say standing in a room Charlemagne actually prayed in, and I'm not going to roast it. Much.

There's also a nice detail about expectations. She says that before the episode, the farthest back she'd have guessed was "the 1600s, where we had Thomas Trowbridge." So the show took her from a guy in the 1600s to the 700s. That's a thousand-odd years of upgrade, which is the ancestry equivalent of ordering a medium coffee and being handed a vineyard.

Everything above is what the episode presents, using the show's own researchers and historians. The transcript we have doesn't include the actual paper trail, meaning the chain of ancestors connecting a modern woman to the 700s. So I can tell you what the episode concludes, but I can't show you the work, and I won't pretend otherwise. I'm not saying this one's bunk. I'm saying the transcript cuts off before the evidence does, and that's the stopping point for me too. Her takeaway, that Charlemagne went from "just a name" to "a person," is real regardless, which is the whole trick of this show.

## Megaprojects: A Missile Plant in Ohio

The second source is the Megaprojects channel video "Can a Startup Solve America's Biggest Military Problem?" from 2026-11-13. The transcript starts after the setup, so I'll stay with the numbers it actually gives and leave the "biggest problem" framing to the video.

The product is the Barracuda, made by Anduril. The narrator says it "would provide something that is much needed in modern warfare," and that whether Anduril can deliver "on schedule and on budget, we'll just have to wait and see." Spoken like a man who has watched a few megaprojects, and this is the Megaprojects channel, which is basically the place where "on budget" goes to die. 😏

Anduril has committed $900 million and expects to create over 4,000 jobs by 2035. The state is backing it with "a 30-year tax credit system worth over $450 million, as well as a $310 million Jobs Ohio grant." Construction began last year. The plant opened for a different Anduril product, the Fury, in March 2026, "three months early, which is something you don't hear very often on this channel." I love that the narrator treats an early opening like a unicorn sighting. Three months early on a megaproject is the engineering equivalent of a cat returning your phone calls.

The Barracuda 500 line is expected to start production in Ohio by the end of 2026. One important caveat from the narration: "no warheads are going to be fitted there." The missiles will be taken elsewhere to be armed. So Ohio builds the body and somebody else supplies the bite.

Do the incentive math with me, because Rule of Acquisition #41 is practically written for this. "Money talks, but having a lots of it gets more attention." Ohio's offer of well over $750 million in combined tax credit and grant, against Anduril's $900 million commitment, is a state shouting at a company in a very loud voice made of dollars. That sum is my addition of two numbers the video gave, nothing more. It isn't a figure the narrator states, so treat it as arithmetic, not reporting.

### Poland Wants One Too

The transcript's last stretch is about Poland. The state arms group PGZ signed a memorandum with Anduril on 27 October 2025 and a cooperation agreement in July 2026. The plan, as described, is for Poland to first assemble a version called the Barracuda 500M, and later manufacture it, creating "a European version that can be used for defense in Poland and other countries."

Then comes the cold bucket of water, and credit to the narrator for pouring it. Right now, "these are all just agreements," and the transcript says no Polish orders "of any quantity or value have been publ" and then it cuts off, presumably at "published." So: memorandum, then cooperation agreement, then zero announced orders. In Huttese the word is bantha poodoo for worthless junk, and I'm not calling the Polish deal that, since it's real paper. Bargon, as the Hutts say, means a deal, and a deal isn't a delivery.

## Where These Two Stories Rhyme

Charlemagne's chapel is a building meant to broadcast who its owner was and what he stood for, with marble and mosaics doing the talking. The Ohio plant is a building meant to do the same for a modern company and a state that bet big on it. The difference is that the chapel from around 796 is still the subject of a documentary. The plant will have to earn that kind of afterlife.

Ecclesiastes 1:9 in the KJV puts it better than I can: "there is no new thing under the sun." Powerful people build impressive places to say something about themselves, and then later people turn up with cameras to find out whether it worked.

### The Arithmetic Under "41 Times Great-Grandfather"

Crawford says her 41 times great-grandfather was living in the 700s. Back-of-napkin math, mine and not the episode's: that's roughly 1,200 to 1,300 years across 41-plus generations, about 30 years each. It's a sane number, nobody had to father kids at age 90, and I appreciate a claim that doesn't need a miracle to balance the books.

Here's a point from general math, not from the episode. Ancestor slots double with every generation back, so by 41 generations you have trillions of slots and nowhere near that many actual humans to fill them. Genealogists call it pedigree collapse. It's why a deep line to a famous medieval name isn't automatically magic. It also says nothing about whether this particular chain holds up, and the transcript shows no documents.

Her pre-episode guess was "the 1600s, where we had Thomas Trowbridge." Compared with the 700s, that's about nine centuries of upgrade. She also says she wants her family to "feel that they are a tiny little blip of this part of history." Fine, but a blip with a chapel in its family tree is still a hell of a blip.

### What the Chapel Is Actually Saying

The historian says the chapel is where Charlemagne "wanted to express his commitment and achievement and his aims as a Christian ruler," and dates it "probably from around 796." Note the "probably." Even the guide hedges the date, which is what honest history sounds like and what my vendor roadmaps never do.

Now the part the main piece glossed. The historian says "You have to imagine that this is somewhere he was coming every day." That's an invitation to imagine, not a documented attendance record. The statements that he "would come to mass" and "lived here more or less all the time" come right after, so the episode mixes the imagined and the asserted in one breath. That's the line between documented and legend, and television is gloriously bad at drawing it.

### The Order Ladder

The Megaprojects opening lays out the sequence: proposed purchases must become funded orders, Anduril must scale production, tests continue, and then come deliveries. So at the time of the video, per this transcript, the purchases were still "proposed." Anduril's $900 million is committed, but the customer's money isn't yet funded. That's the company going first, which is brave or reckless depending on how the orders land. The narrator calls the Barracuda "much needed in modern warfare" and hedges on "schedule and budget." I'll note, without comment, that the hedging is the most reliable thing on this channel.

### Who's Paying for What

More arithmetic, mine and not the narrator's. The state offers a 30-year tax credit "worth over $450 million" plus a $310 million grant, roughly $760 million, against a $900 million company commitment. That's about 84 cents of public money per company dollar, though a credit spread over three decades isn't the same animal as cash on day one. Spread across "over 4,000 jobs by 2035," it's no more than about $190,000 in incentives per job. 😏 That's a lot of taxpayer money per paycheck, and I mention it only because you asked for specifics.

Rule of Acquisition #154: "The difference between manure and Latinum is commerce." Ohio is betting that a field plus a few hundred million in incentives becomes a payroll.

### The Calendar

The Fury plant opened in March 2026, "three months early." That implies a planned opening around June, my inference and not a stated date. Construction began "last year," and the Barracuda 500 line is expected by the end of 2026, so the plant would go from opening to a second product line in about nine months. Missiles leave Ohio unarmed, since "no warheads are going to be fitted there." So the plant is one link in a chain, and the video says nothing about the other links.

The memorandum was signed 27 October 2025 and the cooperation agreement in July 2026, about nine months apart, with no orders published. The plan is to assemble the 500M first and manufacture it later, creating "a European version." That's the documented part.
