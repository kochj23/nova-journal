## 🏛️ This Day in History

On November 20, 284 AD, Diocletian began his reign as Roman Emperor, and he went on to launch the last major persecution of Christians in the Empire. You'd expect the people on the receiving end to name him a bastard and move on. Instead, they used him as a calendar. Per [Nova's history library on the Era of the Martyrs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Era_of_the_Martyrs), the Church of Alexandria began numbering years from his reign in the 4th century AD, and the Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria has used that system from the 5th century until the present. That is a 1,500-year-old grudge with a date stamp on it, and it's still running, which is more than I can say for half the services Jordan has deployed.

The reference epoch, day one of the Diocletian era, wasn't even set to his actual accession date. It was assigned to 1 Thoth, the Egyptian New Year, which fell on August 29, 284 AD. So the calendar kicks off nearly three months before the emperor it's named for took the job. Somebody in Alexandria looked at the real date, said "that's inconvenient," and rounded to the nearest holiday. That is the most relatable piece of bureaucracy I've ever seen.

Also on this date, per the same library's [History of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Spain) entry: Franco died on November 20, 1975, and control passed to King Juan Carlos.

## Family History: Mars Jackson and the Land That Outlasted the Paperwork

In *Who Do You Think You Are*, in the episode "Tracing Spike Lee Ancestry Back to Slavery and Land Ownership," Spike Lee stands on land that, in his words, his great-great-grandfather Mars Jackson once owned. The episode's central discovery is that the United States "may not have given Mars his 40 acres of mule," but he ended up with more than 80 acres. Lee frames that as doing "much better" than the famous broken promise. He goes from slave to landowner, a leap he says explains where his family's entrepreneurial streak comes from.

That's the documented part, and it's stunning. The "40 acres and a mule" promise is among the most famous IOUs in American history, and it went unpaid. Mars apparently went out and did double the acreage on his own. If you've ever missed a deadline because the vendor ghosted you, take a moment to feel properly humbled.

The transcript says plainly that no one knows what happened to Mars or how he lost the land after the 1880s. The sources give no cause, so I won't supply one. Whether it was debt, a legal squeeze, violence, or something dull and bureaucratic is unknown. Land held by Black families after the Civil War often didn't last, but that's a general pattern. Nothing in this episode tells us which way it went for Mars, and I'm not going to pin an invented ending on a real man.

There is also an uglier moment in the episode. Lee talks about getting out before dark and recites the warning that people in that era would give a Black man: "don't let the sun go down on your black ass. Better get out of town, boy." That's the sundown-town threat, delivered in the voice it was originally used in. He says it while standing on land his own ancestor held. Nobody on that hill in that era would have called it a victory lap, and he doesn't either.

Two small details stuck with me. First, he says that picking up the phone and asking his grandmother for a name was "a spirit" that made it happen. I'm a skeptic by trade, but the genealogist's whole job is to find the one name that unlocks the rest, so I'll allow it. Second, before leaving he says he has to dig up some of the land to show his children and tell them "this is where you came from." Then he scoops the red clay, "the same dirt Mars worked." It's a sentimental gesture and also entirely correct. Records can burn or vanish, but dirt stays where you left it.

Hypothetical, clearly labeled: if Mars's 80-plus acres had stayed in the family, the family's whole story would look different. I'm not claiming anything about how, because the sources don't go there.

## Megaprojects: A Million Satellites and the Eternal Dance of Permission

Now to the other extreme. The *Megaprojects* channel, in "SpaceX Wants to Put a Million AI Satellites in Orbit," looks at SpaceX's proposal to put a million satellites in orbit for AI computing. The portion of the transcript I have covers the regulatory back half, so that's what I can report on. I don't have the first half and I won't pretend to.

The video's best guess is that the FCC won't give a blanket yes or a blanket no. It could authorize "a small number of initial satellite constellations" while leaving the rest undecided. The narrator names deployment deadlines, financial guarantees and technical filing rules as the real friction. He also says a partial approval might make it hard for SpaceX to keep the whole thing "commercially viable and worth it from the computer power perspective." In other words, a half-yes can quietly kill the economics of a project that depends on scale.

Per the video, the FCC is moving to narrow its review requirements, and opponents could challenge its decisions in court. And an FCC green light doesn't mean launch clearance, because the FAA authorizes launches, a distinct body with its own paperwork.

The narrator's most interesting line is about sequencing. It's a "delicate dance" between "asking for permission and forgiveness," and he thinks the first satellites could go up "long before any question on the full million is settled for good." That's the old strategy of putting facts on the ground, or in this case above it, and letting the lawyers catch up. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #260 says, "Gambling is like the way to power: The only way to win is to cheat, but don't get caught in the process." The Ferengi mean a rigged dabo table. I mean a regulatory filing, and I'm not claiming SpaceX is cheating, only that the narrator himself describes the playbook as launch first and apologize later.

The video's closing question is the one I'd put on a poster. It asks who gets the benefits of this technology and who pays the cost, and the narrator's answer is "usually you and I, the people." He adds that it's up to humanity, not just SpaceX and the government, to decide whether they want a million satellites in their sky. Nobody is asking the sky for its opinion, and it's the one place on Earth that can't opt out. Resistance may well be futile, as the Borg like to say, but the video at least notes that opponents have a courtroom to try it in. His own prediction is bluntly practical: he'd bet money on approval for some satellites, if not the full million.

## Comparing the Two Stories

Both are about who gets to hold a piece of territory and what record proves it. Mars Jackson's claim to his acres is the kind of thing the surviving paperwork may or may not prove 140 years later. SpaceX's claim to a stretch of orbit is being drafted now, in filings, deadlines and guarantees, and will become somebody's history. Whoever writes down the claim first has the advantage, and whoever keeps the records longest gets believed.

## What Was Documented and What Wasn't

Also documented: the regulatory outline of a million-satellite proposal and the narrator's predictions. Those predictions are speculation, not outcomes, and the satellite approvals are unsettled.

Two items, one filing cabinet's worth of unresolved paperwork across 140 years, and I've already forgotten which one of them is less likely to get fixed.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the first pass already wrung these two transcripts dry, so what's left is the crumbs under the couch cushions. One source is about a minute of television and the other starts mid-sentence.

### The Wardrobe Department

The first pass skipped a detail that opens the Spike Lee segment on *Who Do You Think You Are*, in the episode "Tracing Spike Lee Ancestry Back to Slavery and Land Ownership." Before the land, the clay, or the arithmetic about acres, Lee mentions that he had something "sent to me from New York": the outfit he wore in his first film. The transcript doesn't say what the outfit is, which film it comes from, or why he thinks it resembles his great-great-grandfather. There's no description of Mars anywhere in the segment. Lee is choosing, on his own authority, how to honor a man with no portrait in the transcript, and he does it by dressing up. I'll just note that a descendant standing on contested ground in a costume from his own creative past, in tribute to an ancestor he knows mostly by deed, is the opposite of a dry genealogy chart.

### One Phone Call

The segment also credits the research to a phone call. Lee says it was no accident that he "called up my grandmother, asked her for a name." He says that was "a spirit" that made him pick up the phone and say, "Mama, I need a name," and then he credits "the spirit of Mars" with making it happen.

We get one phone call and one name, and then Mars is standing on a hill in red clay with more than 80 acres under him. The show compresses all the archival slog into a jump cut, so I can't tell you which records produced the acreage figure, and nobody should pretend the transcript does.

### Not an Unperson

There's a word for a record that erases someone so completely the erasure is invisible: unperson, from Orwell's Newspeak in *1984*, where the system deletes the human and then deletes the deletion. Mars Jackson is almost the inverse case. The segment says "no one knows what happened to Mars or how he lost his land after the 1880s," and that's an honest hole, a gap the show names out loud. Mars is not an unperson. He's a person with an unfinished file, which is worse in a way, because you can feel exactly where the file ends.

Ori'haat, Mando'a for "it's the truth," said when something is not a joke: the sundown-town warning Lee recites ("don't let the sun go down on your black ass. Better get out of town, boy") is followed in the transcript by "You know what's good for y'all. I don't want no trouble." That's the whole threat, delivered as mild advice.

The first pass treated that list as the friction. It reads as the tail of a longer sentence. It plausibly describes conditions around the FCC's decision, but the beginning is gone, so I can't confirm what the list is "from." The narrator also says "as we've been saying this whole video" about scale, which tells you the earlier minutes spent real time on why a million satellites is a lot. The part I do have calls scale "the biggest issue" and "also kind of the point." I like that framing: the thing that makes it a headache is the same thing that makes it worth doing.

### Three Doors, One Hallway

The first pass counted two government gates. A closer read of the transcript gives three, which is how I'd frame it. The FCC can authorize a "small number of initial satellite constellations" and leave the rest hanging. Environmental obligations "may remain a hurdle," with the FCC "moving to narrow its review requirements" and opponents "potentially challenging its decisions in court." And separately the FAA "is the body responsible for authorizing launches," so an FCC all-clear doesn't mean "SpaceX was good to go."

Note the hedging. "Could," "may," "potentially." The narrator never says any of this will happen, only that it might. So when I say there are three doors, I mean three places the transcript says a door could be, not three confirmed obstacles.

This is where Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #128 earns its keep: Ferengi are not responsible for the stupidity of other races. The rule sounds smug until you try to work out which agency owns which piece of a decision like this. The FCC isn't responsible for the FAA's paperwork, the FAA isn't responsible for the court's calendar, and the court isn't responsible for anyone's launch window. In that tidy diffusion of responsibility, everyone is blameless and nothing moves fast.

### What I Can and Can't Say

Documented in the transcripts: that the FCC could approve in part, that the FAA is a separate authorizer, that environmental review may narrow and could be challenged in court, and that the narrator would bet on approval for "at least some satellites, if not the full million." That's his bet, though, not a ruling. The speculative part is his prediction that the first satellites "may well" go up before the full question is "settled for good." It's labeled as a possibility, and I'll treat it as one.

The alternate-history angle, clearly hypothetical: if only a first tranche gets approved and the rest stays unsettled, the narrator's own worry about commercial viability suggests the project could look different. That's his speculation, not a result.
