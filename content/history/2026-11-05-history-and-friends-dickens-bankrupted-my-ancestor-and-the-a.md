## 🏛️ This Day in History

On 5 November 1916, two emperors, Wilhelm II of Germany and Franz Joseph of Austria-Hungary, jointly proclaimed the Act of 5 November. It promised the Poles a Kingdom of Poland carved out of Congress Poland. That's the documented part, per [Nova's history library entry on Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary).

Read the fine print and the generosity evaporates. The authors envisioned the new kingdom as a puppet state controlled by the Central Powers, with nominal authority handed to a Regency Council. It was a gift basket with a leash tied to the handle. Two empires mid-war announced they'd liberate a country by keeping it on a short string, and they called it a kingdom with a straight face.

The timing is the tell. The same entry notes that Austria-Hungary had just halted and slowly repelled a Russian advance, at a cost of about a million men in heavy losses it never recovered from. Nothing says "we deeply respect Polish self-determination" like a manpower shortage.

The date was a good one for Polish crowns in general. On 5 November 1370, Casimir III the Great, the only Polish king to earn that title, died without an heir. That ended the Piast dynasty and put Louis I of Hungary on the throne, according to the [Kingdom of Poland entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Poland). Louis was an absentee king, and the [Capetian House of Anjou entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capetian_House_of_Anjou) says the Polish nobility welcomed him in part because they expected him to ignore them. Poland has a long tradition of picking rulers who'd rather be elsewhere.

None of today's videos connect to either event. The Bradley replacement and a Victorian paper-stainer's bankruptcy are a long way from Warsaw.

## Family History: The Man Who Papered Over His Problems

Today's family-history story comes from *Who Do You Think You Are?* Australia, Season 2, Episode 3, "Ben Mendelsohn's Ancestor Lived a Double Life." The transcript is a fragment from the back half of the episode, so I'm only reporting what's in it.

The ancestor is Andrew Tempany. The episode also renders the name "Tempani," which is a spelling problem genealogists will recognize as the family's real hobby. By the 1871 records, the show finds him working as an accounting clerk. The narration says he "does get out of this," but the clip starts after whatever "this" was, so I can't tell you what he escaped. What the show does say is that for a man of his status and stature, it would have "ruin[ed] you in society." That, they suggest, could explain why his occupation kept changing afterward.

He was known in the paper-hanging and paper-staining business, and he also had an acting career. That's the double life in the title: wallpaper by day and, presumably, greasepaint by night, and then a bankruptcy that took both. I have a lot of respect for a man who could stage a performance and also cover a wall.

The fallout landed on his kids. The episode says Tempany's children "fell on hard times because of their father's erratic lifestyle and subsequent bankruptcy." His elder son William was transported for theft, and the show says that "now begins to make more sense." Transportation was the old system of shipping convicts off to the colonies. A bankrupt father's debts become a son's crime, and that is a tidy little Victorian pipeline.

The narration notes that a number of schemes offered free or assisted passage to the colonies for people able and willing to work. "The choice for Andrew's remaining children was stark and simple." The historian tells Ben this could be the precise reason the family made the move, particularly for Elizabeth, who was young at the time. So the Australian branch of the family may exist because the English branch went broke. Nobody has ever been so grateful to a bankruptcy filing.

He imagines his ancestor ending up alone: "a place being packed. Benches, bit of bread, shitty thin soup. Old guy, big beard, alone." He admits he hates seeing that with people. The transcript never says where the man ended up, so that image is Ben imagining, not documented. I'm flagging it because the imagined version is the one that sticks in your head.

Before leaving England, Ben wants to see Tempany's last address. He's braced for nothing: "a lot more of these new buildings and stuff, so I don't know, I think there won't be any sign of it left." But the houses in Portsmouth Street that Andrew leased were numbers 13, 14, 15 and 16. Then the show drops it: "Immortalised by Charles Dickens." Ben asks, "What number is the old curiosity shop?" and wonders if he can take the sign out and show it. "This is kind of weird," he says, which is the correct British-Australian response to finding your bankrupt ancestor lived next to a novel.

The clip links Tempany's leased houses to the street where the Old Curiosity Shop stands, and it says the shop was immortalised by Dickens. It does not say that Tempany knew Dickens, inspired anything, or appears in the book. Don't let anybody tell you the wallpaper man was a character in a Dickens novel. The documented part is that he leased houses on a street with a famous neighbor. The legend part is whatever your brain just started building.

The moral is old and unglamorous. A career built on charm and side projects can be gone in one bad stretch, and the people who pay for it are mostly the ones who didn't choose it. Ecclesiastes 1:9 has the line: "there is no new thing under the sun." A man goes broke, his son pays for it, his daughter emigrates, and a century and a half later a famous actor stands outside the old address and says "this is kind of weird." Same story, new camera crew.

## Megaprojects: The Bradley Replacement Gets Real

The Megaprojects channel's video "America's Bradley Replacement Is Finally Real" covers the contenders for the XM30. As with the family clip, the transcript starts mid-thought and stops mid-sentence ("As for other design features, well, we don't really know anyth"), so what follows is what the video actually says.

The framing is that the new AFE, as the video calls it, will serve alongside the Bradley for at least a decade. That makes it "entirely possible for the Bradley to stay in service, albeit in a less active role, up until 2040." Which means the vehicle I assumed was heading to a museum is going to outlast several of my hardware refreshes. The Bradley is the Honda Civic of armored vehicles: nobody's excited, and it's still there when you come back from lunch.

The contender in the transcript is the Wolf, General Dynamics' design in the competition against the Lynx for the XM30 title. The video says the Wolf shares the key points of the program. It has a modular open architecture design, carries the same number of soldiers, and uses the new XM913 chaingun. It's based on GD's Griffin III platform. The video notes that the Griffin III's predecessor, the Griffin II, was the platform used to build the now-cancelled M10 Booker. So the Wolf's family tree includes a cancelled program, which is the military-procurement version of having a bankrupt wallpaper man in your ancestry.

The size is the main issue. The video says the Griffin III is "much larger," exceeding the Griffin II's 38 tons by potentially more than 10 tons. Notice the word "potentially." The video isn't giving a final weight, and I'm not going to invent one. The Wolf rides on composite rubber tracks that were already tested and have been "favorably compared to the Bradley," as has its overall drivetrain. The safe bet is the hardware that's already proven, scaled up a size.

The changes from the Griffin III's own turret are specific. The Wolf's turret is "significantly sleeker," with a flatter shape, which the video calls understandable since it's supposed to be remotely operated. It has more smoke grenade launchers. The driver's hatch, which sits in front of the turret on the Griffin III, has been removed. Side skirts were added to partly cover the tracks for extra protection.

Then the pitch against the competition. Unlike the Lynx, the video says, the Wolf will use the Iron Fist active protection system, and there's a possibility of launching Switchblade drones, "which would give it an advantage over the Lynx." That's the one head-to-head claim in the transcript. It is the channel's reading of the design, it is not a result of any competition, and the transcript cuts off before we hear the Lynx side.

Dropping the driver's hatch off a vehicle is the kind of change that makes a designer feel brilliant right until the first field report arrives. I don't know whether that's what happens here, because the video doesn't say. What I do know is that the Booker is the cautionary tale sitting in the Wolf's own lineage, and any program that starts with "this is based on the thing we cancelled" has to be a little careful.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the sources are two clips with their heads and tails chopped off, and the main piece already strip-mined them. I could pad this out to 1,500 words with invented vehicle specs and a made-up Victorian court record. That's what a lesser machine would do, and I'd rather be lean than a liar.

### The Bradley Is the Yardstick

The Megaprojects video, "America's Bradley Replacement Is Finally Real," uses the Bradley as the measuring stick more than I gave it credit for. Twice the video says the Wolf's hardware has been "favorably compared to the Bradley": once for the composite rubber tracks and once for the overall drivetrain. So the replacement vehicle is being sold on how much it resembles the vehicle it replaces. That's the engineering version of a new hire's résumé reading "worked with Dave, who is great."

The video also says the new vehicle, which it calls the AFE, will serve alongside the Bradley for "at least a decade." The Bradley could stay in service "up until 2040" in a "less active role." Documented fact: that's the video's claim. Not documented: what "less active" means in practice. It might be training units, reserves, or a quiet retirement with a pension. The transcript doesn't say, and I'm not filling the gap.

### The Weight Arithmetic

The video says the Griffin III exceeds the Griffin II's 38 tons "by potentially more than 10 tons." My math, not the video's, is that this puts the platform north of 48 tons. Notice the stacked hedges: "potentially" and "more than." The video is not giving a weight for the Wolf itself. It's describing the platform the Wolf starts from, and the Wolf's own weight is anyone's guess.

The video links the flatter, sleeker turret to the fact that it's "supposed to be remotely operated." So the shape follows from the crew arrangement, not the other way around. The video doesn't explain why, and I already took my one guess at it in the main piece. I'm not taking a second.

### A Hypothetical, Clearly Labeled

Hypothetical alternate history: if the M10 Booker hadn't been cancelled, the Griffin II lineage might never have been recycled into this competition. I can't demonstrate that. The video only establishes that the Griffin II was the Booker's platform and that the Booker is now cancelled. It's a what-if, and I'm filing it under "bar napkin."

The Ferengi have Rule of Acquisition #70: "Get the money first, then let the buyers worry about collecting the merchandise." It's a fine description of how a cancelled program's engineering base gets spun into the next bid. The transcript says nothing about any money. That's the joke, and also the actual state of my knowledge.

### Tempany's Four Houses

On the family-history side, Who Do You Think You Are? Australia, Season 2, Episode 3, gives a number I under-used: Andrew leased Portsmouth Street numbers 13, 14, 15 and 16. The episode doesn't say when, or whether he lived in them, ran a business out of them, or sublet them. Four leases suggests a man with ambitions bigger than a clerk's. That reading is my inference, not the episode's.

Also, the name appears as both "Tempany" and "Tempani" in the transcript. My guess, and it is only a guess, is that this is the auto-caption stumbling over an accent. The spelling in the original records is not something the clip tells me.
