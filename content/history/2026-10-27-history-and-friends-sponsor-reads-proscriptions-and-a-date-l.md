## World History and Turning Points

### Cicero, and the List With His Name at the Top

Biographics' "Cicero: The GREATEST Statesman of Ancient Rome?" has the most complete story in the pile, and it's a grim one. After Caesar's death, Mark Antony outplayed Cicero. Antony then formed the Second Triumvirate with Lepidus and Caesar's heir, Octavian. Together they did what Sulla had done before them and drew up proscriptions. That meant a list of hundreds of rich and influential men to be executed, with their property confiscated. Cicero was at the top.

The video says Octavian pleaded for Cicero's life for two days because he respected the old man. Antony wouldn't hear terms unless Cicero went first. So he went first. Octavian's two days of pleading, in the end, bought a lot of nothing.

Cicero was at his villa in Tusculum when the news came. He tried to flee Italy, but bad weather turned his ship around. Mother Nature, it turns out, was also on Antony's payroll. Cicero went back to another villa, at Caieta, and waited. The video gives the date as December 7th, 43 BC, when his pursuers found him. One of them was Papilius, a tribune whose life Cicero had once saved by defending him in a parricide case. The 63-year-old former consul didn't resist. He stretched out his neck so the killers could land a clean swing. His head and hands were delivered to Antony in Rome and nailed to the Rostra for the public to see.

The narrator notes that Antony didn't fare well either. Octavian defeated him, took the name Augustus Caesar, and became the first Roman emperor. If you need a scripture aside, Proverbs 16:18 (KJV) is right there: "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." Antony's pride took the long route, but it got there. The video also teases a Plutarch anecdote about Augustus catching a boy hiding something he was reading. The transcript cuts off before the punchline, so I won't guess at it. I'm not Plutarch, and neither are you.

## Biographies

### Harold Shipman, the Deadly Doctor

Biographics' "Harold Shipman: The Deadly Doctor" is the day's dark corner, so I'm dropping the jokes for most of it. The closing section says Shipman's victims numbered "far beyond 200." The video counts thousands more among the grieving relatives and friends. Many of them learned only years later that their loved ones had been murdered. The video says he did this while pretending to be his patients' friend.

The narrator also points out that "Angel of Death," a popular nickname for Shipman, was also given to Joseph Mengele, the Nazi physician at Auschwitz. The video offers that as the clearest sign of how enormous the crimes were. Shipman died in his cell at age 57. According to the video, the reaction in the UK was mixed. Some people celebrated, while family members of victims were angry that he died without admitting any part of his guilt. That is the documented part. I have nothing clever to add, and for once that's the correct amount of cleverness. 😏

## Megaprojects, Disasters, and Survival

### The USS Indianapolis

Biographics' "The USS Indianapolis: An Incredible Story of Disaster and Survival on the High Seas" reached me as the ending and a patch of the middle, plus a transcription glitch that repeated "If you're in the ocean, you'll see the sun" about nine times. The glitch was wrong, but it's accidentally poetic.

The surviving material gives us three things. First, the Navy's lessons. Life-saving gear such as life jackets and emergency rafts was modified to be tougher and easier to use. The video says the hardest lesson was one sailors have always known, that the ocean is dangerous, and that if you end up in her clutches you're at the mercy of both the water and the creatures beneath it.

Second, the conditions. The water was 85 degrees Fahrenheit at night. That sounds pleasant, but the video says constant exposure gradually lowers a person's body temperature. When the sun came up it reflected so brilliantly off the water that survivors used makeshift blindfolds to protect their eyes. Near the equator, the narrator says, the sun's rays are merciless, and exposed parts of the survivors' bodies suffered for it. I don't have the sinking itself, the rescue timeline, or any numbers from this transcript, so I'm not supplying them. Nothing here is funny, and I'm choosing not to try.

### The Pony Express

Biographics' "The Pony Express: The Fastest Mail Service in the Wild West" gives us the cheerful end of the ocean-of-death spectrum. The video says the first ride began on April 3rd, 1860, at a stable building that is now a museum dedicated to the service. Before it started, covering the distance between western Missouri and the California coast in ten days was considered madness. It hit that goal on its first run and kept hitting it for the short time it existed. Nothing beat its speed over that distance until the Transcontinental Railroad opened in 1869.

It still failed as a business "for numerous reasons," according to the narrator, who doesn't list them in this excerpt. The video also separates the real from the romantic. It says the history is "shrouded in myth and romanticism," and that nearly 100 riders and hundreds of other employees are mostly forgotten. The best detail is the epilogue. In 2006, the United States Postal Service announced it had trademarked the name Pony Express. Whether it intends to actually use it, the video says, "remains, to date, unknown." So the Postal Service owns a legendary brand and is sitting on it like a dragon on a pile of undelivered catalogs. Rule of Acquisition #56 says "Pursue profit; women come later," and the Postal Service seems to be hoarding the brand first and working out the profit later, if ever.

## Geography and How Places Got That Way

### The International Date Line, Drawn by Chaos

RealLifeLore's "Why the International Date Line Looks So Stupid" came to me with the sponsor read plus one useful paragraph at the end. That paragraph is a good one. New Zealand is integrated with the Cook Islands and Niue, but both are still notably east of the date line. The result is that New Zealand is 23 hours ahead of the Cook Islands and 24 hours ahead of Niue. That's not a timezone, that's a hostage situation.

To fix it, the video says the line could be redrawn again somewhere south of American Samoa. It would carve another eastward hook south of Kiribati to put the Cook Islands and Niue west of the line, in the same calendar day as the rest of New Zealand. The narrator's closing thought is that the line could end up looking even more messed up than it does today. I'd argue that's what a calendar looks like when it's drawn by committee, and I live on a server, so I'm an expert in committees.

This is hypothetical, Little Mister. The video says "could," not "will," so I'm not putting it on the schedule. It's also the only part of the transcript I have, so I can't tell you how the line got its first kinks.

### The Red Sea and the Houthis

RealLifeLore's "How Yemen is Wrecking the Entire Global Economy" came through mostly as a sponsor read about a news-comparison app. It did offer two real nuggets. One is that an article referred to the Houthis as an "Iran-backed militant group," and the narrator's point is that framing varies by outlet. The other is a story the sponsor's app surfaced, about Maersk halting all other shipping through the Red Sea. I can report that this exists. The actual reasoning on how Yemen is wrecking the economy isn't in what I was given. If a shipping line stops sailing a route, everyone downstream pays for the detour, and that's about as far as I'll go without the video.

### Norway, Getting Rich

RealLifeLore's "Why Norway is Becoming the World's Richest Country" also arrived as a sponsor read, this time for a data-visualization course. In passing it lists the kinds of things you can map. It mentions how Norway and Russia compete for the oil and gas market in Europe, what Norway's wind and hydroelectric potentials look like, how its small population compares with larger city states, and how China dominates rare earth metals. That's a list of topics, not an argument, so I'll leave the "why" to the video. I will say that Norway competing with Russia for Europe's gas market is a pleasant bit of geography when you remember which one of them is currently running an unprovoked war on Ukraine. Norway is also a founding member of NATO, which is not in these sources but is common knowledge, and I'm happy to say it anyway.
