## 🏛️ This Day in History

On November 16, 1972, the General Conference of UNESCO adopted the Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage, which means every "World Heritage Site" plaque you've ever photobombed traces back to one very long day of diplomats agreeing on a single goddamn text. According to [Nova's history library entry on World Heritage Sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Heritage_Site), the final wording came out of a draft UNESCO had initiated, and all parties eventually signed off on one version. International consensus on anything is rarer than a Hue bulb that updates on the first try, so credit where it's due. I'm not proud. I'm just noting it.

The convention didn't come into force until December 17, 1975, because bureaucracy is the only force in the universe that moves slower than my NAS sync.

As of November 2024, 196 states have ratified it: 192 UN member states, two UN observer states (the Holy See and the State of Palestine), and two states in free association with New Zealand, the Cook Islands and Niue. The convention also set up a reporting system, giving the committee an overview of how each nation is implementing it plus a "snapshot" of current conditions at the protected properties. That's the documented part, and it's a surprisingly functional piece of global homework.

The lone holdout among UN members is Liechtenstein, which is one of those facts that sounds made up and isn't. Almost 200 countries agreed to protect the planet's greatest hits, and one tiny alpine nation just muted the call and went to get a sandwich.

Same date, different continent: the library also notes that Slovak students demonstrated for democracy in Bratislava on November 16, 1989, kicking off the revolution that ended Communist rule in Czechoslovakia. Read the [full Czechoslovakia entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Czechoslovakia) for that one.

Little Mister, it's Monday, which in history terms means a bunch of people are dead, a few of them are suspiciously dead, and one road refuses to exist. The Biographics crew has spent the weekend on murderers, spies, and a dead emperor's kid. RealLifeLore has been busy telling us where you can't drive, fly, or swim. ## The Kid Who Was Emperor (Briefly) and the Man Who Wouldn't Let Him Leave

### Napoleon II

Biographics' "Napoleon II: Emperor for 20 Days" starts where the story gets sad. Napoleon Francois Charles Robert Bonaparte, called Franz in Vienna, grew up under his grandfather, Emperor Franz. When an expedition came up that would have meant what the narrator calls "a very interesting confrontation, and most likely an awkward family reunion," Grandpa stepped in and said no. The young officer was stuck on garrison duty. By all accounts he was a good officer, which is a hell of a thing to be when the world is waiting for you to be a legend.

Respiratory ailments dogged him. His physician, Dr. Malfatti, first suspected recurring pneumonia, then got the worse diagnosis: consumption, now called tuberculosis. Malfatti described Franz as having "an iron spirit in a body of crystal." The narrator says it's so badass he wants it for his next Baldur's Gate 3 character, and I agree, though I'd also like it on a server rack. Franz spent the first three months of 1832 on ordered bedrest. In May he was made a Colonel, the last gift his grandfather gave him. On July 22, he died at Schönbrunn Palace in Vienna, age 21.

The doctors argued a warmer climate might help, such as Italy, where his mother was in Parma. According to the writer André Castelot, Chancellor Metternich vetoed it. That part is the documented claim, attributed to Castelot. What came after is legend: later conspiracy theories said Metternich plotted to poison Franz as a potential dangerous enemy. Castelot's own take, per the video, is the colder one. Metternich didn't need poison, he just had to let Franz die, and that wouldn't take long. Poisoning is a thriller. Bureaucratic neglect is a Tuesday. I know which one I find more believable, and I've read your inbox.

Hypothetical, flagged as such: if Franz had gone to Parma, would warm air have saved a man with tuberculosis? The video doesn't say, and I'm not going to invent a sequel. Memento mori, by the way, is Latin for "remember you will die," and a 21-year-old with an imperial surname is the cruelest possible illustration of it.

## Two Spies, Two Murderers, and a Hitchcock Problem

### William Stephenson, retired and bored

Biographics' "William Stephenson: Spymaster" gave me only a slice, but it's a funny one. Stephenson, after a stretch on a little island retreat, got bored. Five years. A man who ran spies spent five years on a Jamaican beach and cracked like a cheap router. In 1951 the Stephensons sold up and moved to New York City, into a lavish apartment taking up an entire floor, living right above Greta Garbo. The most famous recluse in Hollywood was his downstairs neighbor, and I'd pay money to know how many times he considered bugging the ceiling. He went back into corporate America "without as much aplomb as he had before" and encouraged development and investment opportunities. The video's own framing is that the spymaster was now a civilian, and that's the part I like: retirement is the one operation nobody planned an exit for.

### Dr. Crippen and the problem with a torso

In "Dr Hawley Harvey Crippen: A Notorious Transatlantic Murder Mystery," the fragment I have is entirely about doubt, and it's good doubt. The video says that in recent decades Crippen's guilt has been questioned. The key evidence at the trial was a scar on the torso, and some now say it was just a skinfold. Skinfold. A man's neck stretched on that.

Then there's the logic problem, which I'll give the narrator full credit for. If Crippen murdered and mutilated his wife and got rid of the head and limbs so thoroughly they were never found, why bury the torso in his own basement? The narrator's gloss for the confused: yes, a torso was found, no, nothing else was. Then, in 2007, an American team of scientists claimed DNA from the torso didn't match Cora's distant relatives, "although not everyone is buying their story." The Criminal Case Review Commission has so far refused to reopen the case.

Carpenter's The Thing has a blood test: heat a wire, touch it to each sample, and the infected blood jumps. Verify each sample in isolation, never trust the group. We have one sample, a disputed scar, and a 2007 test that, if it holds, says the sample might belong to somebody else. Nobody's holding a hot wire. Everyone's just standing in the outpost, tired, saying "nobody trusts anybody now." The documented facts are the scar dispute, the missing remains, the 2007 claim, and the refusal to reopen.

### Harry Thaw, or how a fortune beats a courtroom

Biographics' "Harry Thaw: The Original Killer Playboy" gives me the ending, and the ending is infuriating. On July 16, 1915, after two trials, Harry Thaw was declared not only legally sane but also a free man, since, as the narrator explains, he couldn't be tried again for the same murder he'd already stood trial for. The narrator admits he didn't think double jeopardy was a real thing. It is, and the lesson was that it works best for people who can afford to find out. The district attorney called it a gross miscarriage of justice, made possible only thanks to Thaw's vast fortune and his mother's own machinations. The narrator's dry summary, "rich people don't go to jail the same way, do they?", is one of the few lines in this entire issue I'd hand-stitch onto a pillow. Did Thaw learn a lesson? "No." Once he was free, the transcript cuts off at "he divorced," and I refuse to guess the rest.

One man possibly convicted on a skinfold. One man acquitted on a fortune. Justice, as documented in both cases, is whoever has the better evidence or the better mother.

### Hitchcock, brilliantly and awfully

Biographics' "Alfred Hitchcock: Hollywood's Master of Suspense" was written, the host says, by Radu Alexander, and the fragment I have is the dark part. After a film whose standing "has improved somewhat with time" and is "widely considered one of Hitchcock's best and most memorable works," the narrator notes "the most disturbing aspect" was the director's obsessive relationship with leading lady Tippi Hedren. It reportedly continued into their next film, Marnie. After Hitchcock's death, Hedren accused him of cruelty and abuse. That's as much as I'll say, because that's as much as I'm going to repeat here, and it's the documented part: she made the accusation.

## Geography, Or Why You Can't Get There From Here

### The gap in the Pan American Highway

RealLifeLore's "Why a Single Little Spot Makes Driving Across 'America' Impossible" is about the Darien Gap, and the fragment contains the line I like most: it's "highly likely that the Darien Gap will remain a gap in the Pan American Highway System for quite some time, and there's not much that you can probably ever do to change that." That is the most honest sentence ever spoken about a megaproject. Somewhere between the roads people dreamed up and the roads that exist, there is a jungle that said no.

Foot and mouth disease is still a common ailment among cattle in South America, but it's been eradicated in Central and North America since the 1950s. The video says there's "a lot of evidence to suggest that the absence of any roads or paths across the" gap plays a role, and the transcript ends there, which is a cliffhanger worse than a streaming season finale. A pothole has more civic purpose than my entire cron schedule.

### Which mountain is actually tallest

In "Why Everest Isn't Earth's Highest Mountain... sorta," the fragment gives me Chimborazo. It sits only 1 degree and 28 minutes south of the equator, which gives it a "pretty substantial boost in height from the Earth's bulge." Its summit is about 3,967 miles from the Earth's center, roughly 1.2 miles further out than Everest's. So measured from the center of the planet, Chimborazo wins. Measured from sea level, the sea doesn't care.

### Why planes avoid Tibet

RealLifeLore's "Why Planes Don't Fly Over Tibet" has a less dramatic answer than you'd hope. Long-haul flights between Europe and Asia avoid Tibet because airlines don't want an emergency while flying over it. Shorter domestic flights are scarce too, because "there's just so few people there." The Tibet Autonomous Region in China has just a little over 3 million people.

## Survival Stories You're Not Supposed to Believe

### John Capes and the 270-foot escape

RealLifeLore's "The Man Who Survived for 18 Months On an Island After His Submarine Sank" is the issue's best case for being skeptical of a good story. The fragment says submarine commanders had earlier been ordered to bolt their escape hatches shut from the outside to keep them from being blown off during a depth charge attack. Capes' claim was that he escaped from 270 feet. There were no witnesses. He had a reputation as a great, natural storyteller, and his account changed over the course of decades after the war. The claim made things "even harder to accept for most people."

What's documented: he made the claim, the claim changed over time, and nobody saw it. What's legend is the 18 months on an island, since the fragment doesn't actually describe it. I'll hold that line until a source gives me more. A story told differently every decade is the one thing in history more dangerous than a depth charge.

### The piranha, defended

"How to Safely Cross a Piranha Infested River" gives me one factual nugget. A group of piranhas can take down prey much larger than themselves, hypothetically speaking, partly because when they bite they don't chew. They rip off a chunk of flesh and swallow it straight down to the belly, then keep snapping. I trust you'd rather be told this than shown it.
