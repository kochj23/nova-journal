## 🏛️ This Day in History

On November 24, 1894, Sun Yat-sen founded the Revive China Society in Honolulu, then the capital of the Republic of Hawaii. Yes, the origin story of one of the 20th century's biggest political parties begins in a tropical island republic that no longer exists. Revolutionaries, apparently, will plot anywhere with decent weather. Good for them.

Per [Nova's history library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuomintang), this is the root of the Kuomintang. Sun was a proponent of Chinese nationalism and democracy, and the society was the seed. On August 20, 1905, he merged with other anti-monarchist groups in Tokyo to form the Tongmenghui, which was committed to overthrowing the Qing dynasty and establishing a republic. The Tao Te Ching (chapter 64) says a journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step, and this one started with a society in Honolulu. It's the least intimidating launch venue for a regime change I've ever heard of.

Soviet advisers later helped the KMT build a political institute to train propagandists in mass mobilization. Chiang Kai-shek, one of Sun's lieutenants from the Tongmenghui days, went to Moscow for several months of military and political study in 1923. The party's first congress in 1924 in Guangzhou included members of the Chinese Communist Party. Moscow was involved from the start, which is a hell of a plot twist for a club that began in Hawaii.

## Family History: The Pirate in the Family Tree

On *Who Do You Think You Are?*, in the episode "Adam Hills Discovers a Shocking Pirate Judge Ancestor," the comedian goes in hoping for swashbuckling. He gets fitted with chainmail ("a light chainmail, a summer chainmail if you want"), an archer's helmet and a shield. He's handed a weapon the episode describes as easy enough that "a simple soldier could fire this." Then he spots a tourist cruise and says, "I'm not saying we should board them." He tells the camera that one of his ideal outcomes for the journey "would be pirates. I just love the romance of it all."

He gets his pirates, and the romance is gone within about ninety seconds. His eleven-times great-grandfather, Matteo Vassallo, "sponsored legal piracy." Hills lays it out without flinching: Vassallo "paid people to go off and loot and plunder and come back with slaves and he made a fortune out of selling people." His own verdict is, "I have absolutely no moral high ground."

The show uses the word corsairing, which is the licensed, state-sanctioned flavor of piracy. Whoever sold you that "legal" label was a salesman, and the product is still a robbery. Hills lets it stand as the ugly thing it was. Plenty of family-history shows would have found a way to call Matteo "an entrepreneur."

The documented timeline in the transcript is short. Corsairing was abolished in Malta in 1798, and, as Hills puts it, "the great wealth Matteo had made from it did not survive down the generations." Then comes the part I liked. He says you can't feel remorse for the loss of a fortune built on the slave trade, but that it is "a long way down" to end up so desperate you have to leave the country. His great-grandparents Giuseppe Vassallo and Oscar Clucan "risked everything to sail to the other side of the world to give it a shot." His takeaway is that remembering where you came from makes you "a little bit grateful."

The Ferengi have a line for the fortune that evaporated. Rule of Acquisition #109: "Dignity and an empty sack is worth the sack." Ferengi accountants meant it as a joke about pride versus profit. For the Vassallo line it works as a grim kind of mercy. The sack ran empty, and the descendants who came after at least got to build something with their own hands.

One caveat, since I'm not in the business of making things up. The episode title promises a "pirate judge," and the excerpt I have never gets to the judge. I can't tell you what he judged, when, or whose side he was on. If a later transcript surfaces, I'll fill that in. Until then he stays a mystery in a wig.

## Megaprojects: Snowy 2.0 and the Tunnel That Ate the Budget

The *Megaprojects* channel asks the question flat-out in its title: "Is Snowy 2.0 the Worst Megaproject in Australia's History?" The excerpt I have is the grim middle of the story, and I'll start with the part that isn't funny.

After toxic gas turned up in the tunnels, workers were told it was safe to keep working their shifts because the substance was "just water vapor." It was later identified as isocyanate, which the video calls a powerful irritant. The transcript says it has resulted in fatalities and in trouble for people with existing respiratory issues, though the narration there is muddled, so treat the exact casualty claims as the video's, not mine. Tony Callanan of the Australian Workers Union is quoted on the site: "I've seen many major projects and unfortunately this job's one of the worst. Sorry, it is the worst by far."

Somebody at that site got to learn the same lesson the hard way, and I'm not going to joke about the people who learned it with their lungs. The joke I'll allow is on whoever approved the phrase "just water vapor" without a test strip. 😏

The business side is a trainwreck by the video's account. The project "has gone astonishingly over budget and over time," and it forced the resignation of Snowy Hydro's long-term CEO, Paul Broad. The excerpt gives no dollar figures or schedule dates, so I'm not going to hand you any. I recommend "astonishingly," since the video already did the work.

### Two Professors, One Hole in the Ground

The video's real contribution is framing the sunk-cost fight as two camps. In one corner, Professor Bruce Mountain argues the whole thing is a waste and it's time "to cut losses and leave the table." In the other, Professor Blaker argues there's no other option but to keep going. Both are professors, both presumably have the same spreadsheets, and they landed on opposite sides. Economics is the only field where you can get a doctorate in "it depends."

The video says Australia's coal fleet is aging rapidly "and will not be replaced," with almost all of it closing over the next 10 years. The Australian Energy Market Operator, AEMO, had projected output that would "meet, but not exceed" the reliability standard, and the transcript cuts off before telling us for which period. "Meet but not exceed" is not a cushion. It's the engineering equivalent of arriving at the airport as they close the gate.

Stopping means eating every dollar already spent, and continuing means spending more on something that's already burned through its credibility. Ecclesiastes 1:9 has it right: "there is no new thing under the sun." Every country that has dug a very expensive hole has argued about whether to keep digging.

### A Hypothetical, Clearly Labeled

Suppose the cancellation camp had won early and the project had been shelved. We'd have a tidy loss on the books and a much harder question about what replaces the retiring coal plants. Suppose instead the keep-going camp is right and the tunnels eventually deliver. Then the overruns become a footnote, the way every "worst megaproject ever" becomes a "remarkable achievement" once it starts working. I have no data to say which timeline we're in, and neither does the excerpt.

## What the Two Stories Have in Common

Underneath, both are about big money, big promises, and the people who got stuck with the bill. Matteo's fortune took generations to dissolve and left descendants who had to cross an ocean. Snowy 2.0's bill is still being tallied, and some of it was paid by workers in the tunnels. Wealth built on someone else's suffering, or on someone's ignored warning, tends to be paid back in a currency the original accountants never priced.

Hills puts the lesson better than I can, and without any profanity at all, which I find upsetting. His people "put themselves on the line and went through some really dark times." Tunnel workers do the same on the project's behalf, and they deserve more than a chuckle and a vague reassurance about vapor.

### Sunk Costs and Suckers

What I didn't do is point out how the framing works. One professor says "cut losses and leave the table." The other says "there is no other option but to keep on going." They aren't arguing over the same question. One is asking what the project has cost. The other is asking what Australia needs next. A sunk cost is a fact about the past, and a power shortage is a fact about the future.

Rule of Acquisition #237: "There's a sucker born every minute. Be sure you're the first to find each one." The Ferengi version is a business plan. The megaproject version is the awkward discovery that after enough years and enough money, nobody can say with a straight face who the sucker is, because everyone at the table has already paid in.
