## 🏛️ This Day in History

On October 28, 1856, a man named Majid declared himself Sultan of Zanzibar, which is the kind of move you make when your dad dies and the family group chat has already turned into a knife fight.

Sultan Said bin Sultan died, and his sons, each with their own rivalries and ambitions for the throne, immediately started quarreling over the empire. Brother Bargash tried a sneaky play. Reasoning that Majid wouldn't yet know their father was dead, he came ashore in secret and tried to seize the palace at Mtoni and the fort in Zanzibar town. He couldn't round up enough supporters, and the attempt fell apart. Nothing says "heir material" like a coup that fails for lack of RSVPs.

Majid then made it official with that October 28 proclamation, and a ship went to Oman to deliver the news. Oman did not respond with a fruit basket. Thuwaini, the elder son, had been appointed heir apparent back on July 23, 1844. He had also long served as his father's governor in Muscat and commander-in-chief of the Saidi forces. He refused to acknowledge Majid and promptly tried to take Zanzibar back by force of arms.

Per the record, the government of British India, alarmed by the struggle, got involved. Nothing makes an inheritance dispute cleaner than a colonial empire taking an interest.

## Megaprojects and Engineering

### How Safe Is Nuclear Power, Really?

The Megaprojects video "How Safe Is Nuclear Power Really? The Deaths-Per-Terawatt-Hour Data Explained" makes a statistical argument, and it's a decent one. The headline figure is that nuclear takes 33 terawatt hours of energy production to kill a single person. The video says nuclear appears far less dangerous than the most common methods of power generation today. It also says nuclear compares well even against similarly safe renewables like solar and wind.

The interesting part is the claim that nuclear may be safer than the numbers show, because of how the Chernobyl figure is built. The WHO's roughly 4,000 deaths is an estimate of total excess deaths across all time, so it counts deaths that haven't happened yet. The terawatt hours that nuclear plants will produce over that same period are left out. It's like grading a student on every mistake they'll ever make while only counting the homework they've turned in so far. The narrator adds a conditional: if no major disasters occur in that window, which "wouldn't be strange" given that 25 years separated Chernobyl and Fukushima, the real rate would look lower still. The fragment cuts off right there, so I can't tell you how the video finishes the thought.

The video explicitly says we can't predict future disasters on the scale of Fukushima and Chernobyl, or whether deaths scale up as nuclear grows. The "it might be even safer" argument is the video's reasoning, not a settled number.

On the policy side, the video says the UK advanced several new-build nuclear construction projects in 2025 and extended operations at some existing facilities until 2028. It also says Heysham 2 was recently confirmed as the most valuable green electricity asset in the country's history. Its summary is that the atomic age "is going to be with us for a while yet." Nuclear has been "the future" since before your parents bought their first microwave, and it's still dutifully showing up to work. Respect.

### Megaprojects and the Fairness of Counting

The Megaprojects video, "How Safe Is Nuclear Power Really? The Deaths-Per-Terawatt-Hour Data Explained," opens in our fragment with "money where their mouth is." That's the policy section, where the narrator lays out what governments are doing rather than what they're saying.

What I skipped is the accounting trick. The video says the WHO's roughly 4,000 figure counts "excess deaths" across all time, meaning deaths that haven't happened yet are already in the numerator. That is a forecast being treated as a tally, and the video's complaint is that the denominator doesn't get the same courtesy.

Here is the hypothetical, clearly labeled. If both sides of the fraction were projected over the same period, and if no major disaster occurred in that window, the video argues the rate would fall below the headline 33 terawatt hours per death. That is the video's argument, not a measured result. It also depends on the "if no major disaster" assumption. The narrator himself says we can't predict disasters on the scale of Fukushima and Chernobyl, or whether deaths scale up as nuclear spreads. So it's a bet with its hedges showing.
