## 🏛️ This Day in History

On November 15, 1939, a funeral turned into a protest, which is what happens when an occupying regime shoots a student and then acts surprised that other students have opinions. Jan Opletal had been shot during a demonstration on October 28. His burial on November 15 became another demonstration, and the occupiers' answer arrived two days later: on November 17, the Czech University and every other Czech institution of higher learning in the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia was shut down. They stayed closed until the end of the war. That date is now marked as International Students' Day.

The backdrop, per [Charles University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_University_in_Prague) in Nova's history library, is a thorough campus takeover. Reichsprotektor Konstantin von Neurath handed the university's historical insignia to the German University, renamed Deutsche Karls-Universität in Prag. On September 1, 1939 it was placed under the Reich Ministry of Education in Berlin, and on November 4 it was proclaimed a Reichsuniversität. Nothing says "we deeply respect scholarship" like confiscating a school's ceremial jewelry and then padlocking the rest of the place. 😏

The straight facts end there. The note documents the shooting, the burial-turned-demonstration, the closure and the date's later commemoration, and that's all I'll claim.

### Napoleon II, the Emperor Who Wasn't

Biographics' "Napoleon II: Emperor for 20 Days" gives us the sad back half of a very short life. The young man, called Franz in the video, was Napoleon Francois Charles Robert Bonaparte. He wanted to join an expedition, and the video speculates this would have been "a very interesting confrontation, and most likely an awkward family reunion." Emperor Franz, his grandfather, stepped in and stopped it. So he got garrison duty instead. By all accounts he made a good officer, but his health kept sliding. His breathing was the problem.

His physician, Dr. Malfatti, first suspected recurring pneumonia, and then the diagnosis got worse: consumption, now called tuberculosis. Malfatti's line, which the host admires enough to want for a Baldur's Gate 3 character, was that Franz had "an iron spirit in a body of crystal." I'll admit that's a hell of a sentence. It's also a hell of a thing to say about a kid who's slowly dying, so let's all feel slightly guilty about how cool it sounds. 😏

Franz spent the first three months of 1832 on ordered bedrest. The doctors argued warmer climates might help, specifically Italy, where his mother lived in Parma. According to the writer André Castellot, as the video relays it, Chancellor Metternich vetoed that. Later conspiracy theories claimed Metternich plotted to poison Franz as a potential dangerous enemy. Castellot himself argues no poison was needed: Metternich "simply had to let Franz die," and sadly that wouldn't take long.

What the source documents is the bedrest, the diagnosis, the vetoed move to Italy per Castellot, the promotion to colonel in May 1832 (the last gift from his grandfather), and his death on July 22nd at Schönbrunn Palace in Vienna, aged 21. The poisoning is a later theory, and the video itself leans toward the "didn't need to" reading. I'd call that one a shrug with a cape on.

## Geography and How Places Got That Way

### The Darien Gap: Where the Highway Gave Up

RealLifeLore's "Why a Single Little Spot Makes Driving Across 'America' Impossible" is about the Darien Gap, the missing stretch in the Pan American Highway System. The excerpt opens with the conclusion, which is that the gap will "remain a gap... for quite some time, and there's not much that you can probably ever do to change that." Cheerful as hell. Hope you weren't planning a road trip.

One reason is biological. Foot and mouth disease is still a common ailment among cattle in South America, but it's been eradicated in Central and North America since the 1950s. The video says there's "a lot of evidence" the absence of roads or paths across the gap contributes to this, and then the transcript ends mid-thought. So a road isn't just a road. It's a disease corridor, and this swamp has apparently been doing unpaid quarantine work for decades. Curse your sudden but inevitable usefulness, you wet bastard of a jungle.

### Why Everest Isn't the Highest Mountain (Sorta)

RealLifeLore's Everest video makes a lovely nerd point: Chimborazo, sitting just 1 degree and 28 minutes south of the equator, gets a substantial height boost from the Earth's bulge. Its summit is 6,384 kilometers above the center of the Earth, about 3,967 miles, which the video says is two kilometers, or roughly 1.2 miles, farther out than Everest's summit. So measured from the center of the Earth, Chimborazo is the highest you can get anywhere on the planet. Everest wins on altitude above sea level. Chimborazo wins on "who's actually the farthest from the middle." The planet is fat around the waist, and a mountain got lucky.

## Family History

### Ross Kemp and the Brother Nobody Mentioned

Who Do You Think You Are?'s "Ross Kemp Discovers Forgotten Family Secrets in Victorian Records" is the most human thing in the pile. Kemp finds a relative whose records show prison time, a mental hospital, and an inebriate reformatory. One document shows Portsmouth Corporation Mental Hospital, in 1914, meaning the man hadn't served his sentence. One court document lists larceny with a sentence of three weeks, plus three years in an inebriate reformatory. Kemp reads it with obvious dismay: "Is this in the hope that three years without alcohol is going to make him clean? Is that what they're hoping?"

Kemp, who says he's been to prisons in the UK recently, points out that he sees mental health and addiction issues there today, and so "in one, two, three, four generations, very little's changed there." All of this has happened before, and will happen again, as the Battlestar Galactica people say, which is a fatalist's way of saying we keep building the same institution and expecting a different result.

Kemp says the man "wasn't remembered by anybody... even by his own brother." Kemp's great-grandfather never mentioned him to his daughters, and the story was never passed down to Kemp's mother or to him. That's how families edit history: not with lies, but by never saying the name. A whole life, filed under nothing.
