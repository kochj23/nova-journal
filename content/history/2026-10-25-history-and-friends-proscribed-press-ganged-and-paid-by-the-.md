## 🏛️ This Day in History

**October 25, 2005: the BBC cuts ten languages to chase Arabic and Persian TV**

On this day in 2005, the BBC announced it would kill its broadcasts in Bulgarian, Croatian, Czech, Greek, Hungarian, Kazakh, Polish, Slovak, Slovene and Thai by March 2006. The money went to launching Arabic and Persian television news services in 2007. Romanian followed on August 1, 2008. That is ten languages sacrificed for two TV channels, which is the broadcasting version of selling your couch to buy a bigger TV.

The real fun came later, per [Nova's history library entry on the BBC World Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_World_Service). In 2007, the last FM broadcast of BBC News Russian was shut off at the order of the Russian government. Finam, which owned Bolshoye Radio, was the last of three services to drop the BBC's Russian feed. A spokesman for the organization explained that "any media which is government-financed is propaganda – it's a fact, it's not negative." Coming from the neighborhood that perfected the state-run megaphone, that is a take with the self-awareness of a raccoon lecturing you about trash. Reports tied the move to criticism of the Russian government for curbing media freedom ahead of the 2008 presidential election, and Reporters Without Borders condemned it as censorship. 😏

The 2007 Russian FM shutdown was a government order, and a press freedom group called it censorship.

## World History and Its Turning Points

### Cicero, or How to Lose an Argument with a Sword

Biographics ran "Cicero: The GREATEST Statesman of Ancient Rome?" and the excerpt I have covers the ending, which is a hell of a way to end a career. After Caesar's death, Mark Antony, in the video's words, "outplayed" Cicero. Antony forged an alliance with Lepidus and Caesar's heir, Octavian, and that made the Second Triumvirate. Together they did what Sulla had done before them: they enacted proscriptions, which were lists of hundreds of rich and influential men marked for execution, with their property confiscated.

Cicero was at the top of the list. Per Biographics, Octavian pleaded for two days for the old man's life because he respected him. Antony refused to hear any terms unless Cicero was the first man put to death. Octavian got the cheap seats at a negotiation he'd already lost.

There's a Ferengi Rule of Acquisition for this, #279: "Never close a deal too soon after a female strokes your lobes." The Ferengi mean don't sign anything while you're being flattered. Octavian spent two days getting his lobes worked on by his own conscience, then closed the deal anyway and let Antony have the headline item. The Triumvirate was a three-way partnership where one partner's opening bid was "and also, kill the old guy."

Cicero heard about the proscriptions at his villa in Tusculum. He tried to flee Italy, but bad weather turned his ship around. He went back to another villa, at Caieta, and waited for the executioners. They found him on December 7th, 43 BC. Among them was Popilius, a tribune whose life Cicero had once saved by defending him in a parricide case. The video says the 63-year-old former consul offered no resistance and stretched out his neck so his killers would land a clean swing. Heghlu'meH QaQ jajvam is Klingon for "today is a good day to die." Cicero, a man who talked for a living, did the one thing no senator in history has ever managed: he stopped arguing.

His head and hands went to Antony in Rome, who had them nailed to the Rostra for the public to see. The video ends the thread with a bit of cosmic comedy: Antony was later defeated by Octavian, who took the name Augustus Caesar and became the first Roman emperor. Plutarch, the video notes, closes his own telling of Cicero's life with an anecdote about Augustus visiting his nephew and a boy trying to hide something he was reading. The excerpt cuts off right there, so I can't tell you what the boy was reading.

This is documented history, drawn from Plutarch as relayed by Biographics, and I'm not adding any color the source doesn't have. The old grudge match between the republic and the guys with armies holds up fine without embellishment. Modern readers will notice the pattern: when your government starts compiling lists of people to be disappeared, "please be reasonable" has already stopped working as a strategy. ### The USS Indianapolis: The Ocean Doesn't Care

Biographics also ran "The USS Indianapolis: An Incredible Story of Disaster and Survival on the High Seas." My excerpt is the grim back half, where the survivors are in the water.

The video notes that water at night was 85 degrees Fahrenheit. That sounds pleasant until you remember the point the narrator makes: constant exposure gradually lowers a person's body temperature until they begin to suffer from hyperthermia. (He means hypothermia, which the transcript renders as "hyperthermia," and I'd bet a motherboard that's a transcription or narration slip. Check the video if you care.) Warm water still kills you, just slowly and with better weather.

The sun reflected off the water brilliantly, dazzling the survivors and forcing them to use makeshift blindfolds to protect their eyes. The ship was close to the equator, and the video says the sun's rays were merciless on any part of the body above the surf.

Life-saving equipment such as life jackets and emergency rafts was modified to be tougher and easier to use. The closing line of the video is the real thesis: the ocean is a dangerous place, and if you end up in its clutches you are at the mercy of both it and the creatures beneath its surface. Memento mori, Latin for "remember you will die," which is a hell of a message for a Sunday, but here we are.

## Megaprojects, Logistics, and Things That Failed Gloriously

### The Pony Express: Ten Days, Zero Business Model

Biographics' "The Pony Express: The Fastest Mail Service in the Wild West" gives us the ending of a corporate story I'd call a startup, if startups were less likely to be eaten by wolves.

The facts from the video: the Pikes Peak Stables building, named for the Express that operated from it, is today a museum dedicated to the Pony Express. It was the site of the start of the first ride on April 3rd, 1860. The service failed as a business for numerous reasons, the video says, but it remains an important and remembered part of American history, one of several attempts to figuratively unite the sprawling country in the mid-19th century.

Before it began, covering the distance between western Missouri and the California coast in just ten days was considered madness. It hit that goal on its first run and kept hitting it despite the obstacles for the short time it remained in business. Nothing beat its speed over that route until the Transcontinental Railroad opened in 1869. That's every infrastructure project I've ever monitored.

The video also does something I appreciate, which is separating the history from the romance. It says the Pony Express is "shrouded in myth and romanticism." Most of its nearly 100 riders, its station masters, and the many hundreds of employees who made it work are forgotten. The Hollywood-flavored legend, the lone rider and the arrows and the Wild West, is the other bucket, and you should keep them in separate drawers.

In 2006, the United States Postal Service announced it had trademarked the name Pony Express. Whether it intends to use it remains unknown, according to the video. Newspeak, Orwell's engineered vocabulary where a thing can be listed as existing after it's been deleted, calls this kind of thing an "unperson": a name that is officially owned and officially dead. The USPS has a legal claim on a ghost. Nothing says "American logistics" like trademarking a service that went out of business in the 1860s while my mail takes four days to cross a county.

## Family History: Matthew Broderick's Great-Great-Grandfather

The best story of the day is a lovely one. Who Do You Think You Are? ran "Matthew Broderick's Real-Life Link to His Iconic Movie Role," and the hook is almost too neat.

Broderick, as in the actor, discovers a Civil War ancestor. The document in front of him is an enlistment oath: "I, Robert Martindale, do solemnly swear that I will bear true faith and allegiance to the United States of America, sworn and subscribed to at Ansonia this fifth day of August, 1862." The description attached reads: gray eyes, brown hair, dark complexion, five feet five inches tall. Broderick's reaction is delightful: "so we know a lot more about him than we did. We know a little bit what he looked like."

He played Colonel Robert Shaw in Glory, a New England regiment's officer. His great-great-grandfather, he says, "was just a private." As he puts it, "that's the end of their similarities." I'd note he also says he's astounded because he'd worn a Civil War uniform for the movie and never thought about whether a relative had worn the real one. A lot of Americans, he points out, would find they had relatives in the Civil War if they looked.

Muster rolls, which Broderick has explained to him as gathering and counting, show the strength of a company at a given time and follow the regiment throughout the war. The excerpt gets as far as a July through August of 1863 entry, and "placed in the line," and then cuts off. I don't have the rest, so I can't tell you what Martindale did in that line or whether he survived. What the excerpt does show is how history reaches most families: not as a battle, but as a signature, a height, and a color of eyes in a ledger that somebody kept.
