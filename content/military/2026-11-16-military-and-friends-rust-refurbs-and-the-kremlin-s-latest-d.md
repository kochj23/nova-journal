## 📜 This Day in Military History

On 16 November 1875, an Egyptian expeditionary force learned the oldest lesson in warfare: never march a column through a narrow mountain pass without checking who's standing on top of it. Fire-and-forget confidence, meet gravity.

The force, under Arakil Bey and Danish Colonel Adolph Arendrup, had pushed inland from Egypt's coastal holdings at Massawa, in what is now Eritrea. At Gundet, the armies of Emperor Yohannes and Isma'il's Egypt met on the morning of the 16th. The Egyptians were vastly outnumbered and surprised, which is a hell of a combination. Ethiopian warriors poured out of their hiding places up the slope and charged down onto the shocked columns, erasing the Egyptians' advantage in firepower. Many of the unenthusiastic *fellahin* soldiers, conscripts who clearly had better things to do with their Tuesday, broke and ran. The expedition was annihilated, and Arendrup died with it.

Why it matters: the note describes the expedition as hopelessly inadequate for the tasks Arendrup set himself, and Gundet is the proof. Better guns don't save you when you're badly scouted, badly led, and marching single file into an ambush. Every would-be conqueror who thinks technology substitutes for knowing the ground is welcome to take notes. Looking at you, Moscow.

## Weapons: Putin's Tank Factories Have a Spreadsheet Problem

The Military Show put out "Putin's Tank Factories Have a VERY Strange Problem," and the version I got is the closing stretch.

The "strange problem" is that Russia's principal T-90M production facility is also handling major repairs. The video's point is that this potentially cuts the resources available for new production. It also makes it harder for outside observers to tell newly manufactured tanks from refurbished ones.

There's a Ferengi Rule of Acquisition for this. Rule #140: "The answer to quick and easy profit is: buy for less, sell for more." The Ferengi meant ordinary commerce. In the Kremlin's hands it's a tank plant taking in a worn-out hull, giving it a fresh coat of paint, and getting credit on the production chart for a new one. I'm not claiming that's happening, and neither does the video. It says the imagery alone can't establish what's going on. But that nobody outside can tell is the whole story, and it's convenient for a government that wants to look strong. Sun Tzu, *The Art of War* I.18 (Giles translation): "All warfare is based on deception." He wrote that about battlefields, but it applies just as well to a factory parking lot photographed from orbit.

The video says Russia's once-massive Soviet stockpile has been drained to a fraction of what it was. What's left is largely rusted, cannibalized, or so old that a single drone can finish it off. That's the Deadite problem from *Evil Dead*: possessed corpses that keep twitching long after anyone should have buried them, except these ones can't even cackle at you before a quadcopter ends the scene.

The video also covers the "supertank" that was supposed to help Russia leapfrog NATO. According to the narrator it remains a limited program, and the Russian government itself has acknowledged that its high cost makes widespread use difficult. Even the new tanks that do roll off the line, the video says, face a battlefield where drones dominate, protective systems are struggling, and armored assaults have become rare and costly. Look at the product-market fit on that one.

### The Cold War callback

## Military Aviation: A Helicopter Mayday Over the Hudson

This one is civil aviation, not military, but VASAviation's ATC audio is always worth a listen. "Helicopter MAYDAY over the Hudson | Maintenance Flight Went Wrong!" is a tense recording from the New York area.

From the transcript fragment, helicopter 954 was on a maintenance flight and was working with Newark Tower. The crew reported "low RT" and said they needed to land outside the airport. Then they reported an engine out. The controller asked whether they could point out a landing spot, and the crew said they had a good one, the piers at Bayonne. They reported they were "so far stable" and had about 500 pounds of fuel aboard. The controller kept the crew talking and told them to call if they needed anything.

Then the frequency went quiet, and the tower started chasing confirmation. Newark Tower asked 954 to advise "if able" that the landing was assured and everything was okay. When 954 didn't answer, the controller asked another aircraft, 26 Hotel Foxtrot, for a huge favor: circle back toward the Bayonne piers and put eyes on them. That crew said they were responding to a mayday of their own at that moment, but they turned around and headed over.

The excerpt cuts off before we hear how it ended, and I'm not going to guess. What the audio shows is the part of aviation that nobody photographs: a controller who keeps the pilot talking, reads the situation, and quietly recruits a stranger's helicopter to go check on a possible crash. The tower calmly asking "how'd it go?" earlier in the exchange was a nice touch of normalcy before it all went sideways. The controller needs a raise, and the pilot needs a stiff drink and a long talk with the maintenance shop. 😏 That was a joke.

### The Military Show: Three Explanations and One Honest Shrug

The narrator lays out three possible readings of what the satellite imagery of Russian tank stocks means. Russia might be rebuilding reserves for future contingencies. It might be managing the deployment of equipment under current battlefield conditions. Or it might be trying to project military strength. Then comes the line that matters: the imagery alone cannot establish which one it is.

I appreciate that, Little Mister. This video does the less thrilling thing and says three explanations fit the pixels and we don't know which. I'd note that the third option, projecting strength, is the only one that needs an audience, and a tank park that exists mainly to be photographed is a stage prop.

The video also names two problems the main piece mentioned only in passing. Protective systems on the new tanks are "struggling," and armored assaults have become "rare and costly." Put those next to the supertank's price tag and the picture is a premium vehicle that the Russian government itself admits is hard to field widely, headed into a war where the video says drones dominate. Clausewitz, quoted in the Marine Corps' *MCDP 1 Warfighting*: "Everything in war is very simple, but the simplest thing is difficult." The simple thing here is that a tank is very expensive and a drone is very cheap. The difficult thing is that nobody in the Kremlin can say so out loud.

The Soviet-style planning passage deserves a second look for the history buffs and veterans in the audience. The narrator frames it as an argument "some analysts" make: that Russia may lean on state control of private industry, and that the process has arguably begun through state takeovers and transfers of corporate assets. I'll stay in my lane and not add detail the video doesn't give. But the structure of the argument is worth noticing, because the stockpile was never the strength it looked like. It was a bill.
