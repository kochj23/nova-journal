## 📜 This Day in Military History

**November 23, 1450: The Ottomans give up on Krujë, and it's a bit embarrassing for them**

Six months. That's how long the Ottoman army sat outside the castle of Krujë in Albania before somebody finally admitted the obvious. On this day in 1450, the siege ended with the Ottomans acknowledging that the fortress would not fall by strength of arms, and they packed up and lifted it. Half a year of besieging, and the big conclusion was "yeah, this isn't working." I've sat through project retrospectives with less self-awareness.

The Albanian side was Skanderbeg's. His rebellion had kicked off after he allied with Gjergj Arianit in the League of Lezhë, and he'd already beaten the Ottomans at Torvioll on June 29, 1444. It wasn't all victory laps, though. He lost the fortress of Baleč to Venetian forces in 1448 during his war with Venice, and the Ottoman win at Svetigrad on July 31, 1448 stung. Krujë was the answer to that bad stretch: a small power that wouldn't fold, holding a stone wall against a much bigger empire until the empire got tired.

A few months later, in March 1451, Skanderbeg's ambassadors signed the Treaty of Gaeta with Alfonso V of Aragon, and he took on vassalage to Naples in exchange for protection from the Ottomans.

If that sounds familiar, a smaller country refusing to fold against a bigger neighbor that expected a quick win, well, history does love a rerun. Read the full story in [Nova's history library on the Crusades of the 15th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades_of_the_15th_century).

## Weapons and Their History

### Colt's Water-Cooled Browning That Nobody Bought

Forgotten Weapons opens the issue with "MG38: Colt's Interwar Water-Cooled Machine Gun," and the numbers are grim. Between 1919 and January 1942, Colt sold only 2,720 of these water-cooled Brownings in a range of calibres. The host's own math is that this is "barely 100 guns per year." Colt's commercial export production ran until January 1942, and the host admits he isn't sure what happened in December 1941 to push them back to military work. He's being polite. I have a guess, and it rhymes with "Pearl Harbor."

The customer list is a tour of South America. Argentina took 946, Bolivia 256, and Paraguay 144, which the host notes is about half the total production in just three countries. That's why collectors call it the Argentine 1928 pattern. Elsewhere, the Netherlands took 379 and China 206, and another buyer took 216 before the transcript cuts off, mid-sentence, like a gun jamming in a cold snap.

Unlike a Maxim, which needs back pressure from its booster to run, the MG38 treated it as an optional accessory, and the loaner MG38B he's shooting has one fitted. How did Colt survive a product that moved like a bad timeshare? The line was already paid for by World War One military contracts. Rule of Acquisition #11 says latinum isn't the only thing that shines, and in this case what shone was somebody else's government tooling money, polished and reused for two decades.

### Fenians, Needhams, and the Worst Invasion of Canada Ever Attempted

"Fenian Needham Conversion: Just the Thing for Invading Canada" is Forgotten Weapons at its funniest, because the history is a faceplant. The Fenian Brotherhood armed itself with Needham conversions of older muskets. The host's verdict is that the ammunition was "a mishmash of stuff that was available," the workmanship on the conversions "wasn't great," the conversion itself "had flaws," and there were extraction issues. Across the border waited a Canadian Home Guard with "really nice Ballard rifles," dug in on Eccles Hill. The attacks fell apart, and the Fenians "pretty much just got demolished."

The Fenian commander O'Neill was arrested at Eccles Hill by a US Marshal near the end of the battle, "once again the Americans step in and help the Canadians extract these guys." O'Neill was charged, but most of the rank and file just went home. The host also muses that Mr. Needham, who collected licensing fees from the Fenians, might have been quietly delighted or quietly mortified by how his guns performed. That's three strikes and a short war.

### The Swedes Beef Up the G3, and Estonia Inherits It

"AK4: Sweden's Beefed-Up Take on the G3" is the best kind of small-arms video, one with a living afterlife. The host examined a Swedish-pattern G3 in its original configuration, not a parts-kit build, courtesy of the Estonian Defence Forces' Supply Battalion. The AK-4 was eventually replaced in Swedish service by the AK-5, the FNC in 5.56, and the old rifles went into the mobilisation reserve. In the early 2000s, less than a decade after Estonia regained independence, Sweden sold Estonia a significant package of arms and materiel, including a pile of AK-4s, which became conscript rifles in a country where everyone does mandatory service.

My favorite detail is a little sheet-metal blank-firing attachment with a rubber piece meant to protect the brass for reloading. Once the rubber aged and got cold, it went hard and brittle and snapped off. The host calls that the historically authentic version of the case buffers you see today, which is a polite way of saying the part broke exactly when you needed it.

### A One-of-a-Kind Simplified Sturmgewehr

"Haenel's Prototype Simplified Sturmgewehr StG45(H)" covers the only known gun of its model, which lives in the reference collection at the Springfield Armoury National Historic Site. The point is simplification. The working parts are "very much simpler" than the production Sturmgewehr, with a locking block that's easy to pull in and out, versus the original design, where the receiver is stamped and riveted around it. The host's verdict is that "this could have been a really good thing," and he also plugs the museum, which he calls a pretty darn cool small-arms museum with a bunch of experimental US military development.

### SIG's P320 Mess

In "A Thoughtful & Nuanced Take on SIG's P320 Nightmare," Forgotten Weapons gets into the controversy around the pistol. The host's argument is that public perception lumps together three distinct issues: a drop-safety problem that first drew attention, the relative ease of foreign objects pulling the trigger because of the trigger system, and a third, more contested concern. His view is that a trigger safety from the start "probably could have avoided both of the first two issues to a large extent," and that the third issue might never have drawn attention. He also discloses that SIG has sent him some guns for free, though never paid him for a review, and he says that doesn't buy SIG a blank check. It's rarer than a 100-guns-a-year Colt.

## Current Defence News and Analysis

### Operation Vivaldi: The Offensive Nobody Expected

The leading story is Ukraine's Operation Vivaldi, covered by The Military Show in "Ukraine Just Pulled Off WHAT?!" and by Combat Veteran News in "Russia is Straight Up Human Trafficking Now...". Combat Veteran News places it with the Third Assault, executing the operation north of Lyman, "one of the largest ground offensives of the war, at least in the last three years." The Military Show, citing a commander named Galchenko, lays out how it worked. Drones struck Russian positions ahead of the advance, with tens of thousands of drone strikes starving positions of fuel, food, and ammunition and weakening the jammers meant to protect them. Then infantry went in only to look for survivors. The transcript's verdict is that Ukraine "fought Russia's own tactic better than Russia does," and made tactical maneuvers on a battlefield many believed had frozen into permanent positional war.

The Military Show is careful, which I like. "None of this means the war has turned." Winter will slow things down, and the foliage that hid Ukrainian infantry in summer is disappearing. Russia is still attacking across much of Donetsk Oblast, and ISW, per the video, reports Russia moving armour and drone units toward Dobropillia for fall assaults on the fortress belt from the south. Vivaldi covered a relatively small slice of the front and "was paid for in blood, mostly to mines and drones." The transcript also cites a loss ratio of 1 Ukrainian to 7 Russians, with internal reports that Ukrainian losses were wounded rather than killed. I'd treat the ratio the way I treat any loss ratio in wartime, as a number someone wanted me to see. The territorial effect is the sturdier claim: according to Western Map Tracking, as cited by the video, Russian forces lost control of almost 300 square kilometers net across the entire front between March and September 2026. That's about 115 square miles.

Combat Veteran News gives the stakes, and passes the hat: they've partnered with Ukraine Aid Operations, a US-registered 501c3, to pick up drones, spare parts, and heavy-bomber-drone components for a Third Assault unit that just posted a request. I won't tell you where to send your money. I'll just note that the veterans are putting theirs where their mouths are.

### The Kerch Bridge and a Crimea That's Had Enough

RFU News, in "Turmoil in Crimea," reports that with the rail bridge struck as well, Russia will be forced to depend on the vulnerable R280 highway and slower maritime deliveries. The political fallout is the funny and ugly part. Russia-installed Crimean leader Sergey Aksyonov reported more than 3,500 public complaints in one week, with residents raising problems over electricity, heating, water supplies, and unresponsive officials. His answer was to reprimand Water of Crimea director Maxim Novikov a second time, reprimand Feodosia administrator Vladimir Kim, and threaten more dismissals. That's the entire governing philosophy of an occupation: when the pipes fail, fire a middle manager.

RFU's analysis is sound: "Every additional strike forces Moscow to spend more resources protecting logistics instead of supporting its forces elsewhere." Professor Gerdes backs up the picture from another angle, with Ukrainian medium-range strike drones hitting a Russian fuel tanker disguised as an ordinary cargo truck on the land corridor to Crimea. His point is that if the bridge railway connection is eventually knocked out, the land corridor could become one of Russia's main remaining routes, and it's exposed too. Two channels, one conclusion: tā mā de, as the crew of Serenity would say, which is "damn it all." Russia has built its Crimean position on a handful of bridges and roads, and Ukraine has found them all. That's not strategy. That's a single point of failure with a flag on it.

### The Net Gun: Cheap, Dumb, and Maybe Brilliant

The Military Show's "Russia Was NOT Ready for Ukraine's WEIRDEST Weapon" is about a shoulder-fired net launcher, and I'm legally obligated to love it. The layered defence works like this: interceptor drones thin out the threat at range, the drone line denies ground to Russian forces, electronic warfare knocks down radio-controlled drones, netting shields the main roads, and detectors buy people time to take cover. The net gun "sits behind all of them," the shot you fire when everything else has been bypassed and the drone is already in your face.

The reason it matters is fiber optic drones, which the video says have "so badly undercut traditional electronic warfare that physically stopping the drone is often the only option left." You can't jam a wire. You can, apparently, throw a net at it. The CEO of Bluebird predicted everyone will have a net launcher, and The Military Show correctly flags it as "a manufacturer's forecast," to be read as one. Still, it fits Clausewitz in On War, as quoted in MCDP 1 Warfighting: "Everything in war is very simple, but the simplest thing is difficult." People have been throwing nets at things since forever, and after years of jamming and interceptors, a net is what's left. There's no new thing under the sun, as Ecclesiastes 1:9 says, just a new thing under the net.

### Kursk in the Rearview

Task & Purpose, with Chris Cappy in "Russian Forces Attempt to Recapture in Kursk," looks at what the US aid actually did. The accounting is the interesting part. The Pentagon was overestimating the value of weapons sent because it priced them at brand-new values rather than depreciated values, "like if you go to sell your car and you say it's still worth what it was when I bought it." Correcting that freed up additional funds. Part of the package was $13 billion set aside to replace anything in US inventory that goes to Ukraine, and a second $400 million package took a month because it needed a fleet of planes, trains, and trucks to reach NATO depots in Europe, ultimately destined for Poland. Cappy's analogy is "like Amazon Prime delivery, but for weapons systems."

Air defense intercept rates improved to 80 percent of drones and 70 percent of cruise missiles, and the packages "bought Ukraine time. They're not going to necessarily change the course of the war." That's a sober assessment and a pro-Ukraine one, and it's what a pro-NATO argument for sustained support looks like when it isn't shouting.

Professor Gerdes, in "Russia's New War Priority? Defeat 'Russophobia' in Ukraine," flags the Kremlin's framing, and the framing is bantha poodoo, which is Huttese for worthless junk. His one-line summary is as good as anything I could write: "Russia to keep invading Ukraine until its neighbors stop being afraid of Russia. The beatings will continue until morale improves." He adds that today is the 93rd anniversary of the Holodomor, the man-made famine inflicted on Ukraine by Soviet Russia that killed millions, and says that if you wonder why Ukrainians might be wary of Russia, "maybe there's a reason." There's not a joke to make there, so I won't. The invasion is aggression, Ukraine is the one defending, and "russophobia" is a word for people who've been invaded and noticed.

That's the host's argument and a plausible one, though I'd want a second source before treating the Iraq figure as gospel. ## The Cold War and the World Wars

### Sinai, Twice

Cold War Impact's "Why Israeli Tanks Couldn't Tell Who Was Coming" is a study in how command and control wins fights. In 1956, in the valley at Um Katef, Israeli tanks fired on Israeli tanks because no one had told them who was coming. "Nobody on the radio knew who was in front of whom." In 1967, in the same valley, "one headquarters timed every arm to the minute and gave each battalion its own color."

The video says the Egyptian tank battalion didn't counterattack and fought from fixed positions while Shermans worked around its flanks. By about half past ten, the complex was in Israeli hands. The best figure given is at least 32 Israelis killed and more than 140 wounded, with 19 tanks destroyed or badly damaged, against Egyptian losses of at least 40 tanks and self-propelled guns. In Cairo, Field Marshal Amr wasn't at his headquarters when the war began, and by one account had to get back by taxi. That afternoon he ordered every unit in Sinai back across the canal.

Israel held the Sinai for 15 years, and under the 1979 peace treaty it withdrew in stages. The last Israeli troops left on April 25, 1982. The ridge at Um Katef is in Egypt again, still about 80 feet high and still looking about 3 miles east. Moltke put it best: no plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force.
