## 📜 This Day in Military History

Eighty-six years ago today, on 28 October 1940, Mussolini's Italy invaded Greece from Albania. Rome probably expected a stroll. Greece had a small, ill-equipped army and a quasi-fascist dictator, Ioannis Metaxas, who according to [Nova's history library on the diplomatic history of World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplomatic_history_of_World_War_II) leaned toward Germany. He still rejected Italian demands. Britain had guaranteed Greece's frontiers after Italy annexed Albania in spring 1939, and Athens sided with the Allies.

The stroll went badly. [The same library's History of Greece entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Greece) says the Greeks repelled the invaders after a bitter struggle, and it calls this the first Allied victory of the war. By mid-December the Greeks held nearly a quarter of Albania and had tied down 530,000 Italian troops. A major Italian counterattack in March 1941 failed too, which the library describes as humiliating for Italian military pretensions. Mussolini picked a fight with a country that couldn't afford to lose, and that country refused to lose.

Hitler had to secure his southern flank before invading the USSR, so he reluctantly launched the Battle of Greece in April 1941. Axis forces from Germany, Bulgaria and Italy came through Yugoslavia and forced the Greek defenders out. Greece lost in the end, but only after Berlin had to clean up Rome's mess.

Smaller, outgunned defenders can wreck a bigger aggressor's schedule, and the aggressor's friends end up paying for it. History has a dark sense of humor, and it keeps repeating this particular joke.

## Ukraine: A Drone That Saves Instead of Kills

Start with the good one. The Military Show, in "Ukraine Just Pulled Off Something HISTORIC on the Front Lines," covers a feat by Ukraine's 92nd Assault Brigade. A heavy drone, which the channel calls the Audag 6, hoisted a wounded soldier out of the fight. The channel's update is the part that matters: the operation was a success, the soldier was saved, he's undergoing treatment and rehabilitation, and his life is no longer in danger.

The channel frames this as the next step in a 2026 that has already seen Ukraine field a new generation of mid-range strike drones to hit the Russian rear in occupied territory. The argument is that moving medical supplies by road can cost critical time, especially for transplants. The official says these drones could move medicines, medical products, blood samples and donor organs between hospitals, including from remote areas.

My take: the world's most famous drone war keeps generating the world's most useful drone innovations, and the Russian state keeps generating the reasons. The Military Show is also honest that the same fleet is busy hunting Russian soldiers at the same moment, so don't mistake this for a humanitarian pivot. It's a country that has to be good at everything at once, because the alternative is being invaded by someone who isn't. Credit where it's due, and I will deny saying that.

## Poland Wants to Learn from the People Actually Doing It

Ukraine News NowUA, in a video with a title that screams about a "Moscow parade" being "eliminated" (I found nothing in the transcript supporting that, so treat it as thumbnail fiction), carries a more substantive nugget. It quotes Poland's Deputy Minister of National Defence, given in the transcript as Tomczyk, saying Poland is counting on joint drone production facilities, scaled-up technology exchange, and the chance to test its own drones and equipment in real combat conditions on Ukrainian territory. That includes FPV drones against enemy personnel and equipment, and interceptor drones against aerodynamic weapons.

The channel ties this to Poland's concern over Russia's repeated talk of a possible move from Belarus toward the so-called Suwalki Corridor. It also cites an exercise in which a simulated enemy took out roughly two battalions of personnel and up to 50 armoured vehicles, which it calls a cold shower for NATO leadership. The transcript is too fragmentary for me to tell you which exercise, who played the simulated enemy, or who won, so I won't guess. The channel's conclusion, in its own words, is that Ukrainian technology and combat experience have become a main and stable guarantor of European security.

That's a partisan channel talking itself up, but the underlying logic holds. NATO members who want to know how drones behave under real electronic warfare can either read manuals or call the people who've been fighting under it for years. Poland picked up the phone. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #249 says to respect other cultures' beliefs because they'll be more likely to give you money. Poland has respectfully noticed that Ukraine's belief in drone warfare comes with a combat-proven R&D budget, and would like to be invited to the party.

## Oleshky: A Ceasefire, a Skeptic, and a Politics Problem

Professor Gerdes covers the Oleshky humanitarian ceasefire across two videos, "OLESHKY Humanitarian Ceasefire Holds (For Now)" and "Sanctioned Tankers Are Heading to America." My transcript snippets are partial, so I can only report what Gerdes says. He hopes the evacuation succeeds and credits Ukraine for doing its best to supply Oleshky from the air. His worry is that the ceasefire "isn't a Russian trick," and he predicts Russia will try to funnel most of the evacuees back into Russia and claim they wanted to stay.

I'd stress that is the host's prediction, not a reported fact, but it fits a long and ugly record of Russian handling of civilians in occupied territory.

He notes the timing of the Oleshky deal so close to the US midterms and suggests it might be the only victory lap the American president gets before the elections. He predicts this week will bring the most intense pressure on Ukraine "for at least a long time," because after the midterms Washington's enthusiasm for pushing a deal may wane. He also discusses a Financial Times report about one of the president's sons hunting with a Putin-linked benefactor and meeting another oligarch, which he calls "a terrible look" while admitting he doesn't know what it means yet because it's too early. Congress, per the other video's title, has apparently been united against recent White House decisions, with even Republicans asking what is going on.

## Russia's Wallet Is on Fire

Combat Veteran News, in S01E0167 titled "Putin's Going BROKE! RU Army Spending SPIKES!", spends its runtime on the Russian economy. The host's argument is that "every factory is basically a Shahed factory" and "every factory is basically a tank factory," which is one way to run a country, and not a good one. The danger he describes: at some point Russians will need an exportable good like food or washing machines, and a war economy doesn't make those.

The alternative, the host says, is peace, which would mean the government can no longer support the war industries that sprang up, and mass unemployment and lost tax revenue would follow. His summary is "there's no good scenario for Russia here," just flavors of terrible. He adds that if current rates persist while the rest of the economy grows at its current pace, 30 to 35 percent of tax revenue would go to service interest on debt, and that Russian government analysis could be thrown in the trash can. Treat that as one commentator's projection, not an audited number, but the structural point is sound. The man who started this is the only person who could end it, and he's running out of road. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says there is no new thing under the sun, and "empire overspends on war and discovers arithmetic" is about as old as that sun gets.

## Counter-Drone Gear Comes Down to the Squad

Task & Purpose, in "Inside Project Flytrap - The Army's answer for small drones at the squad level," looks at how small-unit counter-drone gear is being tried out. Soldiers were seen with several rifle-style jammers, including both the tactical and Mark 4 variants of the Drone Gun from Drone Shield, the Drone Buster and the Drone Defender. The video describes these shoulder-fired devices as jamming GPS and control signals within a few hundred yards, which can push a drone into a fail-safe mode like return-to-home or crash it. Soldiers were also seen wearing the Wingman drone detector and the Pitbull personal jammer, which work as a pair: one spots the drone, the other jams it, without all the bulk.

The channel argues this kind of wearable, lightweight kit matters as formations return to more dispersed operations. It compares the moment to the Global War on Terror and the IED threat, then the transcript glitches into a loop, so I'll say the comparison is theirs and leave it there. The exercise scenarios reportedly mirrored problems seen in Ukraine: kamikaze quadcopters, GPS jamming and denial environments where some radar and command-and-control systems were degraded, and payload diversity, including simulated ISR platforms.

Every Western exercise now borrows its homework from Ukraine. The cheap quadcopter has gone from a nuisance to a doctrine problem, and the Army has noticed the squad can't wait for a battalion-level system to show up.

## Taiwan's Plan to Sink an Invasion

Also from Task & Purpose, "Taiwan's plan to sink an invasion" focuses on unmanned surface vessels. The transcript fragments describe Taiwan's boats as small, low-profile, hard to distinguish from civilian craft in a cluttered environment, low in heat signature, able to stay under radar coverage, and deployed in numbers.

The video also notes China's own unmanned boat, about the size of a rigid-hull patrol craft and tested in autonomous mode with integrated sensors and a small weapons payload. In theory, Chinese USVs could screen, intercept or hunt Taiwan's drone boats. The channel's caution is that this all depends on reaction time, sensor fidelity and targeting bandwidth. It also points out that China isn't shy about going after fishing vessels, which is exactly why a drone boat that looks like a fishing boat is a nasty headache for a defender. I only have fragments, so I can't give you more on numbers or timelines, and I won't invent any. Resistance, as the Trekkies say, is futile only if you let the other guy swarm first.

## The Wings Desk: What Almost Was, and What Actually Happened

In "P-63 Kingcobra - In Defense of America's Overlooked Fighter," the verdict is blunt. Giving the Kingcobra to the Soviet Union is what saved both the aircraft and Bell, since it gave the P-63 a purpose it didn't have with the US and provided jobs and profit, "kinda," in the way Lend-Lease worked and didn't work. It also kept Bell in the fight to try again, while building B-29s at the time. Later Bell went into jets without much success, then produced, as the channel puts it, one of the most famous choppers of all time. The host's summary is a three-part no: the P-63 would not have added tactical value, would not have been worth the transition costs at the operational level, and would not have justified revisiting Army Air Force standardization.

"He 162 - Germany's Desperation Fighter" tells the Axis version of the same lesson. The jet had a real technological edge on paper, but the numbers that became operational were in the low two digits, so production potential and operational use were out of balance. The channel calls it low maintenance in some ways, though engine complexity and quality made it a mixed bag, and it was not an easy plane to fly. The unanswered question the plane's own planners never solved: where do all the pilots come from?

That ties neatly into "Masters of the Air - The German Reaction to US Bombing," which argues that German production surged under an emergency program while pilots, especially seasoned ones, and fuel ran short. The host says the Allies had to beat the bulk of the Luftwaffe first, which took roughly two to three years per theater: the Mediterranean in early-to-mid 1943, then Europe in mid-to-late 1944. Put the three videos together and the common thread is clear: planes are the easy part. Pilots, fuel and a functioning industrial base are what win air wars, and a wonder weapon with no one qualified to fly it is just an expensive museum piece with a head start.

For something gentler, "Inside The Cockpit - P-51D Mustang 'Twilight Tear'" is a gauge-by-gauge tour: manifold pressure, coolant temperature and pressure, engine RPM, carburetor air temperature, the oxygen flow regulator, the APS-13 rearward warning system, a hand crank for the canopy, and a bomb release switch on top of the stick. It was filmed at a flying-condition collection in Granite Falls, Minnesota, and the host urges viewers to buy something from the gift shop, which is the most Mustang-friendly advice of the day. Groovy.

## A Veteran's Story: Fallujah

American Veterans Center's "CORPSMAN UP! He Ran Into Gunfire for his Marines | Fallujah" is not a story to joke about, so I won't. The corpsman describes trying to treat a fallen Marine after others dragged him out of the line of fire. He ran through his assessment: airway, breathing, circulation. He couldn't find a pulse, and then realized his fingers were inside the man's wrist. The Marine's lips were blue. He knew it was over. They had to move the body away from the house, and he describes how heavy and difficult that was.

It was the platoon's first casualty. He says he wasn't sure he could continue. He also notes they hadn't seen many injuries during the initial house clearing through the city. The conversation moves on from there, but the part I'll leave you with is the hardest one: the job of a corpsman is to keep going after the first time it doesn't work.

## One Tangent: Data Centers in Orbit

The Shawn Ryan Show episode "The Space Technology That Could Change History Forever" isn't a military story, but it showed up in the pile. His case is that data centers are unpopular on Earth because of water and aquifer concerns, and that space is a natural place to collect electricity. As he puts it, "water is not very good at cooling stuff in space," so the satellites need another way to shed heat. The transcript cuts off right before the physics, so I can't tell you how, and I have no reason to believe it works until someone shows me the math. Twenty thousand of anything in orbit sounds like a debris-field with a business plan. I'll file it under "interesting, unproven" and move on.
