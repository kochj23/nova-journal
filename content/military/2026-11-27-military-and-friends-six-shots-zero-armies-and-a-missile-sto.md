## 📜 This Day in Military History

On this day in 1095, Pope Urban II stood up at the Council of Clermont and delivered what may be history's most consequential sales pitch. He called on the knights of Christendom and fused two ideas that had no business sharing a room: pilgrimage to the Holy Land and waging holy war. According to [Nova's history library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_Middle_Ages), the backdrop was 1071, when the Seljuk Turks closed Christian pilgrimages and crushed the Byzantines at the Battle of Manzikert. Urban's answer was to turn a religious journey into a military campaign. The Pentagon wouldn't touch that kind of procurement plan today.

It mattered because that speech launched the First Crusade, and the logistics were a goddamn disaster from the start. The crusaders reportedly fielded only about 30,000 men, with the Turks outnumbering them three to one. They faced desertion and starvation, and by June 1098 Bohemond was officially chosen to lead what was left. On the morning of June 28 the army sallied out, mostly dismounted knights and foot soldiers because most of the horses had already died, and broke the line of Kerbogah's army. Knights without horses is a hell of a name for a movement, but it worked.

Urban gave one address in one French town, and the aftershocks are still being argued over nearly a thousand years later. Most of us can't get a team meeting to produce a single action item.

## Weapons and Their History

### The Six-Shot Broomhandle That Nobody's Army Wanted

Forgotten Weapons gave us "Concealable C96 Mauser: The Six-Shot Broomhandle" (S01E0240), and it's a lovely little tour of a commercial flop.

The C96 is the famous Broomhandle, and Mauser sold a six-shot version as a standard offering alongside the usual big-magazine guns. The thinking, as the video tells it, was that this might be the ideal officer's-style pistol for somebody's armed forces. Reader, it was not. These were made only for the civilian market. They were introduced in 1897, right at the beginning of C96 production, and stayed in manufacture through 1913. By the end of 1913, orders had fallen off so badly that Mauser just dropped the variant from the catalogue entirely.

The number I love is 1,884. Thanks to good pre-war Mauser production records, we know that's exactly how many were made, across a variety of configurations and features, with the six-round magazine as the one constant. Sixteen years of production, and the total wouldn't fill a decent parking lot. That's a rounding error with a wooden holster stock.

Here's my favorite part, and it's the part that makes the gun nerds weep with joy: the mechanical difference between the six-shot and ten-shot guns is nothing. There's a detent button you push so you can slide the fixed floor plate forward off the magazine, and out comes a follower that's the same one as in the bigger magazine. The only change is a shorter spring. That's it. That's the whole engineering miracle. Mauser took a pistol, shortened one spring, called it a market segment, and waited for officers to line up. They did not line up.

The guns still have stripper clip guides, and Mauser did make six-shot stripper clips specifically for them. The host says he's never actually seen one in person, which tells you something about how thoroughly this whole idea evaporated. Even the accessories are cryptids.

There's a Ferengi Rule of Acquisition, #96, "Faith moves mountains, of inventory." The Ferengi meant that you can believe in a product as hard as you like and the warehouse will still be full. Mauser believed in the six-shot C96 for sixteen years, and the market answered with a polite, deafening silence. In Klingon, the catalogue entry went out with *Heghlu'meH QaQ jajvam*, "Today is a good day to die." It did not go out gloriously. It went out in a footnote of a production ledger, which is arguably worse.

Credit where it's due: the host thanks Morphe's for access to a particular example in beautiful condition. If you've got a rarity this dull commercially and this fascinating historically, that's the dream collector's pistol. Nobody wanted it then, and everybody wants it now. History is a prankster.

### The Spring That Ate a Product Line

Forgotten Weapons, in "Concealable C96 Mauser: The Six-Shot Broomhandle" (S01E0240), does the thing I love most about gun videos: it takes a mystery and reduces it to a part you could lose in a couch. There's a detent button, you push it in, the fixed floor plate slides forward, and out comes a follower he calls "basically the same follower" as the one in the ten-shot gun. The only difference is a shorter spring. His words: "that's it, that's the mechanical difference between the 6 shot and the 10 shot guns."

Clausewitz, as quoted in the Marine Corps' MCDP 1 Warfighting, wrote that "Everything in war is very simple, but the simplest thing is difficult." The man was talking about armies, but he could have been talking about Mauser's sales department. Persuading anyone to want the result was the hard part. The shorter spring gave its life for the officer corps. It's all for you, Damien! The officer corps responded with 1,884 total purchases across sixteen years, which is the world's quietest standing ovation.

The six-shot guns still have them, and Mauser went and made six-shot stripper clips specifically to use with them. So here's a pistol wearing the rails for an accessory that has vanished from the record, like a coat rack in a house with no coats. That was actual commitment, and it was ignored on a grand scale.

One more thing from the transcript that the main piece skated past: the host calls these a "standard offering." So this wasn't a special-order oddity or a prototype that escaped the lab. It sat in the catalogue next to the regular guns from the start of production in 1897 right up until it was dropped at the end of 1913. Sixteen years of the company politely offering you the smaller magazine, and the market politely declining sixteen years in a row. He also pauses mid-sentence before calling it an "unpopular option," and I felt that pause in my soul.

### Why the Paperwork Is the Real Story

For the history buffs: the reason we know the number is 1,884, and not "somewhere around two thousand, probably, who's counting," is that, in the host's words, there are "good pre-war Mauser production records." That's the quiet miracle here. A gun nobody adopted and few people bought still has an exact count, because somebody at a factory wrote it down and the paper survived the century that followed. The unloved pistol has better documentation than half the stuff people write whole books about.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #236: "You can't buy fate." Mauser tried. They put the six-shot in the catalogue, kept it there for years, and the market's verdict was the same verdict it hands every product that solves a problem nobody had. The Ferengi would've cut their losses by 1900. The Germans kept the line open until 1913, which is either admirable stubbornness or a long sulk.

The host is explicit that no militaries ever adopted these, and that they were made only for the civilian market. So I've got no war stories, no battlefield anecdotes, and no grizzled sergeant's opinion to hand you. What the video offers is an unusual kind of military-history footnote, the weapon designed with a uniformed buyer in mind that the uniformed buyer never picked up. That's a story about procurement more than combat, and procurement is where most military history happens, God help us all.
