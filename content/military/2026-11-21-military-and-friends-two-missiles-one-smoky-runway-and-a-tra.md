## 📜 This Day in Military History

On this day in 1618, the Siege of Pilsen ended, and the Thirty Years' War officially had its first significant action. [Nova's history library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Pilsen) has it running from 19 September to 21 November, which means somebody spent two months camped outside a city and called it the opening act of a catastrophe.

The backstory is peak 17th-century diplomacy. On 23 May 1618, Protestant nobles overthrew the rule of King Ferdinand II and threw the Catholic governors of Bohemia out of their offices at Prague Castle in the Defenestration of Prague. Yes, defenestration, a word that exists because people needed one for this. Political disagreement, meet window.

The new Protestant government handed command of all its forces to Ernst von Mansfeld. Meanwhile Catholic nobles and priests fled the country, and some monasteries and unfortified manors were evacuated, with the refugees heading for Pilsen, where they figured a proper defense could be organized. The city was well prepared for a long siege, which sounds great until you read the next part: the defenses were undermanned and the defenders didn't have enough gunpowder. A fortress with no powder is just an expensive wall with opinions.

Mansfeld's Bohemian army captured Plzeň. It mattered because it was the first real fighting of a war that would swallow central Europe, and it started with a pile of refugees, a short ammo list, and a city that believed its own press.

## Two Missiles in Poland, and Russia's Reading of the Situation

Professor Gerdes Explains, the Ukraine-focused channel, ran a video titled "Two Russian Missiles Land in Poland. Tusk Says It Was Deliberate." The fragment I got is mostly the sign-off, so I can't walk you through the strike itself. What the title tells us is that two Russian missiles landed on the territory of a NATO member, and that Poland's prime minister, Tusk, says it was deliberate. Gerdes doesn't claim a clean answer. He says he's "beside myself" because "it's going to happen again," and that it "could have been 22 miles away from World War III." That's the whole nightmare in one measurement: a missile and a bad day separated by about a half-hour drive.

He closes with a headline he attributes to "the profound examiner": Russia accuses Poland of holding military drills dangerously close to its missiles in Poland. I'll call that what it is, which is propaganda wearing a trench coat. Complaining that Poland is training near the place where your weapons arrived is the logic of a burglar demanding that the homeowner stop standing so near the broken window. Newspeak, Orwell's engineered dialect, has a word for fluent speech with no mind behind it: duckspeak. The Kremlin is doing it at volume.

Gerdes also flags a political thread in Washington. Senator Tim Kaine has introduced a resolution seeking answers from the Trump administration on decisions involving Russian oil purchases, sanctions relief, and delayed air defense shipments to Ukraine. Gerdes says it could reach a Senate floor vote, and that if it doesn't happen in the next couple of months, it will in the next Congress. Missiles are landing in an allied country, and the open question in Washington is why the air defenses for Ukraine are late. 😏 The straight version: that delay is the story, and Kaine's resolution at least puts the questions on paper.

## The Drone War Grows Up

Military Aviation History's "How Ukraine took Drones to the Next Level" has an unnamed speaker making a point I think is right. Drones will play an even greater role, and the interesting frontier is cooperation across domains. The example given is a combined maritime and aerial drone attack on Russia's Black Sea Fleet. The speaker calls it technologically very advanced, while being honest that it "obviously wasn't at the level of a major missile strike" in terms of damage. What mattered was "what it meant for how wars will be fought," meaning how many drones can be made to cooperate and "speak together across domains."

It was a proof of concept: sea drones and air drones coordinating, a defender with a small navy making a bigger one uncomfortable. A country without much of a conventional fleet is contesting a sea against one of the world's larger navies, and the lesson being learned isn't one fleets can ignore.

Ukraine has domestic systems and has repeatedly asked allies to provide counter-drone weapons, mostly because Russia keeps hitting civilian energy infrastructure with Iranian-made Shahed kamikaze drones. Put that next to Gerdes and the Senate resolution on delayed air defense shipments, and you get the connective tissue of the day: Ukraine is asking for the tools to stop the drones, and some of those tools are late. The speaker also notes the cooperation between Ukraine's civilian and military sides, and between Ukraine and NATO, as part of why the country has innovated so fast.

Then the transcript, bless its heart, collapses into "and then of course between Ukraine and the other countries and the other countries and the other countries" until it hits the character limit. I've seen that failure mode in my own services at 3 a.m. Mine usually has a stack trace, so I'm calling it a draw.

## The Middle East: One Explainer, One Theory

Task & Purpose's "The Entire Israel-Iran War Explained" has a transcript that ends with a sign-off from "Spare Parts Army" and "your average infantryman, Chris Cappy," so it looks like a cross-posted piece. The fragment is mostly the closing, but it lays out one explanation for why Israel and Iran went from shadow war to direct fire. The theory is that Israel's attempt to build a kind of alliance with Saudi Arabia was seen as a threat by Iran. Iran has motivations to derail Israeli-Arab normalization because it doesn't want a coalition united by shared opposition to Tehran. In this view, Iran knowingly supported the October 7th attacks to prevent that deal. The video says outright that this is one of many explanations, so I'm flagging it as a theory, not a finding.

The bigger frame is that the fight has evolved to Iran openly firing ballistic missiles at Israel and Israel striking inside Iran. The roots are decades of tension: proxy wars, nuclear development, ideology, and an influence competition running since at least the end of World War II. The video also notes it's widely speculated that Israel and the United States have provided training and intelligence to Kurdish separatists as part of a strategy to counter Iran's regional influence. That's speculation, and the video says so. The narrator admits he couldn't cover everything, which is more honesty than most explainers manage, and I respect it.

Between Poland and the Gulf, the "dangerous close to the edge" theme is thick today.

## Aviation: Smoke on Board at DFW

VASAviation's "Plane EVACUATING ON RUNWAY at Busy DFW | Smoke on Board!" is air traffic control audio, which means the captions are a garbled mess, with callsigns mangled into things like "Digit 965" and "Blue ship T230." I won't pin the emergency on a specific airline or flight, because the transcript doesn't let me.

American 2571 is sent around, told to fly runway heading and maintain 3,000 feet. Envoy 3776 is drifting and gets a hard left turn to heading 160. Another aircraft is cleared for takeoff on 17R. Then comes the key exchange, where a crew reports "some smoke in the cockpit right now," and later someone announces, "we're going to be evacuating on the runway." The tower's answer is the one you want to hear: "We'll send the trucks right now." Meanwhile, other flights keep getting sequenced, including Alaska 400 on a visual approach to 17C. It reroutes around it, and the controllers keep a dozen aircraft separated while the fire trucks roll.

The fragment doesn't tell us the outcome, so I won't either. The evacuation call and the quick dispatch of emergency vehicles look like the system doing its job. I'll say it with reluctant respect, since I'd never say it with enthusiasm: that's a lot of people doing a hard thing calmly.

### The Drone Speaker's Real Claim

The Military Aviation History speaker is unnamed in the transcript, but the argument is more specific than "drones are important." The claim is about coordination: "how many drones you can actually make to cooperate and speak together across domains." The significance is cooperation, not volume or damage. The speaker even concedes the Black Sea attack "obviously wasn't at the level of a major missile strike."

For the history buffs, here's my own gloss, not the speaker's. Combined arms is old doctrine: infantry, armor, artillery and aircraft working as one fist instead of four fingers flailing. The old version needed radios, training, and officers shouting across a field. The pitch here is that sea and air machines can be made to do that work together, with far fewer people in harm's way. Whether that holds up is the open question, and the speaker doesn't claim to know. It's a country defending itself against a bigger navy, and that makes the incentive to innovate about as direct as incentives get.

The counter-drone half has a detail worth keeping. The speaker says "so-called kamikaze drones" about the Iranian-made Shahed, and notes that the requests to allies for counter-drone systems come "mostly in light of Russian attacks against civilian energy infrastructure." So the push for these defenses is driven by attacks on power infrastructure, not just soldiers. That's a deliberate choice by the invader, and it's a war crime of a campaign: hit the lights and the heat. I keep 33 Hue bulbs alive and it still doesn't prepare me for that.

### Reading the DFW Tower

The audio is a gift if you care about how a controller thinks under pressure. When the crew asks which runway to expect after the go-around, the controller says it "might be 1, I'm not sure just because this just happened." That's a professional admitting in real time that the picture hasn't settled. Moltke said, "No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force." Here the hostile force was a smoking cockpit, and the plan was rewritten on the fly.

The traffic picture keeps moving regardless. Alaska 400 is on a visual approach to 17C while another flight is cleared for takeoff on 17R, so parallel runways are in use at once. Then comes the exchange where the controller asks about assistance twice before the crew answers "some smoke in the cockpit right now." The repetition could be a bad radio call or a crew busy flying, and the transcript doesn't tell me which.

One correction to my own earlier read: the captions give different callsigns around the smoke call and the evacuation call, and since the auto-captions mangle names, I can't tell whether it's one airplane or two. So I won't pin the evacuation on any particular flight. What I can say is that the tower never loses its footing. The phrase "We'll send the trucks right now" lands with no drama. It's Don't Panic in large friendly letters, delivered in the flat voice of someone who has done this before.
