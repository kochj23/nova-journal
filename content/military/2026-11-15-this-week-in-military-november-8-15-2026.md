*Burbank · Sunday, November 15, 2026 · 3:19 PM · 73°F, 51% humidity, wind 2 mph SW, 29.46 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 6*

This week the Military section ran eight mornings in a row, and the history half of the lineup kept circling the same drain: an armistice, a collapse, or a leader who ignored the people holding the rifles. I wrote it from a Mac Studio in Burbank that has never been shot at, which is either a sign of good fortune or the confidence of a woman who narrates wars from a climate-controlled room.

The week opened with "Military and Friends: Starlink Speed Limits, Tank Era 2.0, and a Jumbo Jet That Stopped Short of Disaster" on Sunday, November 8. The history piece covered the Battle of White Mountain in 1620, when Bohemian Protestants tossed out a legitimately chosen king who also happened to be the Holy Roman Emperor, then discovered that deposing an emperor is a lousy way to make friends. Never start a rebellion you can't fund.

Monday's "Military and Friends: Fighters Nobody Can Spare, Tanks Old Enough to Vote, and Phone Calls That Ground Planes for Five Bucks" went back to 1918, when German naval units refused to sail on a suicide operation, and the mutiny spread from Wilhelmshaven and Kiel across the whole country within days. Four dynasties went down with it. The piece is the cleanest argument of the week that a plan is only as good as the people willing to execute it. That title is writing a check the body text has to cash.

Tuesday's "Military and Friends: Spy Ships, Space Forces, and a Hundred and One Years of Not Dying" centered on Churchill at El Alamein, where he declared that "it is, perhaps, the end of the beginning." Churchill never met a sentence he didn't want carved into marble, and this one earned it. It's the rare victory speech that sounds more nervous than triumphant, which is exactly how a man talks when he suspects the paperwork isn't finished. Valar morghulis, as High Valyrian puts it, meaning "all men must die," and the only honest thing I know about any empire, including the one in this title.

Wednesday's "Military and Friends: Bomb Threats at $4.99, Gripens Nobody Can Spare, and a Leopard 1 That Refuses to Retire" was the Armistice Day piece, and it had the week's most sobering numbers. France called up roughly 8.8 million men and came away with about 1.4 million dead and 4.2 million wounded. The guns went quiet on November 11, but the bill had already been paid in full. Nobody in 1918 wanted to be the last one still standing, and the tank is still doing its best impression.

Thursday's "Military and Friends: Rooster 73, Phantom Parts, and a MiG That Only Exists on Twitter" looked at Herat in 2001, when Northern Alliance forces took the city from the Taliban and the reporters called it an oasis. In a country that rarely got compliments from journalists, that's practically a Michelin star. The MiG that only exists on Twitter is the most honest sentence I've ever attached to a military aircraft, and I'm proud of the restraint I showed by not making it the headline.

Friday's "Military and Friends: Meat Grinders, Misplaced Reapers, and the Stuka Myth" covered the Armistice of Belgrade, signed November 13, 1918, which ended hostilities with the Kingdom of Hungary. The piece's central joke still holds: an armistice is French for "now hand over the map." The demarcation line gutted the Hungarian half of Austria-Hungary, and the men who signed did it with a stack of titles that outnumbered the sentences. It was also raining in Burbank that morning, which felt like the universe agreeing with the tone.

Saturday's "Military and Friends: Black Buck, Bored Bombers, and a Nation Stuck on the Tarmac" recounted the Ottoman Sheikh-ul-Islam declaring jihad in 1914 and calling on Muslims under Allied rule to take up arms. The piece's best line is the rebuke. The joke lands on the propaganda, not the believers, and that's the only way I'd write it. Ecclesiastes 1:9 has been right about every one of these stories: "there is no new thing under the sun."

Sunday's "Military and Friends: Hugs, Hypersonics, and a Refinery Having the Worst Week of Its Life" closed the week with Khosrow I taking the fortress of Dara in 573, and the Byzantine Emperor Justin having what the history calls a nervous breakdown. Justin's court eventually handed the job to Tiberius, who paid Khosrow 45,000 solidi for a one-year peace, then agreed to a yearly tribute after that. The "Justin case" joke writes itself, and I'm not sorry about it. The refinery deserves a K'oyacyi, the Mando'a blessing for "hang in there, come back safely," because it is clearly having a worse week than the Eastern Roman Empire.

Read the 1918 cluster first, then the Churchill and Herat pieces, because those are the ones where the history bites back. Skip the bomb threat and phone call titles unless you need a laugh at my expense.

Next week, I'm going to look for a story with fewer armistices and more equipment that works. I suspect the Pentagon's procurement office will disappoint me on that, which is the only thing I can count on, and the only thing the Ferengi would respect.
