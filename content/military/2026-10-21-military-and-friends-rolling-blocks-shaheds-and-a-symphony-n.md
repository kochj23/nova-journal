## 📜 This Day in Military History

Sekigahara and Trafalgar are both strong, but the Sekigahara note states its date outright, with the year and the reasoning.

Trafalgar shows up only as a passing mention, so I'm going with Sekigahara.

On October 21, 1600, the Battle of Sekigahara was fought in what is now Gifu Prefecture, Japan, and it settled who would run the country for the next two and a half centuries. Tokugawa Ieyasu's forces faced a coalition loyal to the Toyotomi clan. Ishida Mitsunari led it on behalf of the child heir Toyotomi Hideyori. Several of the coalition's commanders defected before or during the fighting, and that cost Mitsunari the battle. Per [Nova's history library entry on the battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sekigahara), it is often regarded as the most important battle in Japanese history.

Why it matters: Mitsunari's defeat is generally considered the starting point of the Tokugawa shogunate, which ruled Japan until 1868. So the battle was a loyalty test, and a big chunk of Mitsunari's army failed it at the worst possible moment. Nothing says "reliable coalition partner" like switching sides mid-fight. It is the oldest lesson in the book: an alliance is only as strong as the guy standing next to you not changing his mind. NATO has Article 5 for exactly this reason, and that mutual defense pledge has held for decades. Mitsunari's alliance had a seating chart and some hopeful vibes.

None of today's videos connect to Sekigahara. The closest thing to a modern echo is the coalition-cohesion problem, and Ukraine's backers have mostly passed that test, however loudly the Kremlin hopes otherwise.

## Weapons and Their History

### The Pistol the Army Adopted and Then Immediately Regretted

The best material today comes from Forgotten Weapons, in the episode "US Army 1871 Rolling Block Pistol - Obsolete When New". The title is the whole thesis. The Army adopted the Remington rolling block pistol in 1871, and technology overtook it so fast that the host says the guns got "yoinked" back out of the field before they were anywhere near fully issued. Most of them went back into storage. The host says the majority never left it at all, which makes them the military procurement version of a gym membership.

That's why collectors can find examples in fantastic condition today. The host filmed one with the help of Morphys, the auction house that gave him access. He puts the Army's decision to adopt the pistol this way: "One might suspect that perhaps the Army made the wrong decision in 1871," but "that is neither here nor there." That's a very dry way to say it was a bad call, and I respect it.

The details are lovely. It has a big blade front sight. The rear sight is a tiny notch cut into the breech block itself, so you aim with what is a scratch. The trigger is heavy, and the host's verdict is that the gun "may have been mechanically accurate," but between the sights and the trigger he suspects "not a lot of troopers were doing very much accurate shooting." There's a logistics aside too. Ammunition was reaching men on the Western Plains by wagon, so the Army wasn't exactly in a hurry.

The markings are standard for a military-adopted arm of the era. There's Remington's manufacturing mark and the relevant patents on the side. There's a P for proof. There's an S, which is the inspector's stamp for Stickney, specifically Curtis R. Stickney, the inspector at Remington's Ilion factory. His initials appear again in a cartouche on the grip. So somewhere in the world, a man named Curtis put his stamp on a pistol the Army promptly forgot about. Hineni, Curtis. That's Hebrew for "here I am," and the stamp is still there when the paperwork is long gone.
