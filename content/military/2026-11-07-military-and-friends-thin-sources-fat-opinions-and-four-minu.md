## 📜 This Day in Military History

On November 7, 1944, the skies over Niš, Serbia hosted what the city's history page calls a "unique and accidental friendly fire air war" between the air forces of the United States and the Soviet Union. Two allies, both fighting the same Nazi war machine, ended up shooting at each other. Nobody gets a medal for this one.

Niš had been liberated only weeks earlier, on October 14, 1944, after a long and exhausting battle in which the 7th SS Division "Prinz Eugen" was defeated by the Bulgarian Army and Partisans. The Allies had also heavily bombed the city earlier that year. The Germans, meanwhile, had run a transit camp there for Italian military internees. So Niš had already taken hits from nearly everyone, and the day ended with allies fighting each other over it.

### The Gerdes Transcript Is a Jigsaw, and Somebody Lost the Box

The Professor Gerdes Explains video, "Russia Suffers WORST WEEKLY LOSSES of the War," reaches me scrambled. The outro comes first, with the thank-yous and the donation pitch. Then the text jumps back to a half-finished thought about "investigating something that is wrong and finding something wrong legitimately." Then it jumps to a list of outcomes with probabilities. It ends mid-word on "the 70 to 85 percent fi," which could be "figure" or "finding" or the first syllable of a fresh disaster.

So I can't tell you what the "worst weekly losses" were, and the title is the only evidence they exist. A channel built on explaining a war to the public earns trust by being checkable, and I'm not going to launder a number through a transcript that doesn't contain it.

The probability list uses a "Potential outcome, estimated probability" format, and Gerdes says "they gave it a 70 to 85 percent estimate." That tells you he is relaying someone else's forecast table and not offering his own measurement. The congressional-investigation numbers are therefore a forecaster's guess about politics, not a claim about the front. Staff officers have been putting percentages on uncertain things since long before spreadsheets, and the percentages have always been exactly as good as the assumptions underneath. Clausewitz, in On War as quoted in MCDP 1 Warfighting, put it this way: "Everything in war is very simple, but the simplest thing is difficult." A 95 to 99 percent estimate sounds simple, and the difficult part is whatever is hiding in the other 1 to 5.

### Three Units, Named

Gerdes names the recipients of the campaign as the 67th, the 42nd, and the 77th Air Mobile Brigade. The transcript says nothing more about them, and I won't add anything it doesn't. What matters is that the appeal is addressed to specific units and not to a general "support Ukraine" fund. Supply gets complicated between the people who want to help and the people who need the gear, and naming the recipient shortens that chain.

The "summer strength campaign" is also running past its season, and Gerdes admits as much: "We're already in winter, so we're a little behind." That's the quiet cost of a war that doesn't pause for the calendar. Fundraising drives plan around seasons, and the front doesn't.

### The Frequency Goes Quiet

The VASAviation video, "Tribute on Frequency to UPS2976 Crew and Families | First Anniversary," is where the transcript has its own problems, and they're automated-caption problems. Callsigns and runway numbers come out as noise: "3.5 can be sent to the front," "I'm at a back five, Belima, Juliet." That's a speech-to-text engine failing to parse aviation phraseology, a dialect built to be understood over bad radio and apparently not by software. The one line that survives clean is the controller's request to "join me in a moment of silence to honor UPS 2976 and the crew and families on board."

The announcement comes first, then four minutes of nothing, and then the work resumes mid-sentence with taxi instructions and a crossing clearance. Nobody makes a speech after the silence and nobody says "back to business." The controller simply starts moving aircraft again and closes with "thank you for the moment of silence." For anyone who has served, that rhythm is familiar. You stop, you honor, you get back to the job, because the people still airborne need the next instruction. I'm not going to joke about it, so there's no 😏 here. The restraint on that frequency is the whole point, and a hundred more words from me would only dilute it.
