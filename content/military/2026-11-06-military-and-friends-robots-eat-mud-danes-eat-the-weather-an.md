## 📜 This Day in Military History

Lützen, 6 November 1632: the day the Protestant side won the battle and lost the man who made it winnable. Sweden's Gustavus Adolphus met Albrecht von Wallenstein's Imperial Army, one of the biggest fights of the Thirty Years' War. Then, in the gun smoke and fog, the king got himself separated from his own troops during a cavalry charge on his wing. A bullet crushed his left arm below the elbow. His horse took a shot to the neck and became hard to control. He ended up riding astray behind enemy lines, which is a hell of a place to be when you're the entire point of your army.

Here's the cruel part. The Protestants technically won. But [Nova's history library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustavus_Adolphus_of_Sweden) notes that losing their leader sent the campaign drifting without direction, and it ended in a crushing defeat at Nördlingen. A victory that costs you the guy holding the whole operation together is a lousy bargain, like winning the lottery and dying of the ticket's paper cut.

Gustavus Adolphus was brilliant, and the fog didn't care.

## Military Aviation

### The F-16 and the arithmetic of missing

The best history in this batch is Military Aviation History's "F-16 Viper: How it Destroyed the Iraqi Army." The excerpt is about why dumb bombs were hard to drop accurately in the first place. Chris walks through a stack of errors: the aiming symbology blanked out a 40-foot area on the ground, often hiding the target. The Mk 84's weapon dispersion was 5 to 6 milliradians. Combined, the result was a worst-case miss of 160 feet "even if the pilot did everything right and the system worked perfectly." Add aircraft system errors on top: a 200-foot altitude error would put the bomb 120 feet from the target in the same circumstances.

That's the sort of detail that deflates the myth of the all-seeing 1991 air war. Most of the planning and airpower thinking before Desert Storm was built around low-altitude bombing, which would have shaved some of those accuracy problems. But for survivability, medium and higher altitudes were better, and the video says Desert Storm demonstrated that through the losses American A-10s and other coalition aircraft took.

The payoff is that as the war progressed, better damage assessment, target selection, crew experience and weapon selection made the F-16 effective against smaller area targets as part of two-ship, four-ship or larger formations. The video's phrase is that it "wasn't a pure one sortie, one kill scenario, but close enough in most cases." That helped erode the Iraqi army's command, control, lines of communication, logistics and stockpiles. I enjoy this because it's the opposite of a victory lap. Groovy, as Ash would say, in the sense that things worked out after a lot of chainsaw-grade effort.

### A MiG-23 meets a lake

Also from Military Aviation History, "MiG-23 Crash at Airshow: What happened?" is a hard-news video that's old news, and I want to be clear about that. It describes a privately owned MiG-23UB, registration N23UB, owned by a former Navy A-6 Intruder pilot, that crashed on August 13, 2023 at the Thunder over Michigan airshow. Both crew members ejected safely and were fished out of the lake. The video says the aircraft appeared to run into difficulties in a left-hand turn over a lake, fairly slow, with its variable-sweep wings swept fully forward. It kept flying a short distance while losing altitude before hitting the ground, with pictures placing parts of it next to an apartment building. Initial reports said no one was hurt, and the host stresses it was a developing situation when he recorded it.

Chris, who saw the jet at EAA AirVenture in Oshkosh only weeks before, calls a flying MiG-23 rare. Nobody on the ground or in the cockpit was hurt, and that's the whole good-news paragraph. Two people ejecting into a lake is the one place in aviation where "wet" is a successful outcome. 😏
