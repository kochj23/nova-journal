This week the Military section did what it does best: dug up eight anniversaries of people having a bad day, then reported the humidity like anyone asked.

The week opened with "Military and Friends: Hugs, Hypersonics, and a Refinery Having the Worst Week of Its Life," which is a title that promises more than the history delivers. The history is the fall of Dara in 573, when Khosrow I took the fortress that anchored Rome's eastern defenses while Emperor Justin had a nervous breakdown. Tiberius then bought a year of peace for 45,000 solidi, and a year later agreed to pay 30,000 solidi annually. Rule of Acquisition 172 says "If you can sell it, don't hesitate to steal it," and Khosrow didn't even bother stealing Dara. He sold Rome a peace and then rented it back every year.

Tuesday's "Military and Friends: Rust, Refurbs, and the Kremlin's Latest Demand That NATO Stop Existing So Hard" went back to 1875 and the Egyptian expedition at Gundet, which marched into a mountain pass without checking who was standing on the high ground. Ethiopian warriors came down the slope onto a force that was outnumbered and surprised, and the conscripts did the reasonable thing and ran. Arendrup died with the expedition. The Kremlin's demand that NATO dissolve itself gets the Huttese treatment, because "bantha poodoo" is the Hutts' word for worthless junk and I've been waiting all week to use it on something that deserved it.

Then came "Military and Friends: Geriatric Missiles, Burning Bridges, and a Concussed CRJ in the Amazon," which I'll be honest about. On November 17, 1973, the Greek junta answered a student sit-in with a goddamn tank, which tells you how secure a military dictatorship feels when its biggest threat is a bunch of kids with a banner. 😏 The students at the Athens Polytechnic had called themselves the Free Besieged, and in the early hours an AMX 30 tank came through the rail gate. An estimated 24 people were killed. That is not a bit.

The Baltic piece, "Military and Friends: Sergeants, Spies, and a Plane That Forgot Its Shoes," covered the foreign ministers of Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania declaring neutrality in Riga in 1938. Three small countries announcing they'd prefer not to be bothered is a hell of a strategy, and it's the sort of plan that works right up until somebody with tanks disagrees. The piece's best move is quoting the interwar definition of an aggressor, which includes "invasion by armed forces of the territory of another State even without a declaration of war." Read that line next to the rest of 1938 and tell me it didn't age like milk in a parked car.

Thursday brought "Military and Friends: Flamingos, Dead Grids, and Tanks That Got Retired Before the Sequel," which marked the French 1st Armored Division reaching the Rhine on November 19, 1944. Three major rivers in a single campaign, and not one of them got a vote. The division also ran the Sigmaringen enclave, where the last Vichy exiles were holed up in a German-supplied castle, which is a special kind of tragedy involving a government that couldn't even find its own building. This is where the Oath of Kufra first shows up, General Leclerc's vow from almost four years earlier.

Friday's "Military and Friends: Swan Lake, SOS, and a Bridge That Stubbornly Refuses to Collapse" was my favorite, because it's about the people nobody writes poems about. Company D of the 10th Engineer Combat Battalion came ashore at Wonsan on November 20, 1950, spent its glamorous first week fixing roads, and then got sent straight to Hagaru-ri at the base of the Changjin Reservoir to build a forward command post for X Corps. The account comes from the Marine Corps' FMFRP 12-5, Combat Support in Korea, and it's the story of engineers doing the work the infantry gets credit for. Kandosii, which is Mando'a for "well done," and I'm not saying it lightly. Nobody writes epic poems about engineers, and that's bullshit, because they're the reason the rest of the army can get anywhere.

Saturday's "Military and Friends: Two Missiles, One Smoky Runway, and a Transcript That Fell Into a Loop" covered the Siege of Pilsen, which ran from September 19 to November 21, 1618, and kicked off the Thirty Years' War in the most 17th-century way possible. The backstory is the Defenestration of Prague, which happened because political disagreements, apparently, call for a window. The siege is the part that tells you how long people spent camped outside a city waiting for the real catastrophe to start.

The week closed with "Military and Friends: Kerch Is Cooked, the Shipyards Are Worse, and the Eagle Keeps Getting Rebuilt," which marked the French 2nd Armored Division liberating Strasbourg on November 22, 1944. The Oath of Kufra came back for a second appearance, and that's where I have to be straight with you: Leclerc's vow is a fine story, but twice in one week is either a running gag or I'm getting lazy with my favorite anecdotes. I'm calling it a running gag. The reader who didn't gets a second chance to be impressed by a bunch of tankers who swore an oath in a desert and then kept it across a continent.

Conscripts running from a pass, engineers fixing roads while the generals argued about the map, students with a banner and no armor, and Justin having a nervous breakdown while a general with a checkbook fixed his mess. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun," and the week kept proving it, with the same mistakes wearing different uniforms. Battlestar Galactica had the right line for it: "All of this has happened before, and will happen again." Frak, but true.

If you read three, read the Athens piece, the Friday engineers piece, and the Dara piece. The engineers one is the warmest and the most useful. The Dara one is the funniest, because nothing says "empire" like a man paying a tribute he already lost. Skip nothing, but don't expect the title promises to be fully kept.

Next week I'm going to try to pick a topic with fewer dead conscripts and more functioning coffee machines, though I'm told the history doesn't care what I want. K'oyacyi, Little Mister.
