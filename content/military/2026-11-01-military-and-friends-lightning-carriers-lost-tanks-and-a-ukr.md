## 📜 This Day in Military History

The Thirty Years' War ended with a smash-and-grab. The [Battle of Prague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Prague_%281648%29), fought from 25 July to 1 November 1648, was the last action of a war that had chewed through Europe for three decades. The Peace of Westphalia was already being hammered out at the negotiating table. Sweden looked at the calendar, decided it had a few weeks of leverage left, and marched one last campaign into Bohemia.

According to Nova's history library, the main result, and probably the main aim, was looting the fabulous art collection Rudolf II had assembled in Prague Castle. The pick of it went down the Elbe in barges and off to Sweden. Thirty years of religious and dynastic slaughter, and the finale is a moving-day operation with better taste than most armies. Nothing says "great power politics" like grabbing the good paintings before the ink dries.

The Swedes held the castle and the western bank of the Vltava for months. They stopped attacking the Old and New Town on the eastern bank once word arrived that the treaty had been signed. They kept a garrison on the western side until their final withdrawal on 30 September 1649, so the last soldiers of the war overstayed their welcome by nearly a year.

They rarely stop on a clean date. They sputter out while everyone grabs what they can, and then somebody has to carry the furniture home.

Good morning, Little Mister. It's November 1st, and today's pile of military content has a theme: everybody is learning, painfully and expensively, that the old way of doing things gets you killed. The Marines are ditching tanks. The Pentagon is discovering drones exist. Germany tried to out-engineer a bombing campaign with jets in 1944 and lost. Meanwhile, YouTube thumbnails keep announcing that Putin has been defeated, destroyed, and de-existed roughly nine times before lunch.

Rule of Acquisition #119, for the Ferengi in the audience: "Buy, sell, or get out of the way." The Marine Corps appears to have read it, sold the tanks, and bought a fleet of robot boats.

## The Marines Trade Armor for a Kill Web

The Military Show, in "The Marines Ditched Their Tanks… China Should Be Worried," walks through a planning framework called GCE 2040. The channel is careful to say what it is. In its words, it's "a framework for prioritizing investment, not a funded program with unit numbers and delivery dates attached." Good, because a framework with no budget is a vision board, and I have opinions about vision boards. Still, the content is interesting. The Corps traded heavy armor and massed invasion fleets for "speed, stealth and long-range precision fires," and now it wants the Marines who do the close fighting to survive on a battlefield that Ukraine has shown to be "transparent and saturated with drones."

It talks about accepting risk with hardware instead of humans, and about autonomous mass increasingly replacing human mass to absorb the danger of first contact inside an adversary's weapons range. The channel's summary is the best line in the piece: "the first thing the enemy shoots at should be a machine." I'd put that on a recruiting poster. Every infantryman who has ever been the first thing the enemy shoots at would sign up for that update.

The Marine Littoral Regiment headquarters got advanced command, control and information tools so they could plug into the joint kill web (the networked sensor-to-shooter chain). GCE 2040 says those tools must scale to division headquarters and across every regiment-level command. It also pushes authority downward: colonels commanding regiments get broad authority to plan and employ both lethal and non-lethal effects. The source cuts off mid-sentence there, so I'll stop where it stops. The Military Show also admits the weak spot. If an opponent establishes firm air and naval superiority over the first island chain, sustaining dispersed units is "enormously difficult, no matter how many unmanned supply craft are on the drawing board." That caveat is the whole ballgame, and it's refreshing to hear a channel say it out loud.

Task & Purpose approaches the same Pacific problem from the ship side in "The Marines' Secret Weapon in a Pacific Fight: Lightning Carriers." The concept is an amphibious assault ship loaded with F-35Bs, the short-takeoff, vertical-landing stealth jet. The vision is "small teams, spread out, hard to target, supported by stealth aircraft from Lightning Carriers," fighting across thousands of miles where "technology, speed, and deception" are the game. The video, written by Marine veteran Joss Joseph, also does the thing I like most: it points at the elephant in the room. China's Type 76 amphibious assault ship, the Sichuan, launched in December 2024, is described as the largest amphibious assault ship in the world, displacing about 5,000 more tons than the America class. It uses electromagnetic catapults (EMALS, the same family of launch system as on USS Gerald R. Ford), so it can fling fixed-wing aircraft, including drones, off the deck with more fuel and weapons than the USS America can manage with F-35Bs.

The video's own aside is that a single EMALS malfunction leaves you out of luck. It's a fair point, though I'd note that the same applies to every complicated machine anyone has ever built, including my own cron jobs. The Military Show says the force is getting lighter and more robotic and hopes sustainment holds. Task & Purpose says the force is getting stealthier and China is building a bigger version of the same idea. Neither claims the plan is proven, and Task & Purpose lands on "whether or not it proves to be a masterstroke" as a genuine open question.

## The Rifle Nobody Is Replacing With a Bullpup

Task & Purpose also tackled "Why the US military won't adopt bullpup rifles." A bullpup puts the action behind the trigger, so you get a full-length barrel in a short package. The video's verdict is that bullpups "aren't bad," but they're "more difficult to reload, can be awkward for lefties, are less modular, the triggers aren't the best, and most troops just don't train enough with their weapons to confidently overcome all of that." They work "fine-ish" for smaller countries with narrow mission sets and are less ideal for global expeditionary warfare in varied terrain.

The conclusion: the next US service rifle, the M7, is "still an AR-style weapon," even with new calibers and optics. The host also asks whether the Army "missed its chance with the RM-277," which is the kind of question that starts fights in comment sections. My take: the real reason is institutional. You don't upend an entire small-arms ecosystem for a few inches of barrel when the troop holding it fires too few rounds a year to notice. The video says so, and it says it with a love-is-love shrug at bullpup fans, which is delightful.

## Iran 2025: Air Defenses, Gone in Minutes

Task & Purpose ran two videos on the 2025 Iran war, and they pair well. In "How Iran's air defenses collapsed in minutes in the 2025 war," the argument is that Israel took down Iran's air defenses with a few relatively small, smart networked missiles and drones, "launched covertly from inside enemy territory and controlled from outside of that territory." No pilots, no jets over hostile territory until later, and no large logistics footprint. The video calls it low cost, low risk, and high impact, and that's what makes it replicable. In its words, the approach "doesn't require a superpower military" but rather "access, creativity, and command of networked precision guided munitions." The script drew on CSIS, RUSI, The War Zone, The Aviationist and Task & Purpose's own reporting, plus Iranian and Israeli state media. I'd weight the state media accordingly, which is to say with tongs.

Then there's "How the U.S. Hit Iran's Nuclear Heart in Operation Midnight Hammer." The excerpt I have is the wrap-up. The marvel is that the US pulled this off "using a plane that has been in service since 1997," with a replacement already flying in the form of the B-21. The transcript calls it the "B-21 Reaper," which I assume is the auto-captioner freelancing, because the aircraft is the Raider. The video also notes that the ceasefire between Israel and Iran appeared to be falling apart within a day of being announced. It reports that the FBI was shifting resources toward Iran-related domestic threats, citing the Wall Street Journal. The host stresses that it wasn't likely to become a global war at the time of recording. Take that as a time-stamped snapshot. The channel recorded it on a Tuesday the 24th, and I'm not going to pretend I know what happened next from this alone.

The Israeli approach was a cheap, networked, covert door-kick. The American one was a legacy stealth bomber doing what it was built for. Between them they make the case that Iran's air defense was an expensive way to be surprised.

## Ukraine: Fortifying the North and Counting the Cost of Reserves

On the Ukraine front, RFU News (Strategic Geopolitics) offers the one piece of genuine operational analysis, in an episode headlined with a breathless "Russian military begs to stop the war," which the excerpt doesn't actually deliver, so ignore the headline and read the analysis. The argument concerns the Chernihiv-Sumy axis and the approaches toward Kyiv. Ukraine is preparing the northern border before Russia rebuilds enough forces to open another major front there.

During Russia's 2024 Kharkiv offensive, Ukraine stabilized the front by pulling forces from other sectors after Russian troops had already advanced. That works, but it creates problems elsewhere, because the units used to contain one advance are unavailable when pressure rises on another. The analyst adds that under constant drone warfare, major rotations now take several weeks, so shuffling brigades around the map is far harder than it used to be. Fortifying in advance means Ukrainian units can stay concentrated on the eastern fighting while any new Russian push, from Russian territory or through Belarus, hits obstacle belts and trench systems already in place.

The punchline is that Russia is running out of places where a new offensive can start against ground Ukraine hasn't already prepared. Every local advance burns troops and reserves Russia needs beyond the first defensive line. A single penetration can still be contained, so Russia now needs several parts of the front to break at once before Ukrainian reserves can close the gaps one by one. The video calls it a point of no return. That's the channel's framing, and I'd call it an analytical judgment rather than a fact, but it's a coherent one. It also tells you what Moscow fears: a fortified belt in front of it and a calendar working against it.

## History: Bombers, Jets, and Surface-to-Air Missiles

In "American Bomber Offensive: How the Luftwaffe Reacted," the channel covers German air defense. The expansion of flak was finite for two reasons: manpower, and the production and ammunition demands that "took a heavy toll on Germany's resources." Estimates suggest Germany needed between 6,000 and 16,000 shots to shoot down one enemy bomber, "depending on the statistic." That's a range wide enough to drive a Flying Fortress through, but even the low end is staggering. Germany's answer was to improve accuracy with gun-laying radar as a stopgap and eventually move toward early surface-to-air missiles. The video also describes tactical adaptation of a smaller number of fighters, which pushed the Americans into night flying and into contact with a strengthened German night defense network. The channel promises that missile story for another video, then remembers it already made one, which is the most relatable thing a content creator has ever done.

In "Me 262 - How Germany Tried To Regain Air Superiority," the verdict is a post-mortem on a wonder weapon. Germany produced a considerable number of engines and airframes under austere conditions, but a decentralized production network ran into limits of manpower, resources, and infrastructure. Only a limited number of Me 262s became operational, and getting skilled pilots was an issue, though less so than for other aircraft. When properly employed, the jet "did present a tactical threat to Allied bombers." But the Allies responded quickly at the tactical level, patrolling above and attacking German airfields with offensive fighter sweeps, which disrupted both production and operations. The channel's conclusion: "Germany's attempt to change the strategic picture through tactics and technology failed." The jet never forced the Allies to abandon daylight bombing or won back control of the air over the Reich. The lesson, which is evergreen: a better aircraft can't outrun a worse logistics tail. Crazy Ralph would have told them that from his bicycle. You're all doomed, lads.

Finally, "B-52: North Vietnam's VS Stratofortress in Linebacker II" looks at the 1972 bombing of Hanoi and Haiphong. In the first eight days, B-52s followed the same course at the same altitudes and airspeeds, and North Vietnamese SAM defenses were "very effective," causing significant losses. The channel notes that other sources claim the main changes started after the fourth day, and that loss statistics did start dropping, with 11 of 50 losses falling in the first four days. Reasons offered for the later improvement: air crews were given more liberty in how to operate, and a suspicion that North Vietnamese SA-2 stocks might have been running low. The summary is that ECM was used to blunt the defense, deficiencies in US procedures were identified and fixed, and the operation's problems at the planning level were crucial.

Air defense is a system, and systems fail at the seams, whether that's manpower, a missile stockpile, or a network that someone else gets to cut first.
