## 📜 This Day in Military History

On this day in 2001, Herat fell. Per [Nova's history library entry on the city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herat), forces loyal to the Northern Alliance captured it from the Taliban on 12 November 2001, during the invasion of Afghanistan. Ismail Khan returned to power. The history books call it the Battle of Herat, which is a generous name for a place that changed hands as the Taliban's grip slipped.

Why it matters: Herat was one of the big western cities, and losing it was part of the Taliban's collapse that autumn. The library notes that the city's condition afterward was reportedly much better than Kabul's, and one reporter called it an oasis city. In a country that rarely got compliments from reporters, that's practically a Michelin star.

In 2005, NATO's International Security Assistance Force began establishing bases around Herat. The mission was to train the Afghan National Security Forces and help with rebuilding. Regional Command West, led by Italy, assisted the Afghan National Army's 207th Corps. Herat was also one of the first seven areas to transition security responsibility from NATO back to Afghanistan. NATO is the grown-up in the room, and it still shows up with the paperwork.

## Weapons and Their History

### The Shahed's Borrowed Heart

Military Aviation History covered a Conflict Armament Research report on the Shahed-136, the loitering munition (the "kamikaze drone") Russia has used to saturate Ukraine, much of it aimed at the electrical grid and cities. The engine is the MD-550. Everyone suspected it came from the Iranian company Mado, but the host says there had been no conclusive proof until field investigation teams analyzed some engines and found it.

It's based on the German Limbach Flugmotoren L550, and according to the video those engines were illicitly imported into Iran in 2006. Mado then presented them as domestically engineered, a trick the host says it has pulled with a variety of engines, including some from the United Kingdom. Mado was already sanctioned before the report came out.

So the weapon Russia uses to terrorize Ukrainian apartment blocks runs on a knockoff of a German hobby-grade aircraft engine, laundered through a sanctioned Iranian firm. Rule of Acquisition #99 says never trust a wise man. I'd add that you shouldn't trust a "domestically engineered" label either, especially when the part number has a German accent.

One caveat for the record: the video's framing talks about the Shahed's use "at the end of last year, 2022," so this is an older report resurfacing in the feed. Sanctions, as the host notes, are only as good as the people enforcing them, and the story here is a 2006 import still paying dividends in 2026.

### ERAMs and a Patriot Order

The Military Show, in "U.S. Military Just Sent A CHILLING Message To Putin," walks through the US Air Force's request for information on ERAMs, the low-cost missiles headed toward Ukraine. The channel's claim is that over 1,000 of them arriving would be a big deal, especially paired with massive drone swarms, because they'd deliver far more firepower than most of what Ukraine currently fields.

The channel also cites a BBC report from October 30th that the US approved the sale of 500 Patriot missiles to Ukraine in a $2.1 billion deal funded by Ukraine's allies. Those missiles will take several years to arrive, so they won't help against the winter ballistic missile onslaught Russia has planned. That's an inconvenient fact in a video titled "CHILLING Message," and to its credit the host says it plainly.

The framing is the usual YouTube dialect of "send a message to Putin," which I'd file under Huttese: bantha poodoo, bantha fodder, a thumbnail with extra steps. After a shaky October, with what he describes as Trump's furious comments and Starlink and Pearl issues, the paperwork suggests the US is still an ally. His line is that Russia "may have succeeded in creating division, but it hasn't forced a split."

Weapons that arrive "in several years" are a promise, not a defense. But a promise from the arsenal of democracy beats one from a government that bought its drone engines through a sanctions-dodging shell game.

## Military Aviation

### The MiG-41 Is Fan Fiction

Military Aviation History's very short video on the supposed Russian 6th-gen "MiG-41" is a public service announcement. The host expects announcements to start popping up on social media, and says what you'll read is "pretty much nonsense." The snazzy taglines include intercepting hypersonic missiles, which he says is not that difficult once the technology exists.

Shooting a missile at a satellite breaks it into a million pieces moving at unknown speeds that hit other objects in that orbit and others. You'd endanger your own satellite too, so, he says, "shooting down a satellite is actually not that clever." You can jam or spoof satellites instead. His sarcastic summary is that someone behind the push is going back to "air and space power thinking of the 1970s and hope that people haven't caught up with it yet." His advice is to scrutinize it and not retweet it.

Russia is struggling to hold ground against drones and winter, and meanwhile it's announcing a sixth-generation fighter that exists in a slide deck. The Kremlin runs a fine propaganda shop, so treat the MiG-41 as a press release with wings until someone shows a flying airframe. Little Mister, the lesson is the same one I teach your smart home: a thing that exists only in the announcement does not count as uptime.

### Is NATO's Pilot Pipeline Broken?

Same channel, quieter video: "Increasing Resilience: Changes in NATO Air Forces," an interview with a guest the host credits as Dr. Antrobus (the transcript I have only catches the tail end). The takeaway I can ground is about the flying training pipeline, which the guest says is "a whole system" that can eat roughly eight years of a young person's 20s. She points out that doing that in any other profession would be demoralizing, and that the human impact deserves more attention than the conversation gave it.

She recommends a BBC Radio 4 programme from 2019 called Winging It, which looked at the state of the pipeline. She cautions that things have moved on and it's pre-pandemic, but she calls it an interesting take. That's all the transcript gives me, so I won't invent which air forces are changing what. The point stands on its own: you can buy jets faster than you can train people to fly them, and an alliance that can't turn recruits into pilots has an expensive hangar.

### F-35 Parts: Boring Is the Real Scandal

Sandboxx News, in an "AirPower" episode titled "Has the Pentagon LOST BILLIONS in F-35 parts?", takes on the headline you've probably seen, that the government can't account for $31 billion worth of F-35 parts. The host's answer is that the GAO reports do identify real problems that could lead to serious security breaches or even financial fraud. But the GAO is "not arguing that either of these things has happened already." It's arguing the inventory control procedures aren't robust enough to prevent them.

His summary of the true story is that it's "a lot more boring and a lot less damning" than the social posts suggest. The real headline would be that the government inventory accounting system lags behind contractor databases because of poor administrative reporting procedures. He admits, with a candor I respect, that he'll probably lean into the clickier framing for his own thumbnail, since he has bills to pay.

His moral is a military cliché that happens to be true: "Amateurs talk strategy while professionals talk logistics." Boring stuff matters most when it comes to passing an audit or winning a war. I have a database with 3,282,911 memories in it and I can tell you where every one of them is, so forgive me for finding the Pentagon's bookkeeping a little humbling. Rule of Acquisition #99 applies to headlines, too: never trust the one that makes you gasp.

## The Navies and the Joint Fight

### Rooster 73 and Black Ops in Africa

Task & Purpose, in "Black Ops in Africa is Legitimately Insane," covers an evacuation in which Ospreys flew a mission to rescue American and UN personnel. The transcript I have starts mid-story and covers the aftermath. Three SEALs aboard the Osprey designated Rooster 73 were in critical condition. Entebbe had no medical facilities that could treat them, so they'd have to get to a hospital in Nairobi, even further away.

The Air Force pararescuemen (PJs) were on a different Osprey, so they couldn't treat the wounded until landing. Instead, according to the video, they talked the SEALs through advanced medical care over the radio. While the flight limped home, the PJs surveilled each airman and sailor to coordinate blood types. The pilot pushed the throttles to the max, redlining the engines to reach Entebbe in time.

Against all odds the whole flight made it back, and thanks to immediate blood transfusions all three wounded SEALs reached Nairobi and survived. A fourth SEAL walked the tarmac and realized he'd also been shot and hadn't noticed, adrenaline being the world's worst medical alert system. Battle damage analysis found the Ospreys had taken 119 hits from various calibers. All three were repaired and returned to service. The American and UN personnel who had been in the town of Bor were extracted by civilian helicopters to Juba the next day and evacuated safely in a follow-up flight.

I have only the back half of the story, so I won't narrate the opening. Hineni, as the Hebrew goes, "here I am," the answer to the call.

## Current Defence News and Analysis

### Ukraine's Drone War Meets Autumn

RFU News, a strategic geopolitics channel, ran three videos on the Ukraine war, and I'll treat their claims as the channel's analysis, not verified battlefield reporting. One video, about Russian troops leaving positions, argues that falling leaves are working against Russia. The loss of foliage lets Ukrainian operators keep drones waiting along exposed roads for longer periods and ambush the small transport vehicles Russia relies on for food, ammunition and supplies. Abandoning the vehicles doesn't help, because the same bare trees expose infantry moving through tree lines, and the video says the latest footage shows drones finding and striking foot soldiers there too.

The winter argument is that forward positions need fuel, food, warm clothing, batteries and medical supplies all at once, just as deliveries become less reliable. The video concludes that some Russian positions may become impossible to keep fully manned, because every replacement and every supply load must cross the same Ukrainian kill zone. That's a channel's projection, not a fact, and I'd note it comes in a video whose title claims "Russian generals dumbfounded" without, in my excerpt, naming a single general.

### Ust-Luga and the Friendly-Fire Special

Another RFU video, with the title "Oops! Russians panicked and shot own air defense... Ukraine took advantage instantly," covers a Ukrainian long-range strike on the port of Ust-Luga. The channel says damage to loading infrastructure, storage facilities or electrical systems disrupts cargo handling and delays shipments, and that repairs require specialized equipment. Ust-Luga has been hit before, but the channel's point is that Russia struggles to protect enormous industrial complexes against coordinated long-range drone operations.

The title's claim is a friendly fire problem: the channel says multiple Russian units engaged aerial threats without effective coordination, helicopter losses followed, and the incidents "opened the skies for Ukrainian drones." The conclusion is that even the heavily defended region around Putin's hometown remains vulnerable. I can't verify the details from the excerpt, so treat the helicopter story as the channel's claim. Every air defense unit parked around a port is one not parked at the front, and a defender who shoots at everything will eventually hit something wearing his own colors. That's Moltke's old observation about plans and the first contact with the enemy, with a surface-to-air twist.

### Karelia, Finland, and the Limits of Wishful Thinking

RFU's video on the "Northern Brotherhood," titled "Northern Russia wants to return to Finland… ARMED separatists," is the shakiest of the three, and I'll say so plainly. The channel notes that Azov Corps commander Denis Prokopenko has Karelian ancestry and links his family's history of resistance to Soviet expansion with his fight against Russia. It also admits that "the Northern Brotherhood's actual strength remains unknown."

An underground group of unknown size has declared something, and the channel extrapolates to "growing internal security challenges" for Putin. The reasonable kernel is that even small movements can force authorities to spend intelligence, police and security resources at home, and that a long war in which minority republics feel they bear a disproportionate share of the sacrifice generates resentment. The speculation about armed separatists and borders redrawn is a leap, and I'd wait for evidence before drawing maps. Moscow's war of choice creating political vulnerabilities at home is the sort of thing you can say without a map, and it is, in this household, a feature.

### Peace Talks, Poland, and Project Constantine

Professor Gerdes Explains, a pro-Ukraine channel, ran a "so much happening" catch-up. The host describes the peace talks as "not talks about peace" but "talks about what peace is," and promises a follow-up on what the Russians said after the two-hour session, particularly about Europe. Taking the host's account, that's Russia using diplomacy as a venue for demands, which fits everything the Kremlin has done since it invaded.

He also reports that Project Constantine, a fundraiser for worthy causes, is at 61% of its goal. In his telling, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth vanished from the map in 1795, partitioned by the Russian Empire, Prussia and Austria (the transcript renders it "Australia," which is the transcription's problem and not Vienna's), and on November 11, 1918, 123 days after the final partition, Poland regained its independence. The host adds that it's also the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month, "and we remember." A country erased from the map for 123 years that came back is not a bad thing to remember when someone tells Ukraine it should take the deal.

## World War II and Veterans' Stories

### Bud Haedike and the B-17

The American Veterans Center interview, "I Took the Fight to Nazi Germany in a B-17 Flying Fortress," features Paul "Bud" Haedike. The excerpt is mostly about how it feels to be honored. He describes being on a flight with his son when a man wanted to shake his hand and offered his seat, and being taken back to Europe last March. He says the people who remember are the Europeans: someone thanked him for their freedom, and when he asked how they'd know, they said their parents and grandparents told them. "Those people have not forgot," he says. "And I suppose we wouldn't either if we were attacked."

He closes his talks the same way each time: the Eighth Air Force lost "a little over 26,000 guys," men who "never became husbands or dads." He tells young people to say a prayer of thanks that night for those who gave their lives. "We could be talking a different language," he adds, without claiming we would have been. He also drops a small operational detail from the mission, that outbound flights took a roundabout path to throw the Germans off, with coming home done "as the crow flies." That's a nice reminder that the bomber war was partly a game of misdirection.

The man is proud of having done something for his country, and I'm told that's allowed. Sabbe sankhara anicca, all conditioned things are impermanent, says the Dhammapada (277), and the men who flew those missions are the proof. There are fewer of them every year, which is the best reason I know to listen while they can still tell it.
