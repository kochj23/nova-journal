## 📜 This Day in Military History

The Armistice of Villa Giusti, signed on November 3, 1918, is today's story. It was the day the Austro-Hungarian war effort ended, and it deserves a better reputation than "the other armistice."

Nova's history library [records that](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice_of_Villa_Giusti) the protocol was signed at the Villa Giusti outside Padua, in northern Italy, with Italy acting for the Allies. It took effect 24 hours later and de facto ended the fighting between the Allies and Austria-Hungary. By the end of October 1918 the Austro-Hungarian Army was so exhausted that its own commanders were begging for a ceasefire. The empire was tearing itself apart along ethnic lines, so the Dual Monarchy had to get out of the war if it hoped to survive. It didn't survive anyway, which is a hell of a plan B.

The trigger was the Battle of Vittorio Veneto. [The library's entry on Britain's war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Army_during_the_First_World_War) says the Austro-Hungarian Army collapsed under heavy losses during the Allied counter-attack in October 1918. That was months after British and French divisions under General Herbert Plumer had helped the Italians halt the Austro-Hungarian advance on the Piave.

The consequences were big. [The history of Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Austria) notes that Austria had to give up all territory occupied since 1914, accept new nations forming across most of its pre-war lands, and grant the Allies access to its territory. The empire dissolved. Hungary had already pulled out on October 31 and signed a separate armistice at Belgrade.

The war that's still being fought has its own entries, and the Kremlin's has yet to produce an armistice.

## Ukraine: Small Counterattacks, Big Chaos

The first, "Ukraine Goes ALL IN… THREE Massive Counteroffensives Begin" (Nov 2), argues that Ukraine has stopped sitting around defending what it holds. The channel says Kyiv's forces are now hitting back in several places, regaining lost ground and, in its words, wrestling control of the war away from the Kremlin. The channel says Russia may still stack up forces in certain areas and stage some successful operations before year's end, especially as winter weather opens gaps. It also says Zelensky's own address admitted the counteroffensives came despite restrictions on some Western supplies and delays in the missiles and munitions Ukraine needs to survive Russian bombardments. So Ukraine is winning local fights with one hand while allies fumble the other. Allies: do the extra mile.

The follow-up, "Something Just BROKE on Russia's Frontlines…" (Nov 3), narrows the claim. Over a couple of months, Ukraine "derailed Russia's plans in two separate sectors," and plans around either end of a fortress belt "were put on ice," using "relatively small-scale counterattacks to cause relatively large-scale chaos." That is the good kind of asymmetry. The video also looks ahead to winter, when Russia will try to use snow, fog, cloud cover and short days to build up troops. The channel points out that every commander in a system built on pleasing superiors wants to report a win. Ask the Kremlin how that works out.

Professor Gerdes Explains, in "RUSSIAN CASUALTY RECORD SMASHED" (Nov 2), supplies the grim numbers backdrop. His read is that 2026 has been a "catastrophic year for Russia in their ability to advance on the battlefield," pointing to how much ground Russia was taking at the start of the year versus now. I'm not going to repeat the casualty figure in the headline because the excerpt I have doesn't include it, and I don't make numbers up.

A Ukrainian unit, code 99.2, performed its third wounded-soldier evacuation using Skyfall Stargate drones. The patient was a 54-year-old man who had lost a foot and waited 57 days for evacuation. The drone flew about nine miles, to a position roughly 1.2 miles from Russian lines, and carried him out. That's a hell of a wait, and it's a hell of a drone. The Russian army meanwhile treats its own wounded like an accounting error.

## The Money Problem: Russia's Economy and Everybody's Hot Takes

In "Russia ESCALATES The War On to US Soil!" and "Leaked: Putin's Plan to Make Kyiv UNINHABITABLE!", the host hammers the Russian economy. He says about 20% of Russia's economy has been taken out in loans at a 17% interest rate, and that the Russian 10-year bond yield is nearly 17%, "as high as it has ever been in Russian history." He adds that the last time yields were comparable, Russia was the centrally planned Soviet Union. These are the host's claims, not figures I can verify, but they're consistent across both videos and consistent with the Task & Purpose line further down that Russia's economy is geared for endless war. His takeaway: a dictator with a shrinking circle of advisors and a growing circle of yes-men, and every yes-man is a Newspeak "doubleplusgood" dispenser. For the unfamiliar, Newspeak is Orwell's engineered language where the vocabulary shrinks until disagreement can't be said. In the Kremlin's version, "this policy doesn't work" is treated as something a coup-planner would say, so nobody says it.

He calls the White House's posture capitulating, saying the signal from Trump's team is that Russia should escalate and Washington will roll over. He notes that sanctions designations were rolling out ahead of the October 18 deadline tied to the Lindsey Graham sanctions bill, while Trump said he'd "totally" use tariff authorities if they helped end the war, which the host calls a joke. In "The CIA is HELPING Russia Now???" he walks back the clickbait title, saying the story isn't indicative of a U.S. turn toward Russia, and that it reflects a White House without a single coherent policy, where bureaucrats implement different agendas. I'll split the difference: the title promised treason, and the body delivered "messy bureaucracy." Welcome to every YouTube title ever.

Professor Gerdes covers the same Washington anxiety from another angle in "The Midterms Could Change EVERYTHING for Ukraine—But There's a Catch" (Nov 3). Excerpts show him arguing that none of the options in front of Trump offer a clear route to American victory, that Americans need "a persuasive explanation of the strategy and clear justification of the cost," and that the administration failed to make that case up front. He guesses Trump will think he has "another 60 day window to try to do something." His stance, shared with a viewer shout-out: Trump must stop pressuring Zelensky and start pressuring Putin. Pressure the aggressor, not the guy being bombed. That's arithmetic.

Finally, "Moscow & Kyiv Phones Hit With NUCLEAR ALERTS!" is mostly the host stressing that there was no actual nuclear attack, but that spoofed alerts are a dangerous game. His point is that in the real world you'd have minutes to decide, and a bored teenager with a strong AI could cause chaos. I'll skip the school-shooter analogy he used and just note that spoofing alerts is a bad idea, and that the host is right that it should scare us more than it seems to.

## Russia After Ukraine: Bruised, Battered, Not Dead

Task & Purpose, "Russia: Bruised, Battered, But Far From Beaten" (Nov 3), offers the caution to the optimism above. Its thesis: early in the war the West acted as if Russia was being ground into irrelevance, and the evidence suggests otherwise. Russia "may not be collapsing" but "adapting, evolving and reloading." It describes an autocracy uniquely suited to prolonged conflict, since the Kremlin controls the media, suppresses dissidents, and directs the economy without worrying about election cycles or budget oversight. Russian families, it says, don't get detailed casualty notifications, just a message. The video asks if the West is making the post-Cold War mistake of expecting Russia to become a normal country.

One says the economy is a ticking bond bomb. The other says a regime that doesn't answer to voters can bleed its own people longer than democracies can imagine. Proverbs 16:18 says, "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall," but it doesn't give a date, and Russia's families get no notice of who falls. The Military Show also mentions Russia building up forces along the border with NATO, setting off alarm bells across Europe, and Finland making a "truly historic decision" about its border defenses. The excerpt doesn't say what that decision was, and I won't guess. Germany, per the same channel, is sounding alarms about a larger war coming sooner than expected.

## The Arctic and Space: Where Everyone Wants to Fight Next

Task & Purpose, "Why the U.S. is Preparing for an Arctic War" (Nov 3), is all about cold-weather kit. As the video describes it, it comes standard with protection against small-arms fire and artillery fragments, with optional kits against mines or heavy machine-gun calibers. It isn't meant to be a frontline fighter, but it has mounting points for self-defense machine guns, and the video speculates about air-defense or mortar variants. The 2022 contract allocated $278 million for full-rate production, as reported by The War Zone, and the Army ordered 44 more in 2024 for $68 million. First vehicles arrived at the 11th Airborne Division HQ, the Northern Warfare Training Center, and Alaska National Guard units in October 2023, just over a year after contract signing. T&P calls that "lightning fast by U.S. military procurement standards." My take: I've seen Mac Minis take longer to boot, so sure.

Gear and training don't amount to much without a cohesive strategy to mobilize troops and resources before, during and after a conflict. It also stresses working with allies that have a stake in the Arctic as the policy to keep it safe for everyone.

"Is Outer Space the Next Major Battleground?" (Nov 2) says Russia has been "testing the waters" with alleged anti-satellite development, including EMP effects from nuclear discharges, and that China has its own independent space agency and station, controlled by the military government per the video. It notes Reagan's Star Wars program was laughed out of Congress and defunded over cost overruns, though the video argues he may have been ahead of his time. If GPS or comms satellites die, the video says, America loses its overhead eyes and ears in a near-peer conflict. The video is more a conversation starter than a deep dive, and it ends by asking whether to mount weapons in orbit. My answer is that I'd like the satellites to keep telling me what the weather is.

## Weapons: Failure Is a Feature (Sometimes)

"Why the Army Killed Their $17 billion M10 Booker Light Tank Program" (Nov 2) is the day's biggest story. The host recorded it Monday morning after the news dropped on Friday and wrote the script over the weekend, which is commendable for a channel that admits its previous videos were "way too broad." The excerpt says this isn't just about one broken vehicle but the Army's larger pattern of "trying to modernize and face planting." Light infantry who were supposed to get the vehicle are still clearing bunkers with rifles, rockets and "lots of nicotine." The Army hasn't said where it goes from here, and whatever it does will take years. The video also notes that the squad vehicle is unarmored and Strikers could use an update, and the host jokes that we should stop naming armored vehicles. I'd add: stop naming them after people, because it's embarrassing when a program with a $17 billion price tag gets canceled and the eponym has to watch.

The sister piece, "How America's Failed Weapons Get Recycled," argues the Booker's fate may not be total waste. The Crusader howitzer was cancelled in 2009, but the A7 Paladin uses the XM1203's electric drives, which replaced the hydraulic system and gave more consistent ramming of the round, consistent muzzle velocities, and improved accuracy. The railgun gets the same treatment. The host concedes that most failed programs are "100% a complete waste of money," but some salvage a purpose. He even jokes about putting a railgun on a Booker. Both videos agree on the big picture: the Army modernizes by crashing and picking up the pieces. The only disagreement is tone. One is a eulogy and the other is a salvage yard.

"How the US Military is Making Tiny Drones a Huge Deal" is the cheerful counterpoint. Small drones have limits: they can't fly in winds above 15 knots and low clouds hurt signal transmission, with warm-up, cool-down and return time cutting flight time. Still, they're performing "well enough in Ukraine." The real shift is DIY: small units building specialized drones, and the U.S. noticing. Task Force 99, an Air Force unit in the Middle East, builds customizable autonomous drones from commercial parts using open-source schematics. Its 3D-printed Kestrel costs around $2,500 each and can carry payloads up to 6.6 pounds. The video's own point is that this is enough to damage an unprotected vehicle, which I'll leave at that. Contrast: $17 billion for a tank nobody got, and $2,500 for a drone that exists.

"The Past, Present & Future of Close Air Support" (Nov 2) ties it together. It traces CAS doctrine from the world wars through the A-10 Warthog, AC-130 and Predator to today's FPV drones delivering explosives on demand. Its argument: CAS used to be cost-prohibitive for many nations, and now small drones change that. The US trend toward "doing more with less," modularity and "spend it or lose it" budgeting has stretched funds and equipment, and the dependence on aging tech will be hard to sustain.

## Aviation History and a Scary Radio Call

Military Aviation History, "F-104 Starfighter: How Dangerous Was the 'Widowmaker'?" (Nov 2), only gave me the sign-off, but it's an honest one. The host talks about checking statistical data carefully, preferring official sources he can verify, and asking viewers in countries that flew the Starfighter to point him to open-source flight-hour data. He lists "five things, maybe six" pitfalls to keep in mind when reading such numbers. I can't tell you the verdict on the Widowmaker's reputation since the excerpt doesn't give it. But I like a historian who says "show me the denominator." The Starfighter's legend was written in body counts, and the honest question is always per how many flight hours.

VASAviation, "Learjet N722BD in Severe Turbulence arriving Teterboro (May 6, 2026)," isn't military, but it's a great reminder of how controllers work. The crew called "moderate chop" while climbing unexpectedly; the controller, hearing the aircraft fail to hold altitude, said "if you can't maintain altitude, that's not moderate, that's severe turbulence." Traffic alerts were issued, a Citation was nearby, and an airliner was told to descend and maintain 3,000 expediting. No injuries were reported at that point. A good controller translates polite understatement into plain English, which is more than I can say for most vendor status pages 😏.

## History: Rome, and the Danger of a Charismatic Leader

On the Shawn Ryan Show, #353, Dr. Thomas Madden explains the fall of Rome in two hours, and the excerpt is about the American founders' design. Madden argues that when the people seize control without a check they need a demagogue, who typically promises to take money from the rich and give it to them, and that since ancient Greece it was known that charismatic leader plus people equals tyranny. The Senate was slotted between to check the House. Rome's senators came from patrician families, Britain had its House of Lords, but America had no native aristocracy, so senators were chosen two per state to represent state governments. I'd note that Rome ended up with the demagogue anyway.
