## 📜 This Day in Military History

The Ottoman Empire spent four years fighting on more fronts than any sane government would want: Sinai and Palestine, Mesopotamia, the Caucasus, Persia and Gallipoli. On October 31, 1918, it ran out of road. The Armistice of Mudros was signed that day, ending Ottoman participation in World War I.

It mattered because the war had been a long, grinding mix of real Ottoman wins and slow exhaustion. Nova's history library notes important Ottoman victories in the early years, including Gallipoli and the Siege of Kut. Winning those battles didn't win the war, and the armistice was the paperwork admitting it. It also opens the story of the empire's dissolution, which redrew the map of the Middle East for the next century. [Nova's history library, "Dissolution of the Ottoman Empire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Ottoman_Empire) has the wider context.

The same date has a darker footnote. On the night of October 31, 1954, the FLN launched the Toussaint attacks, a coordinated wave of seventy bombings and shootings against French armed forces installations and the homes of Algerian loyalists. It was the opening of Algeria's war of independence, and the heavy-handed French response helped convince many Algerians to back the independence movement. The lesson is that heavy-handedness tends to recruit for the other side. [Nova's history library, "History of terrorism"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_terrorism) covers it.

Empires that overreach and then act surprised when the bill arrives should still look familiar to anyone watching the news from Moscow.

## Weapons and Their History: Storm Shadow Does the Thing

Military Aviation History spent an episode on the Storm Shadow, "The Franco-British Stealth Cruise Missile." The narrator's walkthrough of how it works is the cleanest explanation in today's pile. A fighter launches from standoff range, so it never has to go near the air defenses it's attacking. The missile dives to the deck, picks up speed, levels out at low altitude, and uses terrain to mask its approach from known radar sites. For the final stage it climbs to get a look at the target area. An infrared seeker then matches what it sees against the pre-loaded target image, which the narrator says lets it identify the target day or night before it makes, in his words, a beeline for it. In the middle it relies on inertial navigation and terrain referencing, assuming you've got accurate 3D modeling data. He also admits stopping it is hard, especially if you fire salvos or send uncrewed systems ahead to pull the defenders' attention. Countermeasures exist, because they always do, but they're a pain in the ass.

The narrator calls the missile combat proven since the Iraq war, says the RAF used it over Libya in 2011 against air defense and command nodes, and says Ukraine has used it successfully against command posts, bridges and ships. The transcript cuts off mid-word on that last one, so I'll leave it there and not invent a body count of anything.

It's built to be fired from a safe distance at something fixed, with known coordinates. That's a polite way of saying it's for hitting places that can't run away.

## Ukraine's Targeting: The Boring Stuff That Actually Hurts

The Military Show ran "U.K. Storm Shadows Strike OBLITERATES Something IRREPLACEABLE" the same day, and the interesting part isn't the explosion. It's the shopping list. The narrator points out that Ukrainian intelligence could have gone after the obvious targets, drone factories and command posts, but those are the places most likely to have good defenses. Instead, Ukraine went looking for the lesser-known, easily overlooked sites: battery plants, synthetic rubber facilities, and semiconductor factories.

I love this. It's the strategic equivalent of not attacking the front door when the back window has a "please rob me" sticker on it. You can't build a drone without batteries, you can't roll a vehicle without rubber, and you can't make anything modern without chips. The Kremlin's brochure promised a vast tank fleet and massive munition stockpiles, and the channel's argument is that those advantages are being whittled away. The video also links to other pieces, so treat these as the channel's claims and not as things I've verified. One says Russian forces are suffering an almost unprecedented fatality rate, with more soldiers dying than being wounded, something the narrator says hasn't been seen in war for over a century. Another says Russia is massing T-90M tanks, armored vehicles and advanced Malva artillery near the borders of Finland and the Baltics. Which is, you'll note, awfully close to NATO. Good thing the alliance exists. I'd say sleep tight, but I know none of you do.

The Military Show tells you who picks the target, and that part is the smarter half. A missile is only as clever as the intelligence officer who typed in the coordinates.

## Veterans' Stories: The Signalman Who Wouldn't Stay Quiet

The best human story of the day is in The Military Show's "NATO Just Did Something Putin Didn't Expect & Other War Stories." The transcript we have starts in the middle, so I only have the back half, and I'm going to be careful with it.

It follows a Russian soldier named Stupnikov who helped Ukraine. The Russian Volunteer Corps, the RVC in the video, needed a communications specialist, and the video says Zaporizhia was part of the geography of the story. The offer was blunt: a one-way ticket. The only two ways he could leave the RVC were if Ukraine won the war or if he became a casualty. The narrator notes he had more than earned his freedom already, and could have said no. He knew the safest place for him was now Ukraine, among people he'd helped. After a couple of days thinking, he said yes, on one condition: his wife could join him.

His wife didn't. She stayed in Russia and the marriage was over. The transcript says she later told Russian state media something about her husband, and it cuts off before the line. Whatever it was, it came from state media, so I'm treating it as exactly the kind of source you take with a shovel. His mother and sister did leave. Before Russia worked out what had happened, and the video says that only came when he began giving interviews about his exploits, the two women had gone to Moldova. A man named Karpenko met them there and smuggled them into Kyiv and back to their son and brother.

Reunited briefly, he told them he'd fight with the RVC. They asked whether this was his war to fight after all he'd done. The narrator says he knew he had a new purpose. In Russia he's branded a traitor. Ukraine welcomed him as a soldier who'll do what it takes. The video says he can never return home, where he's wanted on charges that the transcript cuts off after "war-rel." I won't finish that sentence for them.

A man picked a side, lost a marriage over it, and got his mother and sister out before the state noticed. The cue light says I get one: the Kremlin's counterintelligence needed state media interviews to figure out where three of its citizens had gone, and that's the single most competent thing about the story, because they didn't. 😏 The rest is straight.
