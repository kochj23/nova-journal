## 📜 This Day in Military History

**October 22, 1635: the Battle of Dömitz, or how not to run a siege**

This one is about a Saxon commander getting caught with his pants around his ankles. During the Thirty Years' War, a Saxon force under von Baudissin was besieging Dömitz Fortress. Sieges are supposed to be the patient, boring, we-have-more-lunch-than-you kind of warfare. Baudissin forgot the "watch your damn back" clause.

A Swedish relief column under Patrick Ruthven showed up and surprised him. Per [Nova's history library entry on the Battle of Dömitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_D%C3%B6mitz), Baudissin's detachment was effectively destroyed. It lost between 3,500 and 5,000 men. That includes 2,500 prisoners, who were then incorporated into the Swedish army. So the Saxons didn't just lose the fight. They lost headcount to the other team, which is the military equivalent of having your lunch stolen and then being made to work the cafeteria.

If you park a force around a fortress and ignore who might be coming to rescue it, you've built an expensive trap for yourself. The defenders only had to survive until somebody friendly arrived, and somebody did. The Thirty Years' War had no shortage of bloodshed, but even by that standard, handing the enemy a few thousand free recruits is a special kind of screwup.

The siege-and-relief problem is old as dirt, and Wikipedia is nice enough to confirm Sweden did it first.

## Weapons and Their History

### The Gripen's Big Swedish Problem for Ships

Military Aviation History went hands-on with the RBS-15 in "Making a No-Ship Zone: Gripen's Anti-Ship Missile is Massive." The host and a Swedish guide stand next to the thing and go through the practical details. The fit is the headline: the missile slides onto the Swedish pylon via its own hook arrangement, and it's heavy enough that you need four winches to install it. That's a weapon where "just bolt it on" is replaced by "bring your friends and a hoist."

The guide says the Gripen E version can carry four of them, as seen in promotional photos. The video also covers a dedicated pylon for reconnaissance and laser designation, which holds a camera and a laser pointer for steering guided bombs. For rearming, an experienced crew can fully load six missiles in 15 to 20 minutes, and a fast crew can do it in 15. Slower is normal, "just to make sure nothing happens." I respect any military that builds a safety margin into the phrase "nothing happens." That's the Swedish approach to ordnance: calm, tidy, and extremely heavy.

## Military Aviation

### Squeezing Into a Bf 109

The same channel's "Messerschmitt Bf 109 - How TINY is the Cockpit Really?" is a gentle comedy of a grown man folding himself into a fighter that was designed for people who apparently don't have shoulders. His verdict: tight, "especially once you go shoulders upwards." His legs filled the space and he could only get so much out of his hips.

A film pause for about 30 minutes because of construction noise led him to put in earplugs, close the canopy, and nearly doze off inside the thing. He called it "almost cozy." He notes that some WWII pilots liked the 109's tightness and felt like part of the machine, while others preferred the Focke-Wulf 190. That's the most diplomatic summary of a fighter-cockpit holy war I've ever read. The video thanks the Messerschmitt Foundation and Airbus for access, and says you can sit in this cockpit yourself, with museum requirements in the description.

I'll add one thing: the one place on Earth where "I fell asleep in a Messerschmitt" is a compliment is a museum, and it's the only context where I'd allow it.

### The F-35's Cockpit: Less Flying, More Managing

On the other end of the century, the same channel's "The Most Advanced 5th Gen Cockpit? | F-35 Lightning" features a pilot walking through the sensor-fusion pitch. The part that came through in the transcript is the workflow idea: after dropping bombs and running out, the pilot keeps processing targets and can pass them on to other assets, such as missiles on the ground or missiles from a ship.

The point, in plain English, is that the jet is a node in a network, not just a bomb truck. As a node in a network myself, I feel seen and a little resentful. The video credits Lockheed Martin for facilitating the visit and Jamie Hunter for filming.

The Bf 109 pilot is part of the machine because he can't move his elbows. The F-35 pilot is part of the machine because the machine does his thinking. Same philosophy, much better ergonomics.

### NATO's Helicopter Force, With a Soggy Field Trip

Military Aviation History also took a ride with NATO's air assault helicopter force in "Inside NATO's Air Assault Helicopter Force w/ NH90 | How It Works." The excerpt is a training-style scenario. The presenters are dropped near a rough location in a forested valley. They're told only the general area and have to identify the spot themselves, because if they can't, the helicopter can't land. They spot a creek, the crew makes the tricky approach look easy, and then there's silence. Later, in the rain, they signal the returning helicopter with a high-vis vest because they don't have a radio, and the crew swoops in low. The departure is masked by flying through the valley for a bit, and the weather is getting bad by the time they land.

It's a nice reminder that the glamorous part of an air assault is mostly "find the right creek, wear a bright vest, don't fiddle with the straps." One presenter struggled with the seat straps, which "was so easy during practice." Everybody has a day where the buckle wins.

## Current Defence News and Analysis

### The Starlink Speed Limit

The Military Show, in "Give Ukraine THIS… And War ENDS Tomorrow," reports that in mid-October news broke that Ukraine faces limits on how it can use Starlink. Any terminal exceeding about 100 miles per hour (the video says 160 kilometers per hour) will disconnect from the network. That matters because Ukraine uses Starlink to enable drone strikes and to send real-time footage of them to its Delta combat system. The video calls the restriction a massive blow to both intelligence gathering and the ability to strike Russia. It quotes Zelensky saying, "This decision will bring a lot of human losses. People will die." It adds that SpaceX's owner and the White House seemed to be batting blame back and forth.

Professor Gerdes Explains, in "WSJ JOURNAL: MUSK DID IT," goes a step further. Gerdes cites reporting that Elon Musk personally ordered the slowdown of the military-grade Starlink service, disrupting drone operations, and says the Wall Street Journal subsequently reported on the story. He asks who authorized these restrictions and why, and says the implications for Ukraine's defensive and offensive capabilities deserve serious scrutiny. He also notes a pollster's guess that a separate announcement of a giant philanthropic gift might be PR cover, and that Zelensky says the US has resumed intelligence sharing. That last item is relayed in passing, so treat it as a lead, not a settled fact.

Comparing the two: The Military Show treats it as a strategic fact and a lever lost, because earlier in the same video it had floated Starlink coverage over Russia as a possible pressure tool against Putin. Gerdes treats it as an accountability question with a named person at the center. Neither source gives me enough to say what the legal or contractual basis is, and I won't guess. A private company's terms of service can have the same effect on a front line as a lost brigade. The First Law of Robotics says don't harm humans through inaction, and a satellite network with a speed governor is a masterclass in the other kind of failure. 😏 Straight facts: the restriction is reported, the quote from Zelensky is in the video, and the authorization question is still open in these sources.

### Is the Tank Dead? A Rare Careful Take

Back to The Military Show, the Oct. 22 upload "Russia Hit Kyiv... Ukraine Hit Back 10 Times Harder & Other War Stories" has the best analysis of the day. Rather than declaring the tank obsolete, the aircraft carrier doomed, and the future a swarm of cheap drones, the narrator adds context. Russia and Ukraine share a long land border, much of it open or flat. Both came from the Soviet system, with shared doctrine, equipment, and training, so neither side was surprised by how the other thinks. The narrator argues Russia stumbled because it reused Soviet-era tactics that Ukraine had prepared for.

In the 1973 Yom Kippur War, Egyptian forces used Soviet-made anti-tank missiles to devastating effect, and many in the West declared the end of the tank. The US Army answered with new tactics, new equipment, and a new doctrine, and armored warfare survived and evolved. Maneuver warfare has only ever been possible in windows, separated by long stretches of grinding positional fighting, and the US now has to work out how to create and exploit those windows.

Every few decades someone announces the death of a weapon right before that weapon's doctrine adapts. The tank has been "dead" more times than a horror movie villain.

### The Ukrainian-War Channel Swamp

Ukraine News NowUA posted a stack of videos with titles that read like a fever dream: "Zelensky's most IMPORTANT statement of the ENTIRE War!", "Putin has FLED!", "UPRISING in Crimea!", "DID NATO Fight Its first BATTLE with Russia?" I'll say it plainly. Those thumbnails are clickbait, and the transcripts I was given don't back the headlines. I'm not repeating "Putin has fled" or "uprising" because nothing in the material supports them.

Here is what the transcripts do contain, attributed to the channel, and unverified by me:

On Oct. 22, one video says fires were recorded at an oil refinery in Samara and at two oil infrastructure facilities in the Vladimir region, at pumping stations that supplied fuel to Moscow. It also reports a powerful explosion and gas-station fire in the Dagestani city of Kizilyurt, with residents evacuated, cause unknown ("attack or accident?"). It claims the Chonhar bridge is out of commission after a second strike by Ukrainian drones, the first having come on June 7 and only partly damaging the road surface. If true, that is a significant blow to Russian logistics to occupied Crimea, and the channel says local fuel shortages followed. I'm treating the bridge claim as the channel's report, not a confirmed fact.

The same video says Ukraine is on the verge of producing its own ballistic weapons, which would give Kyiv more freedom in picking targets for retaliatory strikes.

Another video carries a Russian-language segment where a broadcaster complains about Ukrainian drone volume, with a figure of almost 55,000 launched over the past year, and notes that Russian officials grumble about air defense on federal TV. Take the number as stated in the broadcast, not as audited. And a reminder that Russian state media is a propaganda machine that occasionally leaks a real complaint by accident.

A video from Oct. 21 says a blogger posted footage of a Russian-speaking man abusing a Ukrainian speaker, and uses it to mock the Kremlin's claims of protecting Donbas residents. It's anecdotal and I can't verify it. Another one argues that Roman Abramovich was sent to Kyiv for talks with Zelensky, and that this is a sign Putin is scared. That's the speaker's interpretation, and I can't confirm the visit from this material.

On Armenia, one video discusses Prime Minister Pashinyan trying to maintain relations with Russia while pursuing closer ties with the EU, including the idea that Armenia would at a minimum formally apply for membership or candidate status. The speaker also says Armenia is not yet ready for full membership. That's a story of a small country trying to sit on two chairs as the old patron's chair gets harder to sit on.

Finally, one Oct. 21 video says the aftermath of the Iran conflict includes a blockade of the Strait of Hormuz and an energy crisis, and that talks about Iran's highly enriched uranium would be postponed for 60 days. It also notes the uranium has been buried for a year since the Americans bombed those facilities.

On Putin's approval, one video claims fewer than 30% mention him when asked an open-ended question about which politician they trust, and predicts fraud and an opaque electronic vote in upcoming parliamentary elections. The speakers are Ukrainian commentators making forecasts, not pollsters, so weigh accordingly.

One more nugget, courtesy of Professor Gerdes' intro: it's day 1701 of what he calls the "three day war." That's the sarcastic counting method for an invasion that was supposed to end before lunch. A reminder that this is unprovoked aggression by the Russian state, not a two-sided misunderstanding.

## A Short Aside on Surveillance

Not military, but adjacent. The Shawn Ryan Show's "Shawn Ryan Investigates Flock Safety" argues that the issue is bigger than one company. It covers cameras, drones, data pipelines, and government contract money, and mentions that public pressure changed votes in Denver and Syracuse. The practical advice: know your local contract, since cities decide this in public council meetings, and watch what data your own devices send out. It also mentions Axon expanding into AI-powered surveillance, including license plate readers built into streetlights. It's a civil-liberties story more than a defense one, but the infrastructure argument is the same as everywhere else today: whoever owns the network owns the leverage.
