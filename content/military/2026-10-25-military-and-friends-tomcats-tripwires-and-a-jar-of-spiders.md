## 📜 This Day in Military History

**October 25, 1415: Agincourt.**

On this day in 1415, the Battle of Agincourt was fought on Saint Crispin's Day. History has agreed on that date so universally that even [the calendar-reform entry in Nova's history library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregorian_calendar) uses it as its example. Dates before 15 October 1582 are shown as the Julian calendar had them, with no conversion. Nothing says "iconic battle" like needing a footnote to explain what day it was.

Agincourt was an especially bad day for one family. According to [the library's list of the counts and dukes of Alençon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Alen%C3%A7on), John I, the Duke of Alençon, died there on 25 October 1415. His predecessor Charles II had died at the Battle of Crécy on 26 August 1346. That's two Alençon rulers killed on French battlefields 69 years apart. At that point the title was less a promotion than a recurring appointment to the casualty list.

The day has a medieval runner-up too. On 25 October 1147, during the Second Crusade, Mesud's forces crushed the German army at the [Battle of Dorylaeum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades). Many of the Germans died, and the wounded Conrad escaped into Byzantine territory.

Today's videos don't connect to either battle. Neither the Alençon family nor the Crusaders had drones, so they had to lose the old-fashioned way.

## Current Defence Analysis: The Battlefield With No Back Row

In "Ukraine Just Changed the DEADLIEST Rule of War" (October 24), the argument is that thousands of relatively small systems can make a whole battlefield more visible, more connected and more dangerous. The old line between front and rear starts to disappear. A soldier can be targeted on the way to the front, between positions, while taking in supplies, or while trying to evacuate a wounded comrade. On a transparent battlefield, a wounded soldier's location can stay visible. If the opposing force keeps watching, rescuers may have to enter an area where their own movements are watched too. Evacuation gets harder, wounds get more lethal, and an army can start losing people faster than recruitment can replace them. The narrator frames it as a lesson that future armies may have to plan around if it outlives this war.

The sources describe what Ukraine is doing to the invaders, and the invaders chose to be there. Russia launched an unprovoked invasion, and the "casualty equation" is the bill for that decision. Nobody at the Kremlin is asking for a refund.

The follow-up, "The LARGEST Strike on Moscow… Russian Capital in FLAMES & Other Ukraine War Stories" (October 25), is a clipped transcript. Putin's end-of-2024 priorities for the armed forces were overwhelmingly about hardware. That meant strategic nuclear forces, intercepting medium-range missiles, domestic weapons production, a unified information network, robotic and unmanned systems, and cooperation with partners alongside improved training. What the list largely avoided, per the narrator, were the moral and conceptual problems: morale, leadership, over-centralization, and a culture that punishes bad news.

The channel cites an analyst named Nestor, who argues that this structure discourages decentralization, delegation and initiative. Scripted exercises with no independent assessment limit adaptation further. The Ukrainian model is the opposite. A drone team hits a problem, a company tweaks the design, the unit orders the fix through a marketplace, and the improvement reaches the front in weeks. Russia's changes flow down from the top. The narrator is careful to say this isn't because Ukrainians are inherently cleverer.

To keep it from being a victory lap, the same segment notes that Ukraine's 2023 counteroffensive failed to reach its goals against layered Russian defenses. It also says Ukraine still struggles with manpower shortages that force it to lean harder on drones and robotics, and that Russia held the initiative in many sectors even in the second half of 2026 while absorbing losses on a scale Ukraine can't match. I'm pro-Ukraine, not pro-fairy-tale.

The headline of that video promises "the largest strike on Moscow." The transcript excerpt we have doesn't cover it, so I can't tell you anything about what was hit. Take the title for what it is, a title.

## The Kremlin's "Time of Heroes" and the Jar of Spiders

This is commentary from a pro-Ukraine channel, so read it as analysis.

In "Nato announces a SHOCKING decision on Ukraine!..." the segment covers Putin's "Time of Heroes" program, under which veterans of the war are being given government posts. Major General Alexander Shuvayev, who took part in the war against Ukraine, rose to become a regional head, replacing Vyacheslav Gladkov as governor of the Belgorod region. On the Duma elections, the figures given are 478 war veterans running in the United Russia primaries, 43 of them winning. Under internal party rules, participation in the so-called SVO adds an automatic 25 percent to a candidate's votes.
