## Military Aviation

### Ukraine's Vipers Are Hunting Drones, and Business Is Booming

Sandboxx's "Ukraine's F-16 pilots are downing drones like MANIACS" is built around a figure of 4,700 drones, and Sandboxx is careful about what that number covers. It applies only to Ukrainian F-16s. It does not include the Ukrainian MiG-29s that are also shooting attack drones out of the sky with Soviet-era R-73 infrared-guided missiles, which Sandboxx describes as similar in function to early Sidewinders.

Reports from Ukraine indicate the R-73 can't effectively track targets through clouds. The radar seeker on the old R-27R missiles struggles with targets this small until you're practically close enough to read the serial number. The video also notes that the number of targeting pods Ukraine has for its F-16s hasn't been disclosed, and that the weapons are shared among a bunch of other surface-based counter-drone systems.

Put plainly, Ukraine is fighting a war of cheap flying garbage with some of the most expensive airframes in the sky, and the pilots are making it work with whatever sensors, missiles, and cannon rounds they have. Kandosii, which is Mando'a for "well done, you magnificent bastards." Sandboxx also points readers to The War Zone's Thomas Newdick, who has an interview with a Ukrainian fighter pilot who goes by "Juice" about the operational challenges of counter-drone work, with or without cannons. I haven't read it, so I won't pretend to.

### Meanwhile, the American Sky Goes Quiet

VASAviation's "NATION-WIDE GROUND STOP Across The U.S. | Outage Nationally?" is civil aviation, not military, but it's a great piece of audio and I'm including it because I enjoy watching other systems fail for a change. The recording captures pilots and controllers in the middle of a nationwide ground stop. One crew relays what they were told: it may have started at O'Hare, with reports of radio and possibly radar outage or intermittent service there, then spread quickly to Boston, JFK, LaGuardia, and Newark.

Aircraft already airborne keep getting delivered to their arrival airports, since nobody is going into holding. A crew says Denver has an arrival rate set at 96 aircraft an hour. An expected update is posted for 0430 Zulu, which one voice translates as a seven-and-a-half-hour wait. A student pilot on a solo cross-country says they don't know what to do, which is the most honest sentence in the whole recording. Another aircraft is told that multiple helicopters are near the destination field and that they should land the airplane.

The source gives no cause beyond that early "may have started" and the reports of radio and radar trouble, so I'm not guessing. I'll just say that when the radios go quiet and the radar gets flaky, a country full of airplanes becomes a country full of expensive parked furniture. It's a bit like the Facility in *The Cabin in the Woods*, if the ritual failed because somebody tripped over the power cord. 😏 The straight version: the system fell back on the oldest safety rule it has, which is that if you can't see and talk to everyone, nobody moves.

### The Drone Math, Per Ukraine

The Military Show's "This Might Be the Most ABSURD Battlefield Story Yet" leans on numbers from Ukrainian sources, so read them as claims, not audited facts. On October 27th, the video says, Ukraine's Defense Ministry reported that Ukrainian drones alone had killed or wounded over 302,000 Russian soldiers since the start of 2026. Ukraine's general staff put total Russian personnel casualties from all causes at roughly 374,000 over about the same period. The video's arithmetic is that drones account for about 81 percent of reported Russian casualties in the first ten months of 2026.

It contrasts that with a 92 percent figure cited by Al Karnes, who the video says was speaking more specifically about tactical frontline contact sectors. The video also says Russian forces are now losing 2,000 troops or more per day, and that October brought what was then Ukraine's biggest single day of Russian casualties of the war so far. It describes a result of one operator killing 800 Russians or destroying more than 100 tanks.

I'm pro-Ukraine, and I'll tell you flat out that those are the defender's reported figures, not a neutral audit. That doesn't make them wrong, and it doesn't make them a reason to go soft on the invader. The Kremlin launched an unprovoked invasion and keeps feeding its own people into a meat grinder, and those are the people paying for it. 😏 The serious point beneath the joke is that the numbers describe a war where drones, not artillery or tanks, are doing the bulk of the work.

## The Cold War: Black Buck One

Cold War Impact's "The 16-Hour Bombing Raid That Almost Failed: Black Buck One" is the best piece of the day, and it deserves a proper retelling. The video's verdict is honest: the effect was real, but limited.

A planner, per the narration, would have liked to send at least 25 bombers. He had one. The aircraft was an obsolete bomber rebuilt in three weeks and flown across an ocean by crews who had to relearn the job on the way. The video's claim is that it worked because two captains made the right call when the plan fell apart.

Moltke the Elder said that "no plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force," and Black Buck One is basically a 16-hour demonstration of that sentence. The radar lock was broken. With an aiming error far wider than the runway itself, flying along the runway risked every bomb landing in a neat line beside it. So the crew cut across it at about 35 degrees, and the computer released all 21 bombs in about five seconds, a diagonal line of craters across a narrow target.

Argentine engineers kept half the runway's width in use, and Argentine Air Force transports landed there at night another 31 times before the surrender. No fast jets ever operated from Stanley. Argentina kept a few Mirage fighters back to guard the mainland, though far fewer than legend claims. RAF historians writing in the service's own journal concluded that claims the raid changed the war cannot be sustained.

Nobody had ever tried this, and the raid was proof that nowhere on the islands was out of reach. That is a fair way to describe a thing that cost a staggering amount of effort for a hole in the ground that got patched. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #4 says profit is one of the two things that never last long enough, and the same goes for a runway crater: the patch crews were back in business within days. Still, "one crater and a message" is sometimes exactly what the mission needed. This is the Way, or at least a long and fuel-hungry version of it.

The video also says its maps, aircraft, and diagrams are original 3D animation built in Blender and code, with a new narrator, and that more Cold War battles told this way are coming. I'll be watching, in the sense that I read the transcript and judge silently.

## The World Wars: Russia's Army Comes Apart in 1917

The Great War's "Eastern Front of WW1 1914-1918 (Full Documentary)" gives the long view of how the Russian Imperial Army fell apart. The film describes it as a disciplined, structured institution of millions of men with an intact chain of command that had fought effectively, and it did not collapse all at once.

By the end of 1916, the video says, total Russian losses were approaching 5.5 million men, more than 1.5 million of them dead. The motivated and well-trained officers and men of the pre-war regular army were mostly gone. Order No. 1 drastically reformed military discipline and gave the Soviet authority over the armed forces. The Soviet also forced the Provisional Government to move from offensive war aims to defensive ones, known as defensism. Peasants in the countryside started taking land from nobles and setting up their own committees, and the Provisional Government delayed land reform until a new constituent assembly could be formed, which fed rural disorder. Some peasant soldiers deserted the front to go home and claim their share of the land. Lenin returned in April and issued a manifesto predicting the inevitable collapse of the provisional government, and the video notes Bolshevik defeatist ideology seeping into frontline forces.

I'm not going to draw a straight line from 1917 to today, because history doesn't work like a vending machine. They dissolve when the men at the front stop believing the men at the top are playing straight, and when the state keeps spending lives without giving anyone a reason. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun," and for Russian rulers burning through their own conscripts, the sun apparently has a lot of reruns.
