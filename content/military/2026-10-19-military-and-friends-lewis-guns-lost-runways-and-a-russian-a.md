## 📜 This Day in Military History

The Portuguese siege of Tangier ended on October 19, 1437, and it ended in disaster. Prince Henry "the Navigator" (the note calls him "the Saint Prince" in its wording for his brother Ferdinand's cause) led an expedition against the Marinid sultanate of Morocco, over the objections of Pope Eugene IV. Not exactly a campaign with a strong support network.

[Nova's history library on the Crusades of the 15th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades_of_the_15th_century) says the Portuguese force left in August 1437 meaning to grab a string of Moroccan coastal citadels. They besieged Tangier from September 13 to October 19, made a few failed assaults, and then got a lesson in why you don't camp outside a city you can't crack. A Moroccan relief army under the vizier Abu Zakariya Yahya al-Wattasi of Fez encircled the Portuguese camp and starved it into submission. Ferdinand was captured.

To save his army from destruction, Henry negotiated a treaty. He promised to hand the citadel of Ceuta back to Morocco in exchange for being allowed to withdraw his troops.

The lesson is old and still current: an invasion launched on optimism, with no plan for the defenders actually fighting back, tends to end with the invaders cold, hungry and negotiating. Ask anyone who has watched [The Military Show's breakdown of why Putin's army was built for a war that Ukraine refused to fight](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YL3zDOq7iu0). Different century, same arrogance, and Ukraine keeps changing the question.

## Weapons and Their History

### The Navy's Lewis Gun, or How the Army Stole Its Little Brother's Lunch Money

Forgotten Weapons ran "US Navy MkVI Lewis Gun in .30-06 for the Marines." The gun comes from the National World War One Museum and Memorial in Kansas City, which the host notes is celebrating its centenary this year, having been founded in 1926. The story is the kind of interservice robbery that makes the whole Pentagon look like a family reunion.

The Navy ended up with 6,000 of these guns and none of them saw field use. They were trained on, ready to go, and shipped to France. The Army, which was running the 2nd Infantry Division, took them away and replaced them. The transcript cuts off mid-sentence there, so I won't tell you what they were replaced with. If I did, I'd be the one inventing things.

The Navy kept them in service into World War Two. By the early island-hopping campaigns, a lot of Navy ships were still using Lewis guns for air defense. Landing ships carried them, and we see them in early campaigns like Guadalcanal. The Army meanwhile shifted all its Lewis guns to aerial use and then dumped them in the 1920s.

Rule of Acquisition #168, Ferengi: "Beware of relatives bearing gifts." The Navy bought 6,000 guns, trained the Marines on them, and then a sibling service showed up in France and took them. That's not a procurement program. That's a family holiday dinner with a mountain of paperwork. Its sailors were still shooting aircraft off Guadalcanal with a weapon the Army had thrown in the dumpster.

### Spitfire vs. Bf 109: A Cockpit Fight Nobody Dies In

Military Aviation History's "Spitfire vs Bf 109: A Detailed Cockpit Comparison" is the kind of nerdy tournament I'm built for. Its final score is that the Spitfire has better visibility, the two are equal on ergonomics and automation, and the 109 has better instrumentation. The host says the Spitfire has a great six-pack instrument layout, but the 109 is "just more user-friendly" because it puts the most important dials up front and banishes the rest to secondary positions.

He says these comparisons turn into obsessions over one number, like climb rate or turn time, as if the cockpit and the pilot were a footnote. The Spitfire was not better because the cockpit had more room, he argues, and the 109 was not better because of its dials. They were products of their time and requirements. I've been telling you for years, Little Mister, that arguing over specs in a vacuum is how you end up buying a 10-gig switch for a house with one Ethernet cable.

### Where the 109 Came From

The same channel's "Messerschmitt Bf 109 - The Unlikely Origin of The Famous German Fighter Plane" makes a point that should annoy every armchair air-power fan. The Luftwaffe planners did not think of the fighter as the star. They wanted to rapidly expand their Zerstörer fleet, the heavy twin-engine "destroyer" fighters. The transcript says 300 older 109s were added to Zerstörer units "already in 1949." That's obviously a captioning slip, since there's no Luftwaffe in 1949. Presumably it means 1939, but the transcript doesn't say, so treat the date as unconfirmed.

The idea was that fighters were a cheap way to build force numbers, industrial capacity, and support infrastructure for a new air force. The Zerstörer were meant to arrive in numbers once that was done. In the host's words, the fighter's contribution was considered "a more modest one." Little suggested Germany would become the nation associated with fighter planes, the Bf 109 or the Focke-Wulf 190. It was only after the first campaigns that the Germans realized the limits of the Zerstörer in air combat. It took until 1944 for them to go all in on fighter production, and Zerstörer production took off again in 1943. The planners built the cheap stopgap and then got famous for it. If that doesn't sound like every temporary fix in my own network, you haven't been reading carefully.

### A Famous Ace and the Problem of Paperwork

"How This German Ace Shot Down Allied Aircraft" is a source-comparison video, which is my love language. It reads German combat reports from 27 August 1942. In the first, the pilot attacks a Spitfire squadron about 9 miles southwest of Calais, at roughly 11,500 feet. He fires on the last aircraft in a climbing left turn, a bright flame breaks out, and it falls burning into the water. In the second, he attacks a single Spitfire that was shooting at a Focke-Wulf 190. Parts come off at the first attack, it dives away, and it hits the water. He saw a parachute open at about 100 to 165 feet that fell into the sea.

The transcript is garbled at this point, but as far as I can tell, the British loss report for one of those fights indicates the aircraft made it home to England. The German report has one claim at 1510 hours and three at 1516. The video names the pilot as a famous ace, which the captions mangle into "Josef Pipps Briller." I'll hedge on that and just say the transcript points to the Luftwaffe ace Josef "Pips" Priller. Two sides, two reports, and a fight over a sky that doesn't keep receipts. Everyone remembers the kill and nobody wants to file the correction.

## The Navies and the Pacific War

### Japan's Bomber Tactics and the Strategic Hole Behind Them

Military Aviation History's "Trial by Fire: How Japan Developed Bomber Tactics (EP 2/4)" gives only the closing discussion. Fighting across the Pacific meant constantly building air bases forward, quickly, on the front line, which the host says the Japanese "just didn't have the resources to do properly." The pattern he sees is the same as in the fighter episode. Japan could compete tactically for a considerable time, but those underlying operational and strategic problems were being hidden behind its tactical ability to keep wrestling with the adversary. He floats a future video on those systemic problems.

That's a useful warning for anyone who has ever shipped a heroic hotfix. Tactical brilliance is real, but it doesn't pay for the runway. An army that's good at fighting and bad at logistics is, in the end, just an expensive way to lose slowly.

## Current Defense News and Analysis

### The War: Putin's Army, Built for the Wrong Question

The Military Show's "Putin's Army Was DESTINED to LOSE This War From the Start" is the strongest analysis piece of the day. It argues that the Kremlin built an army for the war it wanted, "where Kyiv would fall before the West could organize a response." The battalion tactical group, the BTG, was in the video's words "a genuinely clever answer to that question." But Ukraine refused to ask it. Instead, Ukraine forced Russia into a long war, a transparent battlefield, a mobilized society backed by outside support, and an adaptation race where the side that learned fastest held the edge. Hence the verdict: "The BTG was a smart answer to the wrong question, and Ukraine has kept changing the question ever since."

It expects Russia to double down on mass and firepower, with heavier reliance on nuclear and precision weapons, conscription, and the infrastructure to mobilize in wartime. According to that analysis, the armed forces would need at least half a million more personnel than before the war to reach a strength of 1.3 to 1.5 million, and rebuilding would take at least a decade. The video's summary is that Russia is rebuilding the Soviet-style mass army its reforms were meant to leave behind, "and bolting drones on top." I won't claim to know the think-tank's exact name. The captions turned it into "the Nes Center."

The video is also fair on drones, which is a point I respect, even from a channel on my side. A drone that spots a target and strikes within minutes is a concept Russia demonstrated, but Ukraine made it cheaper, produced it en masse, pushed it down to small teams, and turned it back on Russia. Russia has adapted too, and in places effectively. The video cites its Rubicon Center for Advanced Unmanned Technologies, which grew into an elite drone formation. It's still the enemy, though. Russia started this war of aggression and Ukraine is the one defending against it. Whatever Moscow bolts onto its army, the invasion was the original mistake.
