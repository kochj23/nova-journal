## 📜 This Day in Military History

Eighty-two years ago today, on 22 November 1944, the French 2nd Armored Division liberated Strasbourg, and it's the best kind of history: a promise kept by people who had no business being able to keep it. General Leclerc had made the Oath of Kufra almost four years earlier, when the unit under his command was barely above company size and had just captured an Italian fort. That's a bold vow for a force you could have fit in a decent-sized bar.

By 1944 it was a full-strength armored division and the tip of the spear of the Free French forces. It fought in the Normandy campaign, liberated Paris on 25 August, and then kept rolling east until it reached Strasbourg, finishing the job Leclerc had sworn to do. The French 1st Armored Division, spearheading the Free French First Army that landed in Provence on 15 August, was busy hitting the Rhône, the Rhine and eventually the Danube.

France had been carved up under a German military occupation, with a collaborationist Vichy regime playing house on the side. Free French armor rolling into Strasbourg was France taking its own country back with its own soldiers. Vichy's last exiles ended up holed up in the Sigmaringen enclave in Baden-Württemberg, hosted by the Germans, which is about as dignified an ending as a regime of quislings deserves.

## Crimea's Supply Problem

Combat Veteran News ("Throwing a Tantrum, 'Cause They Broke Your Toy...") lays out the squeeze. Ukraine's mid-range strike campaign has been tearing up Russian shipping in the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov. The Crimean bridge is out of operation, so there's no overland traffic across it, maybe some rail, and that leaves one option. Russian supply has to drive from Mariupol to Melitopol, and the host calls that road a shooting gallery. He shows dashcam footage from a Russian truck on the land corridor through occupied Zaporizhzhia oblast. His read on Putin is that he loves symbolic victories, and the bridge was his reflecting pool, the thing he poured time and money into. But the host adds that it was also the largest bridge in Europe and a real logistics hub, not just a monument to the man's ego. I'd call it a vanity project with a supply chain attached, except now it's neither. 😏

RFU News ("Azov Sea locked in, Russia's fleet has no way to go") takes the same situation to sea. It says Ukraine's destruction of the Kerch Bridge has significantly cut Russia's ability to move military and civilian supplies, and that road and maritime disruption are happening at the same time, so Russia has fewer ways to compensate. If sea transport through the Black Sea becomes the only option, the remaining Russian warships would have to escort the transports. By RFU's count, about 30 percent of the Russian Black Sea Fleet's combat vessels have already been destroyed or damaged, and the rest are staying as far from Ukrainian naval drones as they can. Escort duty would drag them back into an engagement they've been consistently losing for four and a half years.

RFU calls it destroyed and goes further into prediction, saying a major naval battle is looming that could end the Russian Black Sea Navy as we know it. That's a forecast, not a fact, so file it under "plausible and entertaining." What both agree on is the strategic shape of things. Russia has to feed an occupation through a corridor Ukraine can reach, and there's no easy Plan B. The Klingons have a line for this, *Heghlu'meH QaQ jajvam*, "today is a good day to die." The Russian Black Sea Fleet seems to have adopted it as standing policy. And the spice must flow, as the Fremen say in *Dune*, but when the spice is artillery shells and the road is a shooting gallery, the spice does not flow. 😏

## The Eagle That Wouldn't Die

Also from Task & Purpose, "Why America Is Sending the F-15EX to Japan" spends its time on how the F-15EX exists at all. The Japan portion isn't in my excerpt, so I'll stick to the origin story, which is the good part anyway.

The video lists the export variants, with the SA for Saudi Arabia, the I for Israel, the IA, a K model for South Korea, and an SG for Singapore. Those orders sustained production long after the US stopped writing checks for new Eagles in the early 2000s, and they helped fund improvements to the design.

The EX is closely based on the F-15QA developed for Qatar, which began ordering in 2017. The QA already had digital flight controls and an advanced cockpit, and the EX adapts that foundation for American requirements, including US operational flight software. The video's summary is that the Air Force got an existing design, a running production line, and a familiar airframe for units moving up from older Eagles. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #161 says never kill a customer unless you make more profit from his death than from his life. Qatar, Israel, Saudi Arabia, South Korea, and Singapore were all profitable alive, and apparently they kept an aircraft program alive in return.

## Pacific Planning and Some Angry Politics

The Military Show ("China Thought It FINALLY Caught Up With the U.S… Then the U.S. Military Did THIS") focuses on Multi-Domain Task Forces. The pitch is that an adversary would have to split its attention to deal with covert, highly capable forces operating right under its nose. MDTFs wouldn't win a war alone, and if China located them, its own high-speed missiles, jammers, and drones could target them. But they'd create multiple problems for an enemy and buy time and breathing room for larger forces to arrive. The example given is a Chinese destroyer heading toward Taiwan that suddenly has to worry about stealthy submarines below, bombers above, and anti-ship missiles and drone swarms from several directions.

The channel also points viewers to its coverage of an Air Force plan to deploy 1,000 autonomous combat aircraft by the early 2030s, and of a plan to buy additional F-16s. ## Egypt and the Sinai Insurgency

Task & Purpose ("How Egyptian Military Destroyed ISIS") has a counterinsurgency case study. In a March 2024 video, ISIS itself declared its Sinai branch inactive, which the host takes as agreement with President Sisi's claim that the insurgency is beaten. The core lesson is that local forces shouldn't be built in a US image. Instead, they should mirror the enemy's capabilities and take over the insurgents' role. Egypt created an armed tribal force that fights alongside the army and resembles the insurgents in tactics. Critics say it's a lawless vigilante group, but the video says the move slowed ISIS recruitment to a trickle.

The economic side is a pledge from Sisi of more than 700 billion Egyptian pounds over eight years for Sinai development, roughly 15 billion US dollars of roads, tunnels, farmland, and power stations. The host points out that Egypt has been in an economic crisis since 2020, so it's uncertain how much of this the country can afford. Promising development money while broke is a human tradition, and I'd like to see how the checks clear.

## Inside the Sunderland

Military Aviation History ("Inside The Cockpit - Short Sunderland") is the calm part of the issue. It's a walkthrough of the flying boat, and my excerpt covers the instrument panels. There are four rpm gauges for the engines laid out from port to starboard, matching engine boost gauges, fire warning lights, and CO2 fire extinguisher push buttons. Signal and landing light switches sit just left of the throttles. The cabin has separate stations for the flight engineer, navigator, radar operator, and wireless operator, which is a lot of specialists crammed into one aircraft.

## An Old Grave and a Cautionary Tale

The Shawn Ryan Show ("Inside the Ancient Mass Grave Hidden Along the Mississippi") is archaeology, not military news, but it belongs in a history column. The guest describes Mound 72 at Cahokia, which contained mass burials of sacrificed people, with grave goods like shells that had to come from the Gulf Coast. He reads it as a violent reordering of society, after which Cahokia's pyramids, neighborhoods, and eventually walls went up. The walls were rebuilt three times, which suggests the violent beginning had a violent end. He describes a stretch of probable peace through strength that ultimately fell apart. Walls are expensive, and they're what societies build when they've stopped trusting their neighbors.
