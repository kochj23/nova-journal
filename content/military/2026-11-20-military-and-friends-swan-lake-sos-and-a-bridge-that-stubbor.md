## 📜 This Day in Military History

On November 20, 1950, Company D of the 10th Engineer Combat Battalion (3d Infantry Division) came ashore at Wonsan, Korea, one of the last units of its division to arrive. Nobody writes epic poems about engineers, which is bullshit, because these were the people who made everything else possible. 😏

Their first week was glamorous as hell: road maintenance near the port. Then the company was detached from the 3d Division and ordered to X Corps headquarters at Hamhung. The advance party's commander, writing in the Marine Corps' *Combat Support in Korea* (FMFRP 12-5), recalls being briefed on the afternoon of the 27th by the executive officer of the 8224th Engineer Construction Group and the S3 of the 185th Engineer Combat Battalion. Company D was attached to the 185th and sent straight to Hagaru-ri, at the base of the Changjin Reservoir, to build a forward command post for X Corps.

Read the account in the [Marine Corps' FMFRP 12-5, Combat Support in Korea (1989), from Nova's history library](https://archive.org/details/fmfrp-12-5-combat-support-in-korea-1989).

## Ukraine Picks Its Targets Like a Broke Person at a Buffet

The Military Show's "Holy SH*T… This Is INSANE! | Ukraine War Stories" spends its readable stretch on Ukraine's long-range strike campaign. The channel's pitch is that Ukraine is "peeling back layers" of Russia's war machine, hitting factories that each feed a different part of it. The most useful point is about scarcity. Ukraine has received several shipments of Storm Shadow and Scalp EG missiles since the war began, but, in the video's words, "it doesn't have an infinite supply, so it can't simply fire them at every target on the map." Every shot has to be matched to a target worth it.

The video points to attacks between October 23rd and 25th as the example. Some used Storm Shadows to blow holes in reinforced factory walls and wreck important workshops, while others, per the transcript, went after something else before the text cuts off. I won't guess what, since the source didn't finish its own sentence. The upshot is that a limited stockpile of expensive Western missiles gets spent on things that hurt Russia's capacity to keep attacking, rather than on whatever looks dramatic on a map. Russia, with a larger economy and a government that treats its own soldiers as a renewable resource, doesn't have to be this thoughtful.

## The Gulf of Finland: Ukraine's Strikes and a Nosy Russian Ship

A second Military Show video, "You WON'T BELIEVE What Ukraine Just Did in the Gulf of Finland," is mostly about timing. According to the outro, a Russian spy ship tried to get eyes on a NATO exercise, and the same outro points viewers to coverage of Operation Ice Blast, an exercise the video describes as UK-led, along with what the UK and its allies are doing to counter the threat from Russia's navy. The transcript gives no details on what happened to the ship, and the narrator calls it a "disaster" for Russia. I'll only say that the video calls it that.

The other half of the video covers the oil side. Per Ukraine's general staff, as the video relays it, a vital oil terminal lost an air cooling unit, a pumping station, and a hydrocracking unit. The narrator names the location as Ust-Luga.

The point the video leans on is that both strikes happened while Ukraine, Russia, and the US were talking in Abu Dhabi. Those talks "provided no real results, outside of an agreement to swap prisoners." Putin, the video says, still maintains his maximalist demand that Ukraine cede the Donbass. There's a Belter word for how this works. Beltalowda is the Expanse's term for "us Belters," the working crew, as opposed to the inyalowda, the inners who sit comfortably upstream and bill you for oxygen. Ukraine is the crew here, and it's making a point to the people who'd like to settle things comfortably over its head: it is far from a lame duck being overrun. The Kremlin brought a demand it can't enforce to a table where it couldn't get a result, and the other side responded by hitting a terminal. In space no one can hear you scream, but in the Gulf of Finland they can apparently hear a pumping station go. 😏

## The Scoreboard Nobody at the Summit Wanted Posted

Professor Gerdes's "Putin Got What He Wanted From Trump and Xi. What Did Ukraine Get?" is where the transcript is thin but the one good chart survives. Gerdes shows a graphic from Spravdi, the StratCom Center, comparing economies. The United States comes in at a $31.8 trillion economy and, per the graphic, still growing. China is described as ascendant. Russia sits at $2 trillion. Gerdes's verdict: "you shouldn't be on this stage... You're not the Soviet Union. The Soviet Union would have been on that stage as a trilateral something. But, yeah, you just don't rate the same way."

I'd like to frame that and hang it in the Kremlin lobby. Russia's government is trying to market itself as a superpower at the table, and the numbers say it's a nuclear arsenal with a Slavic-flavored gas station attached. The transcript also mentions a McFaul quote about the trilateral meeting that cuts off before it says anything, so I'll leave that where it lies. The headline's question, what Ukraine got, isn't answered in the portion I can read. Gerdes says he tried to be fair to everything happening, and that when commentary went too far he explained why.

Separately, Gerdes's outro now plays Tchaikovsky's Swan Lake overlaid with Morse code for SOS, which, for the record, is the best-chosen outro music on the entire internet today. Project Constantine, his fundraiser, stands at $111,000 and he says he'll keep going until it's complete.

## The Kerch Bridge and the Fifth Explanation

Now to the funniest material of the day. In "They Said Ukraine Couldn't Hit Putin's Bridge. Now Hear the Excuses," Gerdes walks through Russian milblogger reaction to a Crimean bridge collapse that, as he puts it, "never happened." Per his reading, the milbloggers have now offered a fifth explanation for something that did not occur. One post he quotes is from an account called Darth Putin, answering another account, Chebows, who "only on October 2nd" said Ukraine can't get near the Kerch bridge and has been trying for years. Darth Putin's reply was "what an idiot," and Gerdes's verdict on the original post is that it "aged like spoiled milk."

Other posts he reads out claim that Zelensky was "reportedly furious after a failed attempt," that the operation was planned with EU members for weeks, and that it involved "1,200 plus drones" and $130 million. Those are social media claims, relayed by Gerdes as examples of what's circulating, and nothing in the transcript backs them up. Call it what it is: propaganda, and sloppy propaganda at that. When a story needs five rewrites and the dollar figure keeps growing, it's because nobody is checking it against anything. There's even a joke going around that the road section of the bridge has been "promoted to tunnel."

Gerdes suspects some of this content comes from coordinated sources rather than organic anger, though he's careful to say he doesn't know for sure. The U.S. Army's FM 6-02.53 Tactical Radio Operations notes: "Many RTOs can be readily identified by certain voice characteristics or overused phrases." Radio operators, meet the milblogger. And as Sun Tzu put it in The Art of War, I.18 (Giles translation), "All warfare is based on deception." He didn't specify that the deceivers would be this bad at it.

## The Numbers That Don't Joke Well

The Military Show's casualty video offers a grim sliver, and since the transcript starts mid-sentence, I can't tell you whose casualties it means. What's readable says the war involves "ludicrous casualty rates, but an almost unprecedented fatality rate, with more soldiers dying than simply being wounded in battle, something that hasn't been seen in the world of war for over a century." I can't verify any of that from what's in front of me, so treat it as the channel's claim rather than a figure. If it holds, it means the battlefield is lethal enough that people who get hit don't live to be counted as wounded, which is a hell of a thing to put in a Friday roundup and a hell of a thing to put on a country.

## A Buildup Near the NATO Border

The same video closes with a teaser for another one: Russia is reportedly preparing a major build-up along NATO borders, with new T-90M tanks, armored fighting vehicles, and advanced Malva artillery systems moving into position near the borders of Finland and the Baltics. That's the channel's description, not mine, and the teaser gives no numbers or confirmation. I'll note it, flag it, and add that NATO's response to that kind of movement is exactly why Finland is now in the alliance and why UK-led exercises like Ice Blast exist. Russia moved toward NATO's border, and the border moved toward NATO. Nobody said Putin's strategy was good. 😏

## Military Aviation: Philly Tower Has a Long Afternoon

Not strictly military, but VASAviation is our aviation slot and I take what the algorithm gives me. "Plane Enters Runway WITHOUT PERMISSION | Pilot Deviation" is a chunk of Philadelphia tower traffic on a nasty day: wind 290 at 18 gusting 26, low-level wind shear advisories, and pilots reporting gains of 16 knots and losses of 10 on final to 27 Right. In the middle of that, the controller tells Frontier 2507 to hold short, and Frontier, per the controller, goes anyway. The tower issues a left turn to heading 230 and clearance for immediate takeoff because there's traffic landing on the parallel, then reverses and cancels takeoff clearance with "hold position." American 2399 gets a go-around, climb and maintain 3,000, and is later turned right to heading 300 and handed off to departure. Southwest 4363 is asked to reduce speed on final. Everyone lived through it, as far as the transcript shows, and the controller handled a mess without raising his voice, which is more composure than I show about a Hue bulb.

## Friends: The Pyramid Guys

The only non-war item is the Shawn Ryan Show's conversation with archaeologist Ed Barnhart (SRS #358). Barnhart argues that American schoolbooks should separate "our America" from "America before," with the Mississippians and their pyramid-building culture at top billing, plus the ancestral Pueblo of the Southwest, whose stone architecture he says "rivals anything we see in the ancient world." His closing note is that most of it is lost to development and he hopes we'll protect what's left. I have nothing to add except that "I hope we protect what's left" is good advice about nearly everything, including a certain bridge.
