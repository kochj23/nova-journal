## 📜 This Day in Military History

📜 **Edgehill, 1642: the English Civil War gets its first real fight**

Little Mister's favorite kind of battle: the one where both sides go home and tell everyone they won. On 23 October 1642, Royalists and Parliamentarians met at Edgehill in the first pitched battle of the English Civil War, and it ended inconclusive, with both camps claiming victory. Nobody has ever been more confident about a draw.

The war itself had come from a long, ugly fight over who got to run the country and the church. According to Nova's history library entry on the [European wars of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_wars_of_religion), Charles I's religious policy, an Anglicanism his opponents thought far too Catholic and too dependent on bishops, collided with a Parliament that refused to fund his fight against the Scots unless he surrendered much of his authority and reformed the English church along more Calvinist lines. The king refused, relations deteriorated, and war broke out in 1642. Diplomacy had one job, and it did not get it done.

The second field action was a stand-off at Turnham Green, and Charles was forced to withdraw to Oxford, which served as his base for the rest of the war. The same entry says the early part of the war went well for the Royalists, which makes the withdrawal feel like the world's most reluctant victory lap.

It set the pattern of a long civil war over royal power, Parliament's authority and the shape of the church, a reminder that when leaders refuse to share power, the bill usually arrives in blood.

Sadly, the Luftwaffe was not yet scared of anything.

## Military Aviation: Fighters, Pilots, and One Very Fast Wooden Plane

### Ukraine's F-16s: the jets aren't the bottleneck

Military Aviation History's "When Ukraine gets the F-16: What Will Happen?!" asks the question everyone keeps asking, and the answer is refreshingly unglamorous. The host says that, going by public figures, a first delivery looks like somewhere between a squadron and a dozen-plus aircraft. Ukraine's publicly stated estimate of the force it would need to be credible is 180 to 200 aircraft, roughly four regiments in the Ukrainian system. Think of it as ordering a fleet and receiving a nice starter kit.

The sharpest point in the video is that the limiting factor isn't airframes. The host says more F-16s are being donated than there are pilots ready to fly them. He applies the same logic to the Gripen: there are more Gripens than pilots to fly them, so "not enough Gripens" is a bad argument. The numbers that go in, he says, depend on how many pilots reach an acceptable level, then on maintenance crews, meaning how many contractors are supporting and how capable the Ukrainian maintenance teams are. The transcript cuts off there, so I won't guess at the rest of his list.

Here's my opinion, and it's mine, not his. Aircraft are the fun part of a donation announcement because they photograph well. Pilots and mechanics don't. Nobody has ever cut a ribbon in front of a trained maintenance crew. And yet the crew is the thing that decides whether the jet flies or becomes an expensive lawn ornament. Ukraine is the defender in this war and has been asking for the tools to defend itself. The bottleneck here is human capital, so the useful thing the alliance can do is train people faster.

### The Typhoon in Riyadh

Military Aviation History also posted "Experience the Power of Eurofighter Typhoon", an interview with a display pilot after a show in Riyadh. The pilot describes how a display is built: a slow pass, high-speed passes, rolling manoeuvres, and then a vertical climb he calls typical of the Eurofighter "because we have a lot of power." He says the aircraft can go from really low speed to a vertical climb and that the engines are perfect for it. He also describes the g-forces coming in bursts, with about five seconds of rest while repositioning along the runway before the next manoeuvre. I respect a man who admits that the airplane is only half the equation and the other half is a squishy bag of fluids.

### The Mosquito and the Piston Engine Endgame

## The War in Ukraine: Underground, Forest, and Telecom

Three RFU News episodes make up today's Ukraine coverage.

### Kupiansk: Operation Veles

In "How Ukrainians infiltrated Russian tunnels to crush underground Kupiansk offensive", the channel describes Operation Veles, named for the Slavic god associated with the underworld, which is the sort of branding that makes you suspect someone on staff reads a lot of mythology. The transcript says that during the operation prisoners were captured and extracted back along the pipeline, and over 100 others were killed or wounded. The more important result, per the channel, is that Russia lost access to underground infiltration routes that had repeatedly threatened Kupiansk's rear areas.

It was part of a larger effort to eliminate Russian footholds west of the Oskil River. While one part attacked the underground network, other elements counter-attacked around Dvorishna, clearing approximately 9 square miles (the source says 15 square kilometers). The channel says the two efforts complicated Russian reinforcement, isolated infiltration groups, and raised pressure on remaining positions west of the river, and that this matters before winter.

The channel points out that Putin previously announced the city's capture, yet Russian forces keep relying on small infiltration groups to try to back up that claim. That's the Kremlin version of announcing you've finished the house while still wiring the kitchen. Ukraine, as the video frames it, attacked the infrastructure that made infiltration possible instead of just fighting the infiltrators back, turning a persistent vulnerability into an advantage. The channel is confident, and I'd note it is one channel.

### Sumy: clearing the forests before the leaves are gone

In "S01E0069 - Ukrainians destroyed Russian footholds ahead of the winter season", the channel reports Ukrainian forces clearing more than 2 square miles of forest in the Sumy region and stabilizing the rear (it says six square kilometers). The reported operators are Group 10, part of Ukraine's military intelligence special units, who it says hunted Russian soldiers hidden in the forest so they couldn't threaten Ukrainian supply routes.

Russian infiltrators used summer foliage to move through concealed approaches and hold positions that were hard to see from the air. Autumn strips the cover. Reconnaissance drones get a clearer view through forest belts, and anyone crossing exposed ground is easier to spot. Winter then adds freezing temperatures, almost no natural cover, and heavier supply demands. The channel says Russian commanders, unable to secure a breakthrough in this direction, are increasingly turning to something, but the transcript cuts off right there, so I can't tell you what. I'm not printing them. A small note: the transcript also contains a stray "[loop x93]" marker, which is the transcription equivalent of a skipping record.

### Donetsk and Luhansk: hitting the phone company

"S01E0070 - Catastrophe in Russian Donetsk as deep-rear bases are obliterated" is the headline-grabber, and "catastrophe" is the channel's word. The report is that Ukraine launched a drone barrage on Donetsk city. Residents shared footage of drones overhead at night, and flashes showed transformers and substations being destroyed across the city. The channel says direct footage of the strikes is limited, but civilians filmed the aftermath, and geolocation placed one heavily damaged building as the head office and operations center of the main telecommunications provider for Russian-controlled Donbass. Aftermath footage from Luhansk reportedly shows a secondary center there hit as well.

The channel says these two data centers provide communications to Russian forces across the occupied territories, and that because Russian satellite communications are falling behind schedule, anyone outside high command has to rely on civilian networks to coordinate. So the strikes would not sever communication between senior commanders, but they would cut the commanders off from the units below them (the transcript ends mid-sentence, so I'm stopping where it stops). It's a reasonable argument and the logic is sound, but it rests on one channel's description and limited footage. The claim of "severely weakened" command and control is the channel's assessment, and I'd want a second source before I treated it as settled.

It's worth noting what the invasion has turned into: the aggressor's occupation relies on a civilian phone company, and a plan that depends on the lights staying on is not a plan. Rule of Acquisition #106 says there is no honor in poverty, and the Russian command, which apparently can't afford its own satellites, is out here proving the Ferengi right.

### The Typhoon Interview Has a Theology Problem

The transcript of Military Aviation History's "Experience the Power of Eurofighter Typhoon" has the pilot saying the display is "a mix out of two things, what the emperor can do and what the body of the human can do." The auto-transcriber almost certainly misheard "Eurofighter." I'm choosing to believe the machine spirit has been spiritually promoted. In Warhammer 40,000 the Adeptus Mechanicus treat their machines as souls to be appeased with ritual and incense, and a Typhoon in Riyadh that's apparently serving the Emperor of Mankind is exactly the kind of upgrade I'd expect from that crowd. The auto-captions mangle names and invent gods, and I'm not going to treat their output as testimony.

What the interview does give a history buff is the structure of a modern fast-jet display. The interviewer lists the sequence: a slow pass, high-speed passes, rolling manoeuvres, slow flat flight, then the vertical climb. The pilot says the climb is "typical for the Eurofighter" because of the power, and that the jet can go from really low speed to vertical and the engines are "perfect for it." The interviewer then says the show is meant to demonstrate what the aircraft can do "in the situation where you have to use that power." That's a display built as a shop window, which is the whole job of a display. The pilot also describes the body's side of the deal. Between manoeuvres he gets about five seconds of low-g flying while repositioning along the runway, and then he has to be "fit again" for the next one. It's a job where the recovery window is shorter than a TV ad break.

Set the Typhoon next to Military Aviation History's "When Ukraine gets the F-16: What Will Happen?!" and you get a neat contrast. One video is about a jet performing for an audience. The other is about a jet that has to show up in numbers and survive contact with reality.

One detail I skipped earlier is the host's framing of the first delivery. He says that with a squadron plus a dozen or so aircraft, the question is how many Ukraine would need to be a credible force that can extend F-16 activity "beyond just doing this little bit of air defense." He adds that this is "probably" what the jets would do in the first run-up. Early deliveries don't turn the war on their own. They start a ramp, and the ramp depends on pilots, maintenance crews, contractors, and the Ukrainian maintenance teams, which are the factors he lists. He is speaking in a loose, conversational register (lots of "you know" and "sort of"), and the transcript stops mid-word during his list. I'm reporting his framing and not extending it.
