## 📜 This Day in Military History

**On this day in 1942, Dwight D. Eisenhower moved into a hole in a rock to run a war.**

On November 5, 1942, General Eisenhower arrived in Gibraltar to take command of Operation Torch, the first large-scale Anglo-American operation of the war. Churchill had also put the Rock itself under his military command. The plan was to strike from French colonies in North Africa (Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia) and drive Italy out of the war. Eisenhower had been appointed Allied Commander-in-Chief that July.

Eisenhower slept in comfort at The Convent, the governor's official residence, because even a war needs a decent mattress. His operational headquarters, though, was a small chamber in a tunnel in the heart of the Rock. So the man steering one of the biggest amphibious gambles of the war worked out of a glorified closet inside a limestone cliff, and I have seen Jordan's server rack arrangements that were more spacious. Not by much. Don't tell him.

It matters because Torch was the first real test of a British-American command structure. Two allies, one commander, one tunnel. That is the same basic idea NATO later made permanent, minus the tunnel and with far better coffee.

## Ukraine: Robots, Old Armor, and a Very Expensive Target

### Operation Vivaldi gets a robot upgrade

The Military Show, in "Something Just BROKE on Russia's Frontlines… Putin's Army Is DYING & Other War Stories," opens mid-thought on a detail worth noting. The third phase of Operation Vivaldi brought in kamikaze ground robots alongside modified Soviet-era armor. Ukraine is taking the Soviet hardware the invaders left lying around, bolting modern ideas onto it, and sending it back with a return address. The channel says it has a separate video on how Ukraine gets these robots deep behind enemy lines. The excerpt gives no more detail than that, and I'm not going to make any up. I'll note only that "kamikaze ground robot" is a phrase that would have sounded like a bad sci-fi pitch a decade ago, and now it's a line item.

### Robert Brovdy and the bounty

The Military Show also covers Robert Brovdy. His drone units, it says, haven't been as directly involved in Vivaldi as they've been elsewhere in Ukraine. His charisma is a morale booster, and the channel says his drone operators have scored 656 Russian casualties for every one of their own 79 members that Russia has managed to kill. Run that ratio past anyone who has ever managed an on-call rotation and they'll weep with envy. The channel calls the bounty "ridiculously large," though the excerpt doesn't give the figure, so neither will I.

The channel's read is that a bounty like that tells you Russia isn't heading for a ceasefire anytime soon. When a state puts a price on one drone commander, it's admitting that the commander's unit is hurting it more than any press release will confess. Memento mori, Moscow, which is Latin for "remember you will die." Remembering it hasn't made your targeting any better.

### Putin's theory of winning, again

The same video leans on the ISW, the Institute for the Study of War. According to that reporting, Putin acknowledged that Ukraine is pushing for a ceasefire, but he believes the reason is how effective Russia's long-range drones are. He thinks Russia can still collapse Ukraine's economic and production capacity and weaken Kyiv's hand in negotiations. The Military Show's verdict is dry: "not a bad concept, sure," but one Putin has held since 2024. Back then he believed even slow advances would pressure Ukraine into... and there the excerpt cuts off, mid-sentence, which is the most appropriate place to leave a Putin strategy.

My own gloss, clearly labeled as mine: a plan that has been "about to work" for two years is not a plan. It's a hobby. There's a word for a leader whose dashboards report doubleplusgood while the thing is face down in a ditch. That's Newspeak, Orwell's dialect built so the vocabulary shrinks until certain thoughts can't be assembled. The Kremlin has been fluent for years. The invasion was unprovoked, and Ukraine is the one defending itself. No amount of spin about drone effectiveness changes who started this.

## The Politics Around the War

### Professor Gerdes on the world's reaction to the US election

Professor Gerdes, in "The World Reacts to Trump's Election Defeat—And It's Revealing," spends his time on how foreign audiences and commenters are reacting to the American vote. I'm covering this only for what it means for Ukraine. The snippets I have are the tail end of his reading of comments. One viewer says the right move was to pressure Putin rather than Zelensky, and the video's own refrain is that Trump "must pressure Putin." Gerdes adds his own doubt in plain words: "I don't know that he's going to." He thinks Trump will "lose more interest than not."

Another commenter says they voted Republican for local and state races and Democrat for federal ones, and Gerdes notes he heard that from a lot of people. A third hopes that if Republicans lose badly, the remaining ones "may finally grow a spine." Gerdes himself hopes "the spell" might be broken, like something out of a fairy tale.

I'd treat all of that as audience sentiment and one analyst's hopes, not as a forecast. The excerpt doesn't contain vote counts, so I won't invent any. What's solid is the framing: Gerdes, like most Ukraine watchers, is asking whether Washington will keep pressure on the aggressor or lean on the victim. That's the question that matters for this war, and a Congress of any color should be able to answer it without a focus group. Gerdes also promises a follow-up on the American reaction, what may happen to Hegseth and Rubio, and who the right's next presidential candidates might be.
