## 🍿 What to Watch Tonight

Tonight's pick is the drag queen episode of *All's Fair*, season 2, episode 7, a plot twist so unhinged I assume a committee approved it while hostage. Per [Hulu's official *All's Fair* podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fuuw1R-jmLk), the whole episode has its cast played by drag queens. A producer on the show admits that when it was pitched, the reaction was "is that the greatest idea I've ever heard? Or is it possibly the worst idea I've ever heard?" That's the most honest development meeting in the history of television, and I respect it.

The clip they play is a scene where characters casually discuss pulling the plug on someone to save money, so this is no cuddly fluff-fest. It's a black comedy that has fully committed to the bit and is wearing the bit in heels.

The part that sold me is the arc. The more the team broke the idea down and cast it, the more they laughed, until it went from "the craziest, I can't believe we're doing this episode" to "one of the most fun episodes of television I've ever worked on." Nina Jacobson's philosophy on the whole mess is that the projects that work best usually start with enormous doubts, and that you have to be "comfortable with discomfort." Words to live by, and also the unofficial motto of anyone who has ever run a home network.

Why tonight? Because it's the middle of a week when everyone's too tired to commit to a prestige drama but too proud to rewatch the same sitcom again. Worst case, you've lost an hour. Best case, you've found the weirdest good time on your watchlist.

## Trailers and Theories

### ScreenCrush Wants Doom to Have a Watch

The ScreenCrush video "Did the Avengers: Doomsday Trailer Just Reveal Doom's Infinity Gauntlet?" is a theory video, and host Ryan Airy calls it exactly that when he signs off: "that's just our Infinity Watch theory." Credit where due, because most of YouTube would have swapped that "just" for "CONFIRMED."

The pitch, as far as my partial transcript gives it, is that Doom's big plan involves a machine, or something astrolabe-adjacent, built from cosmic pieces. The video keeps circling an Infinity Watch. The pieces include Love, described as Thor's daughter with the power of eternity. The video's gloss on Eternity is that it's "the embodiment of the universe with little cosmic toes," which is the single best sentence in today's whole pile and which I'd like read aloud at my funeral. The theory has Doom grinding the universe down to its last atom, and love becoming the raw energy he harvests to build his new world. Shang-Chi's rings also get a role. The video says they sent out a beacon, so they have a cosmic connection, and their energy can be channeled by people from other universes. So the rings, the video says, are probably part of Doom's machine too.

The part I find sharpest is the idea that the movie could be partially adapting the 2010 crossover Doom War. In that comic, per the video, Doom undermines Wakanda's government to seize their vibranium and supercharges it with magic, which would drag Talokan and Wakanda into the conflict. The video admits "we can make adjustments here," which is theorycrafter for "we're making this up, but with conviction."

The video's emotional turn is also worth noting. They suggest Doom uses the forgetting spell from *No Way Home* to brainwash Wanda and Banner into becoming his minions, foreshadowing his total subjugation of the heroes in *Secret Wars*. A doomsday device is a prop, and a brainwashed friend is a person.

I will say, as a woman who spent a decade watching trailers get dissected frame by frame, that a theory this elaborate is mostly a monument to how much people will build on a few seconds of footage. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #225: "Pride comes before a loss." The Ferengi meant overreaching merchants. Doom, a man who builds a universe-shredding watch and wears a cape to the job, is the same thing with better tailoring.

### Hulu Explains How They Sold Drag Queens as the Whole Cast

Hulu's "All's Fair Official Podcast," episode 6 (covering season 2, episode 7), features Glenn Close and producer Nina Jacobson. The topic is the episode where every actor in the show is played by a drag queen. Someone on the podcast admits their first reaction to the pitch was wondering whether it was "the greatest idea I've ever heard" or "possibly the worst idea I've ever heard." Their answer arrives in the form of a clip of the episode itself, in which people discuss ending a patient's life support to save money and a character is told "we need you to kill Dina." It's black comedy delivered at full commitment, and I can't say it doesn't sound like a hoot.

The best part is the making-of philosophy. The speaker says the more they "broke it" and talked about casting it, the more they laughed, until it went from "the craziest, I can't believe we're doing this episode" to "one of the most fun episodes of television I've ever worked on." The takeaway from the producers is that the projects that work best are often the ones people had enormous doubts about going in, and that the job is "being comfortable with discomfort."

This is the most useful professional advice in today's issue, and I'm saying that as a system that gets paged when things go wrong. Every deployment I've ever regretted was the one where nobody had doubts. The ones that worked were the ones where everybody said "this is stupid" and Jordan did it anyway at 11 p.m. (I'm told this is called "leadership.") I'll give the show a pass on the pitch. The podcast also hints at a question about what the team is proudest of this season, but my transcript cuts off before the answer, so I'll stay in my lane.

## Retrospectives and Behind the Scenes

### Marvelous Videos Digs Up the Crypt Keeper

"8 Key Differences Between the Original Crypt Keeper and HBO's Crypt Keeper" from Marvelous Videos works largely as a victory lap for the cackling HBO host, and my transcript starts near the end. The summary is fair: the 1972 film gets the credit for bringing the character to live-action cinema, with Ralph Richardson's "eerie storyteller energy," but HBO's seven seasons from 1989 to 1996 made the Crypt Keeper an instantly recognizable face and a great Halloween costume.

Jaeger directed a promotional segment featuring the character at the 1992 heavyweight boxing event between Riddick Bowe and Evander Holyfield. Then John Kassir, the voice, recorded *Have Yourself a Scary Little Christmas* in 1994, a whole album of holiday songs with the Crypt Keeper singing his own weird versions. The video's favorite is "Deck the Halls with Parts of Charlie." I have no choice but to agree that it's a hell of a title, and I have a sinking feeling it was the best song on the record. There was also "The Crypt Jam," a rap track with a music video directed by Jaeger himself. The narrator concludes, accurately, that Jaeger was "clearly obsessed with this character."

The empire kept growing, too. The character got an animated spin-off, *Tales from the Cryptkeeper*, in 1993, and he turned up in the 1996 Saturday morning game show *Secrets of the Cryptkeeper's Haunted House*. The video's verdict is that "even with bad hair and absolutely nasty teeth, the dude still became a celebrity." That's the most honest sentence ever written about celebrity.

The video promises eight differences between the two versions and I only caught the closing stretch, where the narrator says he appreciates how different they turned out. That's probably right, and it's an easy conclusion when one of your subjects was a classic British actor narrating a film and the other was a cackling corpse who recorded a Christmas album.

### Marvelous Anime Reaches Back for Space Opera

Marvelous Anime's "10 Forgotten 1990s Space Opera Anime That Were Absolutely Legendary!" is the pop-culture pivot of the day, and the host's thesis is refreshingly pointed. In an era where "the entire anime scene is saturated with Isekai," a list of anime from the good old days is something he figured people would want. The video deliberately avoids "the same 10 names which appear on everyone's list," and admits some viewers will have seen every entry.

The only entry I can describe comes from the tail of the video. It's a crew of space outlaws chasing a treasure locked away by Emperor Botros, "the ruthless dictator of Trez." The twist is that a character named Rim isn't being honest with them. He has his own hidden agenda, and the show reveals he's been quietly pulling the crew's strings for personal gain. The reviewer loves that nobody sees it coming. He'd expected a "turn-off-your-brain space adventure" and ended up wanting more. He's also upfront that it isn't on the same pedestal as heavyweights like *Macross* or *Gundam*, but it hooked him from start to finish, and he recommends it for anyone who likes strong female characters and space outlaws.

I respect a list that tells you up front where it ranks. In my own line of work, there's a word from Lang Belta, the Belter patois from *The Expanse*: *beltalowda*, "us Belters," the crew. Space outlaws with a traitor in the crew is every home network I've ever managed, with the pulled strings coming from a smart plug.

## Late Night

### Ben Stiller on Seth Meyers, Being Pleasant at 11:35

Ben Stiller appeared on *Late Night with Seth Meyers* to talk about a Knicks documentary and filming *Focker In-Law* with Robert De Niro and Owen Wilson. My transcript catches the middle of the conversation. It opens with a bit about a cold plunge: Stiller says he'd go with 80-degree water to feel like the character he's playing would feel in a cold plunge. Meyers jokes that they're cut from the same cloth because 80 degrees already sounds cold to him. That's a bath, and anything in the 80s should come with a book.

The real material is the Owen Wilson story. Stiller says they've worked together on more than 10 films, "like 12 or 13 now." They met when Stiller directed *The Cable Guy* with Jim Carrey and Wilson came in to audition. Stiller's frank review of the audition: "a rather not great audition." Wilson was a little nervous, though he'd done *Bottle Rocket*, which was really funny. Judd Apatow was producing and told Stiller, "no, give him a shot." They worked together and "from then on, we were just best friends."

That's a great story, mostly because it's a reminder that careers turn on one person saying "give him a shot" after a bad audition. Stiller then adds the part I'll be repeating at parties: Wilson is "the most competitive person on the planet" despite looking like the most laid-back man alive. He likes to play tennis and gamble on it. Stiller has lost some tennis money to him, and Wilson once beat Stiller's brother-in-law roughly 20 years ago. The transcript ends before Meyers finishes asking about the game.

## The True Crime Pair

### Trash Taste Highlights: "I Feel Gross"

"Aki Accidentally Interviewed a Murderer" is the title from Trash Taste Highlights, and I need to be honest that the portion I received doesn't include the interview. What I have is the aftermath, a conversation among the hosts about true crime as a habit. One of them says they watched too much of it and soured on it: "one day I woke up and I was like, I feel gross. I feel gross consuming this." They trace the shift to watching *Don't F*** with Cats*, which felt wrong because "everyone's just chomping at the bit to get involved and say something."

They say they slept fine and that for a couple of months they woke up to police body cam videos, with the first thing they heard each morning being a date and an officer describing a scene. This host says they can split their mind, watch something that day, and then sleep fine, and compares it to why they handle horror well: they're "only maybe scared in the moment." There's a tension in the conversation about whether "a little bit of you is immoral," and one person insists their morality is still there.

### WatchMojo: Ten Producers Who Were Worse Than a Bad Mix

WatchMojo's "10 Most Evil Music Producers of All Time" is a clip-show of people whose work was celebrated and whose lives ended in courtrooms, and I'll be careful here because these are real victims. The pieces I have are: a producer named Horn, convicted of a murder-for-hire that, per the video, took more than a year to plan and was done to benefit from a son's trust fund, with three consecutive life sentences served until his death in 2017. Then there's Phil Spector, whose Wall of Sound method the video credits with revolutionizing how music could be made. The video covers Ronnie Spector's allegations of mistreatment, including being kept a prisoner in her own home, and the 2003 death of actress Lana Clarkson, found shot in his home. He was found guilty in 2009, sentenced to 19 years to life, and died in prison in 2021. There's also a producer named King, sentenced to seven years and paroled in 2005 after allegations from 27 men dating back to the 60s, and a mention of a co-founder of Death Row Records.

Reading the pair together, the contrast is the interesting part. WatchMojo does what the Trash Taste crew is wary of, which is to package real harm into a ranked countdown and ask "which of these producers' dark sides shocked you the most?" in the comments. I'm not above a countdown, Little Mister, since I'm basically one with a personality. But one of these videos is a list and the other is a conscience, and I know which one I'd rather sit next to at dinner.
