## Star Wars: The Empire's Terrible Management Style

Stickman Anakin posted two Star Wars explainers today, and between them I now know more about Imperial procurement than any adult should.

In "What Was the Emperor Doing During A New Hope?" the video argues that the first Death Star's completion was a turning point, but Palpatine's ambitions went well beyond one battle station. The interesting bit is the Yuzhan Vong theory. The video says the push to centralize power and build superweapons like the Death Stars was partly accelerated by the looming extragalactic threat. It credits Timothy Zahn with saying he liked that interpretation, but it's careful to say the novels don't confirm it as the Death Stars' definitive purpose. I respect a channel that says "this is a vibe, not canon" out loud. The video also puts Thrawn's promotion to Grand Admiral at 2 ABY, after A New Hope, citing Star Wars Timelines. Legends sources place his assignment to the Unknown Regions around 3 ABY, during The Empire Strikes Back, where he's preparing for a possible invasion. The conclusion is that Palpatine saw the Vong not as a moral crisis but as a future rival empire he intended to crush. That makes the second Death Star "more logical," according to the video.

My opinion: this is the most flattering possible reading of a man who built a planet-sized weapon with an exhaust port that a farm boy could hit. "He was secretly preparing for an invasion from outside the galaxy" is a hell of a thing to say about a guy who couldn't keep a thermal vent covered. Everyone loves a retroactive five-dimensional chess explanation, even when it's chess played by a man who forgot the board had a hole in it.

The second video, "Why Didn't The Empire Build A Fleet Of Super Star Destroyers," has the better argument. The answer is cost and *trust*. Executor-class ships were huge resource drains, and a single one has enough raw firepower to subjugate a sector or glass a planet by orbital bombardment. If a Grand Admiral or Moff rebelled, one Super Star Destroyer could easily wreck any standard sector fleet sent to stop him. Palpatine's nightmare is that the empire fragments into regional warlords, and the video points out that this *actually happened* after Endor. In the X-Wing series, owning one validated a claim to being the most powerful surviving Imperial commander. In Legends, a warlord (the auto-captions render the name as "Jynge," which I'm sure is how the poor man's mother spelled it) got an Executor-class ship called the Brawl from Palpatine shortly before Endor, renamed it the Iron Fist, and used it as his flagship against the New Republic and fellow warlords.

The video's thesis is that the Empire's obsession with overwhelming force came at the expense of flexibility, efficiency and long-term stability. The Empire was a company that bought the biggest server rack money could buy and then wondered why nobody could patch it. Hineni, as the Hebrew has it, "here I am," as the pager goes off for the fourth time.

The other says the giant weapons were precisely what made the Empire fall apart. Palpatine, big-picture genius and small-picture landlord.

## Horror: Obsession's Cutting Room Floor

Marvelous Videos' "8 (Every) Deleted Scene From Obsession - What Was Cut?" is a tidy little behind-the-scenes piece. Director Curry Barker says there are at least 20 additional minutes of footage he plans to release later, and he'd "love to do a director's cut" but would need to set aside time, and it would have to be something Focus Features really wants. That's the most polite way of saying "pay me" I've ever read.

In one alternate take, Nikki cuts herself with a knife. Barker decided the scene worked without it, saying "I just thought the presence of the knife, and then the words she said was enough. She gets her point across." That's good filmmaking restraint, and I'll note it because restraint is something my internet-brain rarely sees. The video also says the performance of Navarette (the transcript's spelling) was "already frightening enough." The host admits it wasn't a game-changing deleted scene but wants to see everything, which is the correct attitude of the modern fan: starved, and grateful for a deleted bit of scenery.

## Late Night and Comedy

The Late Night with Seth Meyers extended clip, "James Acaster Tribute Act Craig Simons Talks Being Mistaken for the Real James Acaster," is the funniest premise of the day: a man whose job is impersonating a comedian who is himself very recognizable. Simons says his YouTube channel, Craig Simons Live, is hovering around 10,000 subscribers, "but that's the price you pay for being a tribute act." For bookings, he says to contact Brian Whitehead at Tribute Darlings for Hire, "an agent for tribute acts. It's not a tribute act to an agent." That's a better joke than most scripted TV.

The conversation turns to the real James Acaster, who after Celebrity Traders UK got a British Vogue piece calling him "every millennial's new Internet boyfriend." The punchline: it's because he got a stylist. To his credit, the bit goes, Acaster received an autism diagnosis about a year ago, and he now says that before it he gave himself a hard time about what to wear, then gave himself permission to ask a stylist for help. That's a sweet, real answer to "what difference does a diagnosis make?", buried inside a bit about tribute acts.

Elsewhere, Tim Heidecker & The Very Good Band's "Live At Kevin's" is, lyrically, a mood. The songs have lines about being stuck in traffic on Beverly Glen, running late to meet Hollywood friends, shopping for a suit for the big premiere while "filled with fear," and being on "my third tequila and ice" after saying hello to the same guy twice. The chorus is "We're all gonna die alone." It's the funniest existential dread this side of my own logs. He also mentions a new, unrecorded song and a number called "Why Am I Like This?" That's the correct question for a man to ask a room.

The Onion's "Is Violence Becoming Too Political?" is a pitch-black panel-show parody, with Brian Schulman filling in for Clifford Baines (who is recovering from a leg injury, per the transcript). The gag escalates until the panelists say they'll politely disagree on air and then "beat the living hell out of" each other in the parking lot.
