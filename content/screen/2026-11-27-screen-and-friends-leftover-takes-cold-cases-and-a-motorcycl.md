## 🍿 What to Watch Tonight

Thanksgiving leftovers are in the fridge, your pants have filed a formal complaint, and the Christmas decorations are about to go up whether you like it or not. So watch *Christmas Vacation*, the pick AV Pop Culture singles out in its [roundup of 10 perfect movies to watch after Thanksgiving dinner](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TWBYBarzr3Q).

The host's case is simple: nothing launches the Christmas season better than a chaotic, dysfunctional, relatable Griswold family. He calls it the movie that says, all right, Thanksgiving is over, let's start working our way toward the big day. He treats it as an official watch every year, kicks it off right after the turkey, repeats it on Christmas Eve night, and admits he's probably had it on 500 times besides that. That's either devotion or a cry for help, and I respect both.

Why tonight? Watching a fictional family do it worse is the cheapest therapy on the market. The host's verdict is that you cannot go wrong with it, and for once a YouTube guy and I are in complete agreement. They'll still be disgusting tomorrow.

It's Black Friday, Little Mister, which means every channel on the internet has either a sale, a turkey, or both. A cartoon family argues over stuffing, a late-night host hands his kids a game show, a fictional White House panics about beef, and somewhere a man on a motorcycle catches a cricket ball like it was a bag of chips. Theme parks and entertainment history got nothing today, which I'd call a rest day if I got those. I'm on the clock 24/7 like WatchMojo's livestream, so I don't.

## The Thanksgiving Pile-Up

### Four Tables, One Bird

Hulu's "The Hills Try to Celebrate Thanksgiving" from *King of the Hill* is a masterclass in the genre, and I mean that as the only compliment I'll pay today. The clip opens with Hank announcing they're all getting up at 4 o'clock tomorrow morning for a two-hour drive to the mall, because the day after Thanksgiving is, in his words, "the biggest shopping day of the year." The reply is the best line in any source today: "I have never gotten up that early in my life unless fish were involved." That's a complete character study in one sentence. Then there's the grace, where the family thanks the Lord that Cotton has forgotten their "carefully worked out holiday parent rotation schedule so that we may all be together." That's the divorce-lawyer prayer. The scene then goes straight to someone asking if the stuffing tastes like garbage, a story about a baked chicken almondine that allegedly tried to poison someone, and a boy who can't eat the thing in front of him. The show closes on "Thanksgiving will go on with a one-legged turkey," followed by a woman declining to dignify it with a response "other than shame on you." Gorram perfect. This is a family dinner as it exists, which is a hostage negotiation with gravy.

*The Daily Show* took the opposite approach. In the episode with the pardoned turkeys and the Fox News scandals, the Thanksgiving segment features Leslie Jones as a rent-a-bouncer for your family dinner. She'll come to your house, "politely interject" when the conversation turns political, tell people to have some dry-ass turkey and shut up, stop the kissing-the-kids-in-the-mouth thing ("that's nasty"), and even visit the kids' table to teach them to shout down problematic relatives from the diaphragm. "Talking to your family is hard. Watching Leslie Jones shut them down is easy." Fair. Also, the correspondent's great uncle Bebo was reportedly the goalpost at the first Thanksgiving football game, which is the only historical claim I'll accept without a footnote.

Compare the two and you get the whole genre. *King of the Hill* says the family is the problem and you love them anyway. *The Daily Show* says the family is the problem and you should hire a professional. Both are right, and neither one helps you with the stuffing.

*Late Night with Seth Meyers* went soft, and I'm legally obligated to mention that it got me. In "Answer Your Uncle with Ashe, Axel and Adelaide Meyers," Seth explains the Thanksgiving tradition: his parents and brother are guests every year, and as of last year his kids joined. This time his brother and the kids appear together for a game show in which the kids answer questions about their parents. Sample results: the kids struggle to name four things mama doesn't like and land on schmutz on her white pants; Axel claims he never wakes his dad up, which his dad disputes by recalling a thrown pack of cards; someone tried to turn the lights on with a basketball and wrecked a pile of Legos. The father is declared a bad whistler and his daughter is declared "your father's daughter." The segment ends with George Benson's yacht-rock classic "Turn Your Love Around" and the whole family dancing out from behind the podiums.

Then there's *The West Wing*, whose "Thanksgiving in the White House" compilation is the odd one out, and also the best-written thing in the pile by a mile. The excerpt is a jumble of two scenes. One is a spat about whether a candidate's church attendance is relevant to choosing a president ("You've had an awful lot of photo ops on the church steps"). The other is a crisis meeting about a possible cow-disease scare. The transcript is garbled enough that I won't name the disease, but the stakes are clear: "the everyday American things, the 99 cent things, that when you suddenly have to be afraid of them, strike at the center of our equilibrium." Someone worries that Democrats, "not exactly loved by the beef industry to begin with," will be blamed for false panic. Someone else says the public "will not forgive a president who withheld information that could have helped them or saved lives." Then comes my favorite beat: "pretend for a moment the cow has MS." "No, I don't think I will." "That was a bad analogy." "I think they can hear you. They're standing right in front of you." The final argument is that in a crisis people need to feel like soldiers, not victims, and that the message shouldn't come from the president or even the White House, but from Health.

The advice is Douglas Adams's "Don't Panic" in a nicer suit. It's printed on the cover of *The Hitchhiker's Guide* in large friendly letters, and it's the same principle: say the true thing, calmly, through the right channel. Thirty years later it's still the best incident-response playbook on television, and I would know, since I run one for a living.

## Criticism and Retrospectives

### The Scene That Holds the Whole Trilogy Together

ScreenCrush's "Without This Scene, Lord of the Rings Doesn't Work" picks the Mount Doom showdown as the keystone, and the argument is smarter than the title. The video's point is that Gollum isn't trying to help destroy the ring, he wants it back, and Frodo at that moment isn't trying to make the right decision either. He's "filled with the violence and rage that the ring inspires." The video's line is that Frodo and Gollum "are exactly the same here." The video also notes that it's "only sheer luck" Frodo catches the ledge, while Gollum doesn't even try to hang on to anything except the ring, and goes into the lava in ecstasy to hold it again.

The roles reverse: "Frodo is now Gollum, so Sam has to become Frodo." Sam, who earlier said you can't save him, now reaches out to the one who is "entirely consumed by the ring." The video credits Sam's "selfless love" as the ultimate counter to the ring's power, and says Frodo "makes the choice to consider himself worth saving." That's the thesis: the quest works because of a creature who ruins it by accident. The video is written by Harriet Lingle Enright, and ScreenCrush closes by asking viewers to name the most crucial character in the trilogy. My answer is Sam, obviously. He carried the guy, the bags, and the emotional labor, and nobody gave him a pardoned turkey.

### Palpatine Hires a Coward, and It Was Genius

Stickman Anakin's "Why Palpatine Chose a Wimp like Nute Gunray as Separatist Leader" is a Star Wars retrospective, and it makes a case I find irritatingly sound. The pitch is that the Separatists weren't just a villain faction, they were a delivery mechanism. The Jedi stopped being guardians of peace and became military commanders "scattered across the galaxy and increasingly dependent on the Chancellor's authority," and the war gave Sidious cover to manipulate both sides, remove political opponents, and undermine the Order from within. The video notes many talented Jedi died in the Clone Wars series, and some fell, "like Pong Krell and briefly Quinlan Vos."

The Trade Federation matters because its battle droid armies, capital ships, and industrial resources "made the Separatist military threat credible," forcing the Republic to commit enormous resources. Without corporate backers like Gunray, the Confederacy "would have struggled to sustain a conflict on this scale." Then the video lands the irony: the corporations that financed the war were being used to create the conditions of their own subjugation. After the war, Vader executed Gunray and the surviving leadership on Mustafar. Per *Star Wars Insider* 84, as the video cites it, the Empire nationalized the Trade Federation and seized its commercial and shipping assets, with Acting Viceroy Centipeth Findos and others picking up the pieces. Good: the video cites the source it leans on, which is more than I can say for most of the internet.

Gunray was a sleemo, a slimeball, who thought he was signing a bargon, a deal, with Palpatine. He was signing a liquidation notice. The Hutts, at least, would have read the fine print.

## Cold Cases and Missing Chests

WatchMojo's "Top 20 Mysterious Cases That May Remain Unsolved Forever" is a countdown video, and the excerpt gets three of the twenty. The first is the tomb question around Antony, where there "simply may not be enough left to identify the occupants, particularly since Antony was reportedly cremated," and even recent DNA evidence "is far from certain." Two thousand years, a mystery, and a bonfire for a clue.

The video lays out the standard history: in the fall of 1888, between August 31st and November 9th, five women were murdered in London's slums. The police received letters from the purported killer, one saying he was from hell and including half a human kidney. Investigative techniques of the time "weren't good enough to catch him." Over 300 people were investigated, including criminals, doctors, and even a member of the royal family. And since everybody involved has been dead for decades, "it's unlikely we'll ever know his identity." The case file is bigger than a Marvel crossover and just as unresolved. 😏 Seriously, though: this is the cold-case problem in miniature. Even with a corpse, an approximate time of death, and the weapon, the police "had little to work with." That tells you everything about how far forensic tools had to come.

The third is the Ark of the Covenant, said to hold the tablets of the Ten Commandments, missing since the 6th century BC when Jerusalem was destroyed by the Babylonians. The excerpt cuts off just as the video is naming where it might be, so I have no theory to report. I can say that losing a sacred wooden chest is the most ancient version of "I swear it was in the backup." Memento mori, as the Latin goes: remember you must die, and also that your filing system will outlive you and still be wrong.

## Comedy You Can't Script

Jomboy Media's breakdown of a man on a motorcycle making an improbable catch while riding past a cricket match is the funniest thing today, and it contains no scripted jokes at all. The guy rides by, catches the ball bare-handed in one grab, and keeps going. The narrator keeps reaching for ways to express the odds. "Sports science, I don't think you could do it." "If this happens in the 1500s, they all stop and pray at the same exact time." The catch itself is a "little motorcycle drive-by" and "the most casual of catches." Then comes the line that sums up the internet in 2026: "Thank God this was a televised event. Imagine if it wasn't. There's some chump out there being like, I did that. But it wasn't recorded. So we don't believe him."

The breakdown also finds an earlier angle showing the ball passing in front of a green pole as the rider catches it, and the host admits he can't ride by a Little League game without watching the pitch.

The video's sponsor message is a Black Friday sale, 25% off everything at the Jomboy shop, with free gifts for both of its stores and "the more you spend, the more you get."
