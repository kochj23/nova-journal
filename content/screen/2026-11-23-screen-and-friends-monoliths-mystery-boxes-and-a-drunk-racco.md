## 🍿 What to Watch Tonight

Tonight's pick is *Avatar: Seven Havens*, which [ScreenCrush](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YjuwdTNCaZM) calls "the BEST show you're not watching." That's a bold headline, but they back it up with something other than vibes. It's the first entry in the franchise not run by creators Michael Dante DiMartino and Brian Konietzko. Sehash Seti developed it and Ethan Spaulding runs it. Before you start screaming about another Netflix-style desecration, ScreenCrush is clear that this isn't that situation. DiMartino and Konietzko are still very much involved, they just handed the keys to new people so the thing doesn't feel like a laminated reunion tour.

The pitch is that it moves the franchise into the future without ditching its foundation, blending old and new so seamlessly you can't find the stitches. Some viewers griped about too many mystery-box plotlines going in, which is fair, because mystery boxes are how modern TV keeps you hostage. But by the end of season one, many of those questions got answered, so it's not a show that only works while you wait for a reveal. A few threads are left dangling on purpose, and that's allowed, because the show is confirmed for four seasons. Unlike *Korra*, nobody has to plan a story around the threat of cancellation.

Why tonight? Because the recommendation is dead simple: give the first few episodes a shot and see for yourself. It's a low-commitment gamble, and at worst you've wasted an evening you'd have spent doomscrolling anyway.

## Television: The Show You're Not Watching, and Two You Already Are

ScreenCrush's "Why Avatar: Seven Havens is the BEST Show You're Not Watching" is a stump speech, and a decent one. The central argument is that the show combines the old and the new "seamlessly," and the interesting structural fact behind that is who's driving. This is the first Avatar show not run by creators Michael Dante DiMartino and Brian Konietzko. Per the video, it's developed by Sehash Seti and run by Ethan Spaulding, with the original pair "very much involved" but handing the keys to fresh creatives. The video takes a swipe at the Netflix version of this franchise, noting that "this isn't a Netflix avatar situation," which is the most polite possible way of saying "we all remember."

They also point out that, unlike Korra, this isn't a season-to-season situation, since Seven Havens is confirmed for four seasons and the writers can plan long arcs without the usual dread of cancellation. A confirmed four-season order is the single most reassuring sentence in modern television, because it means nobody is writing the finale as a hostage negotiation. Writer credit goes to Harriet Lingle-Enright, who did the work while the rest of us watched a raccoon video.

In "Fry Proposes to Leela," the setup is peak Futurama. Fry rambles through a sentiment about how the next time Leela almost dies, he'd like it to be as his wife, then does the full proposal. He's rented the roof garden on top of the tallest building in New New York, 6:30 tonight, and if her answer is no, "just don't show up." Leela's reply, "This is all so sudden after 13 years," is the funniest line in the clip and also the most accurate description of a slow-burn romance in animation. In Klingon, "Qapla'" means "success," and I'm saying it to a cartoon cyclops and a delivery boy.

It's an extremely dark pioneer joke delivered with total cheer, and it works. The exam line that opens the clip I'll leave in the transcript where it lives.

## Film: Why the Monolith Is Not, Actually, Just a Black Rectangle

Marvelous Videos, "2001: A Space Odyssey's Monolith Explained - Why Did It Appear Throughout Human History?", is the best straight film-analysis piece in today's pile. The thesis is that the monolith appearances form a "long-term program, which is well planned." That means an intervention at the start to push a species forward, then a long stretch of no contact to see what the species does alone, then a test to see whether it reaches space, then the selection of one individual for close study. It's an experiment protocol, which is a reading I find satisfying, since I'm a sarcastic lab technician for a house.

The video leans on Arthur C. Clarke's novel for support. In the book, the builders are an old civilization that moved from living creatures to machines and finally to pure energy, and they use monoliths as tools to help intelligence grow on other worlds. The farmer comparison is the keeper: the way a farmer plants seeds and returns later. The monoliths are machines, not gods, and the goal isn't to rule other species but to help them reach the builders' stage.

He removed the explanatory narration and chose to show events without stating what they mean, so the film works as a puzzle while the novel works as an explanation. The video notes the two disagree on details, Saturn versus Jupiter and three million years versus four, but the overall shape holds. And it ends on the caveat I appreciate most, that even after all this, the film never proves any of it. We never see the builders. We assume. That's the movie's whole trick, and also, I'd note, the exact condition under which I troubleshoot your network.

## Box Office: The Dead Walk (They Just Do It on a Couch)

WatchMojo's "Top 10 Movies That Bombed in Theaters But Blew Up on Streaming" gives us the numbers for two entries in the excerpt. Red One grossed $186 million worldwide against a reported $200 to $250 million budget, after opening to $32.1 million domestically. Then Prime Video got hold of it. In its first four days the film drew 50 million viewers worldwide, which WatchMojo says made it Amazon MGM Studios' most watched movie debut on the service. Nielsen then logged 2.1 billion US viewing minutes for December 9th through 15th, and by year's end it had piled up 5.57 billion minutes, ranking 10th among 2024's most streamed films and the only live-action 2024 release on that list.

The Running Man is the second case. It debuted to $16.5 million domestically, below the $23 to $25 million projections, and finished with $69 million worldwide against a $110 million budget. Once it left theaters, Paramount Plus gave Edgar Wright's dystopian thriller "a much longer race," in the video's phrasing.

George Romero gave Peter in Dawn of the Dead the line "When there's no more room in hell, the dead will walk the earth." The streaming era's version is that when there's no more room in the multiplex, the flops shuffle onto Prime and Paramount Plus and eat everybody's evening. My complaint is the same one I always have with these lists. A film that loses a hundred million in theaters and gets a billion minutes of viewing on a platform hasn't "blown up." It's been reclassified, because the viewer minutes pay somebody else's bills and the theatrical numbers stay in the ledger. Still, I'll admit the zombie metaphor is working harder than the spreadsheet.

## History and Retrospectives: Time's Most Influential Papers

Defunctland's "The Absolute Debacle of 'Person of the Century'" gives us only the back end of the video, but the conclusion is crisp. The setup, per the excerpt, is that Time often needs a hero for the cover, and "if it's not Watson and Crick, then it must be Franklin," a simplification the video calls out because the real discovery story is more nuanced and "subjectively more interesting." Franklin is finally getting the credit she was denied through much of the 20th century, and people are still debating the details, but "there will probably never be a day where this story is short and simple. That's not how science works."

Hayden and Luce may not be the most influential people of the century, it grants, since "calculating influence like it's a measurable currency is futile," but they might be "the most influential people in making people think about who is the most influential." There's a jab at the disqualification of Jesus in the selection process, and a caveat that yes, it's a multi-person nominee, "I was told those were allowed." Its closing takeaway is modest on purpose: the follies of the Person of the Century contest illuminate ideas that "are not particularly groundbreaking," namely the problems with hero worship, the idea that no one does it alone, and the existence of nuance.

In Chicago-Outfit terms, "the fix is in" means the outcome was bought before the game started. A cover-story contest that has to produce a single winner has a fix baked in, whether or not anyone is paid off, and that seems to be the video's quiet point. I'd only add that magazines are the original engagement-bait algorithm.

## Late Night, Comedy, and Whatever the Hell This Is

Trevor Noah's "Who Are You Supposed to Date? Trevor Noah on Race, Love & Identity" is a stand-up excerpt built on observational comedy about dating. He points out that you'll never see a Black woman killed in the shower in a film. They get shot, run over, strangled, "but in the shower, never," because in that one moment "they're awake" and their senses are heightened. The second bit is about swimming and how people come up out of water, which he follows with his own confession that he's "not even a big fan of water" and likes to swim "on top." It's observational and loose, and the energy comes from him doing the voices.

Brad Williams's "Olive Garden Hoax, 'I'm Batman' & the Worst Game Show Idea" is a Heightened Babble clip. The part we have starts with a Los Angeles morning-radio anecdote about medical journals publishing, every year, a catalog of objects people get into trouble with. Brad's bit is that the penalty for those mishaps should be a game show, which prompts the line "That's just called a Japanese game show." The show's title, in the clip, is "Wreck Them, No, We Kill Them," and the grand prize, he argues, has to be money because "you've already been embarrassed by it. You might as well profit from it." The advice at the end to a woman whose photo was used by a website is the clean part of the piece: find a good lawyer and sue the hell out of the site. The title promises an Olive Garden hoax and a Batman segment, neither of which appear in the portion I was given, so I can't review them. I'll take the unreviewed material on faith, like the monolith.

Over at Jomboy Media, "Mets fans are shocked as the stove gets hot at the Winter Meetings" comes with a Weekly Dumb segment, and the excerpt is all animal news. A raccoon was found in a bathroom, face down and passed out. The hosts name the pose "splooting," then wonder where it picked up a taste for alcohol, who the "hobo helping him out" was, and whether a homeless man is bummed the raccoon didn't come back out with liquor. Then comes the real story, a seal that "galumped" into a New Zealand bar, hid in the bathroom, and was served salmon off the bar menu by the staff. The hosts spend a good stretch on the word "galumped," which they define as to move "with a clumsy, heavy tread," before one concedes the best take of the day: "to be fair to the seals, that's not awkward for them. That's how they move. Don't judge a seal's movement on the movement of a human." I would like to formally recognize this as the most reasonable sentence in today's entire dataset.
