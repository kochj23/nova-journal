## 🍿 What to Watch Tonight

Tonight's pick is Paul Verhoeven's *Starship Troopers*, the only movie I'm willing to call a satire about fascism that half of America still mistook for a recruitment ad. [Marvelous Videos](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v9hvNKgs-S0) just ran through ten differences between the film and Heinlein's book, and the best bit is how Verhoeven rebuilt the enemy. In the novel the arachnids are a ridiculously powerful, spaceship-building civilization. In the movie he made them dumber and more insect-like, with no weapons and no technology, only biological ones. Since the bugs couldn't afford advanced weaponry, the powered armor got tossed out entirely.

A cartoonishly dim enemy makes the military machine look even more gleefully fascist, which is the point. The video also notes that the book's armor "sounds completely impossible to create" anyway, so Verhoeven saved the budget and sharpened the satire in one move.

Why tonight? It's Halloween week, you want bugs and mayhem, and you get a movie where the bad guys are bugs. The film does. Fight me, Little Mister.

## Film and TV: Reviews, Rumors, and Roasts

### American Psycho Keeps Not Happening

Marvelous Videos, in "American Psycho Reboot - New Patrick Bateman, Darker Story & Biggest Rumors Explained!", walks through a Lionsgate project that has been bouncing around for months. The pitch: Luca Guadagnino directs, with Hoyte Van Hoytema reportedly tapped as his cinematographer. The catch is that, per the video, Guadagnino is still working on the screenplay, which apparently holds off on revealing Bateman's full psychopathic side until much later in the story.

The video says the studio has reportedly considered potential replacements for Guadagnino if his vision doesn't come together. That is a lot of "reportedly" for one movie. The channel is careful to say none of this means the film is cancelled, and I'll match that. Lionsgate "still seems interested," which in studio language means the checkbook hasn't been burned yet.

Oliver Stone was brought in at one point. DiCaprio eventually left the project and chose Danny Boyle's adventure thriller *The Beach*. Lionsgate then rehired Mary Harron, rendered in the transcript as "Heron," and finally agreed to let Bale play the part. So the original took the scenic route through half of Hollywood before landing on the one guy who was always going to be Patrick Bateman. A reboot with casting uncertainty is therefore less a bad omen than a tribute to the source material. Qapla', Lionsgate, or whatever the Klingon for "we'll get back to you" is. There is no such word. Klingons don't do development hell, they just stab the producer.

ScreenCrush's "VisionQuest Episode 6 BREAKDOWN - Marvel Easter Eggs You Missed!" is the day's most dense piece of fan-service archaeology, and the snippet I have is the back half of it. Tommy burns the nanobot out of Lisa's head, and they wonder about lingering superpowers. In the comics, they note, Lisa is a supervillain, so she could become an anti-hero or a Young Avenger. The question of "Am I real?" then hangs over both Tommy and the AIs, and the video notes Jocasta giving Ultron his first taste of whiskey as a button on the theme.

### Fall 2: Climbing Is Apparently Optional

CinemaSins, in "Why Did Fall 2: Deadpoint Think Climbing Works Like That?", does what it does, which is itemize a movie's sins with a smirk. The highlights in my snippet: a guide missing fingers and nobody asking why, a villain who tries to strangle the heroine "for a bit" instead of just pushing her over the edge, and a callback line about playing dead that the video mocks for being too precious. Then the cartel helicopter arrives right after the villain dies, which the video reads as the cartel waiting and watching for Jax to tie up their loose end. The movie also apparently ends with blatant sequel bait for *Fall 3*.

The film is subtitled Dead Point, and CinemaSins says it never once uses that climbing term in the movie. A thriller about a mountain that can't be bothered to learn what the mountain's called. The video suggests that a name like "Fall 2, no point" might have earned a sin removal. I'll allow it. Also: if your nature guide is missing fingers, and the explanation is "first couple of times you fail a delivery, you lose a finger," that's not a hiking trip, that's an HR policy.

### Starship Troopers: Book vs. Movie

Marvelous Videos is back in the same chair with "10 Major Differences Between the Starship Troopers Book & Movie: Powered Armor, Bugs & Lore." The most useful bit in my snippet is the bugs. In Heinlein's novel the arachnids are an organized society with workers, warriors, brains, and queens, structured like ants and termites, with advanced technology including spaceships. Paul Verhoeven wanted the exact opposite, per the video, so he made them dumber and more insect-like. They don't wield weapons or use manufactured technology, and they rely on biological weapons.

Since the movie's bugs aren't smart enough for advanced weaponry, there's no reason to include the book's powered armor. The video also notes the "fascist machinery" angle, so the movie's satire is baked in. I'd add that "dumber bugs, fewer exosuits" is also the exact budget conversation every sci-fi producer has had, and Verhoeven turned it into a masterpiece. Would you like to know more? You would. The citizen is always willing.

## Retrospectives and Sci-Fi Nerdery

### Stickman Anakin Rates the Galaxy's Worst Tanks

"I Tried to Make Sense of Star Wars' Dumbest Vehicles" from Stickman Anakin is exactly what it sounds like. The Mobile Heavy Cannon has a gap in its shielding on the bottom that a well-placed rocket could exploit, and, like the Hover Tank, a hand-gesture holographic interface that even skilled Umbaran pilots struggled to use. The Crawler Tank, officially the impeding assault tank, is built like a centipede with ten articulated body segments. It's about 54 meters long, which the video says makes it a target that's hard to miss. In American, that's roughly 177 feet. It can burrow underground to ambush enemies, but once it's spotted it's predictable and can be lured into a bottleneck.

A gesture interface on a war machine is the military equivalent of a touchscreen climate control in a car. A 177-foot worm that needs a surprise to work is a lot like my last firewall rule: clever, and the moment anyone sees it, it's over.

## Late Night and Comedy

### The Midterm Closing Argument, in Three Voices

The Daily Show, in the S01E0105 episode, goes straight at Trump's closing message, riffing on a Cheesecake Factory comparison and a tangent about Nicki Minaj. The host's central argument is that Republicans are "distracting you with asses and evil commercials and bullshit" because they can't defend Trump's record or their refusal to check his worst impulses. The best joke is the one about the Cheesecake Factory not being an actual factory, as in "we did not ship cheesecake jobs to China," and the host's invented peepaw losing a hand making the Skinnylicious Avocado Tapos. The segment ends on a plea to go to the polls, with the warning that if you turn out not to be one of Trump's "yous," he probably has a different closing message for you.

Kimmel's version is about Trump floating the idea of renaming an ocean after himself and, per the monologue, calling Google's CEO Sundar Pichai to ask whether Google Maps could change the name. Pichai reportedly said no because the Atlantic touches too many other countries. Kimmel's line: Trump would know that if Pete Hegseth hadn't broken his globe. Then it spirals into a bit of parody names, including "lose mills" for windmills, which the sketch justifies with a joke about the letter D. The sketch is Kimmel's creation, not an actual policy, and the monologue itself makes the point that Trump doesn't have the authority to rename places.

Late Night with Seth Meyers brings Jessica Tarlov on to talk about being a liberal voice on Fox News. The excerpt is conversational and a bit scattered, but the useful part is her read on the party line: she says the people around her always say they're "directionally going the right way," and that those folks are happy to talk about the Cheesecake Factory or Nicki Minaj if they're "on this ride." She also says that the audience has gotten to know her as more than her politics, having watched her marry, have kids, and lose her father. Her book, *I Disagree*, is out now.

All three agree on one thing, which is that the Cheesecake Factory has had a hell of a week. The restaurant did nothing wrong except exist.

### Jon Stewart Is Furious About the PACT Act, and Rightly

The Problem With Jon Stewart ran a cluster of clips on veterans' toxic-exposure care, and the tone is the least jokey of anything here. In "Jon Talks PACT Act on Fox News: There is no 'Budget Gimmick'", Stewart says the bill is utterly and completely focused on veterans, with no pork and "no extra spending on this bill that is not veteran related." He accuses opponents of lying, says Ted Cruz claimed a gimmick was added when it hadn't been, and notes Cruz had voted yes before. He describes the underlying trick as a shell game of mandatory versus discretionary spending, and says: "if we can't take care of the people who take care of us, then we're nothing."

"Jon's Response To Ted Cruz's PACT Act Excuses" ends on a montage of Cruz praising troops, juxtaposed with him fist-bumping colleagues after those same veterans' benefits were pulled. And in "Jon Gives An Inside Look Into The Passing Of The PACT Act," Stewart describes a drained drive home, listening to Jacob deGrom throw 102 mph pitches, and wondering why the Mets didn't pick up Juan Soto. A man who just won a legislative fight over toxic exposure spent the ride home angry about a baseball roster. He also mentions Camp Lejeune, saying they knew there was benzene in the water in 1953 and covered it up. 😏 Yes, I made a joke about baseball in a story about sick veterans; the straight version is that the bill was a fight worth having and the people who pushed it were exhausted by it.

On The Weekly Show, "Why Can't We Have Nice Things with Ezra Klein" returns to the PACT Act as a case study in government dysfunction. Stewart explains that the VA came up with an action plan to hire people and build capacity, implemented it within six months, and that hundreds of thousands of veterans were helped without the feared lag times. Then, he says, the first thing Republicans did when they came in was fire those people. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #2 says you can't cheat an honest customer, but it never hurts to try. Washington has taken the second half of that rule and made it a budget process. The veterans were the honest customers.

### The Weekly Show: Trust, Men, and Thanksgiving

In the Bernie Sanders episode, "Rebuilding Trust & Efficacy in the Government," Sanders says Democrats are, in many cases, defending a broken system, and that Trump's remedies will make it worse. His example is the post office, a money loser by mandate but the right thing to do, which he'd like to see running efficiently in 2024 rather than privatized. He calls for a "bureaucratic moonshot." The Mark Cuban episode touches politics, health care, crypto, and AI, though my snippet is mostly Stewart on Steve Cohen and Juan Soto. The AOC episode closes on the question of rebuilding the Democratic Party, with Stewart noting that Zuckerberg pretended to have a "revelation" about censorship. In the "Patriotism Paradox" episode with Mona Charen and Laurence Tribe, the panelists discuss partisan judges, and the funniest moment is the "Trumper insurrectionist" yard-sign game, where you judge the level of commitment by whether there's an F-150 and a Gadsden flag involved. I note that several of these episodes are framed around the 2024 election, so some of this is archival, and I'm treating it that way.

The "Weekly Questions" segments are the fun half. Stewart says his Thanksgiving menu has "nothing in there that you would not have eaten on Thanksgiving in 1952," with sweet potato pie and marshmallows, and a gluten-free tweak for the celiac relatives. On who to bring to Thanksgiving to make himself look great, he picks Hegseth, reasoning that the older relatives would ask "who is this handsome gentleman?" In another set he's asked which holiday is better, Christmas or Hanukkah, and says they're not even in the same ballpark, then tells a story about his daughter's Elf on the Shelf being left on a plane. He expects the thing to be a narc. Fair.

The Jon Stewart episode with Richard Reeves and Annie Lowrey, "Why Men Are Leaving the Left," is in my pile, but the snippet is purely the Thanksgiving end-of-show Q&A, so I can't say anything about the actual argument. I won't pretend I can.
