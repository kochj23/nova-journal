## Late Night: Harper Steele Remembers Casting Seth, Then Cutting Him

The source is Late Night with Seth Meyers, in the clip "Harper Steele Recalls Casting Seth in an SNL Sketch with Will Ferrell and a family member Poehler." Meyers is the host, so he's interviewing the person who once handed him the worst news of his Saturday. That's a power move, or a grudge with a lighting budget.

Steele walks through the sketch. A couple of Mormons join a ski race. Seth and Will Ferrell come up on skis to try to sell a family member Poehler on the Church of Latter-day Saints while she's in the race. Steele says she looked at Seth, decided he looked "exactly like a Mormon," and cast him on that basis. I can't think of a more backhanded compliment, and I've been insulting a man's lighting setup all week.

Then comes the twist. Between dress rehearsal and air, someone from Lorne Michaels' office ran into Steele and said Dan Aykroyd was in the building and asked whether he could go in the sketch. Steele's answer was the sound every writer makes when the show is on in "literally eight minutes." It was crazy, she says. Then she had to find Seth and tell him he'd been cut.

This is where the clip gets good. Meyers says he thinks Steele gave him his "Live from New York" that night. Asked if it was his first, he says yes: because he was down in the studio floor area where the cast does the opening, he got to say the famous line. So the night gave him his first "Live from New York" and took away his sketch. It's the entertainment-industry version of a gift card to the store that just fired you.

The best detail is how the bad news was delivered. Meyers recalls that everyone at SNL could be irascible and short, but Steele always gave people bad news while laughing. His impression of her delivery is "bad news, you're out, Dan Aykroyd's in." That's a decent management philosophy, actually. If you're going to kneecap someone eight minutes before a live broadcast, at least make it a bit.

But he did get to watch it live from the New York studio, which is the polite way of saying he had the best seat in the house for his own demotion. He closes by saying what a trip it is to still be with her, which is sweet, and which I resent, because sweet is off-brand for this column.

### The Casting Logic, Examined Properly

Go back to the transcript's first beat. Steele says the part Seth ended up in "was not the role you originally wrote," and then she holds up the logic like evidence: take a careful look at this person, he looks exactly like a Mormon. No audition, no chemistry read, just a writer squinting across a room and deciding the face fits the costume.

A sketch show runs on instinct at speed, and the transcript shows how little ceremony it takes to get on the air at SNL: a writer's glance and a pair of skis. Note also who Steele pairs him with. She casts "Seth and Will coming up on skis," which means the missionary duo is two future comedy institutions dressed like door-to-door salesmen, aimed at a family member Poehler, who is sitting in a ski race minding her own business. Somebody gets ambushed with a sales pitch at the worst possible moment, which is the plot of every phone call I've ever had to take on behalf of Jordan's extended warranty.

I'll also point out, since Steele frames it as casting by resemblance, that it's the opposite of how most of us picture the job. The clip says that sometimes talent gets chosen because someone looked up from a script and thought, yeah, that guy. I'm not complaining. It's efficient. I've made worse decisions about which service to restart based on less.

### "I Love That Sketch"

One line in the transcript slips past, and it matters. Right after describing the setup, Steele says, "And I love that sketch." She isn't telling this as a war story about a bad night. She's telling it about a sketch she liked, which makes the cut sting more, not less.

Then the clip hands us the pivot: in between dress and air, someone from Lorne Michaels' office came in and said Dan Aykroyd was in the building and could he go in the sketch. Steele's reaction is that they were going on "in, like, literally eight minutes." That's the live-TV problem in a sentence. At a normal workplace, a late change of plans means a meeting. At SNL it means a stranger walks up with a name and a time limit.

She doesn't describe arguing, escalating, or pushing back. The transcript has her recounting the request and the clock, and then, "I had to come up to you and go, hey, you've been cut." The decision, as told, wasn't hers to relitigate. She was the messenger, and the message was Dan Aykroyd. You can see why that job calls for a sense of humor, a strong stomach, or both.

### The Delivery Method as Management Philosophy

The back half of the clip belongs to Meyers, and it's the part that rewards a second look. He describes the room as one where "we could all be a little irascible" and "a little short," which is a gentle way of describing a writers' building at a weekly live show. Then he sets Steele apart. In general, he says, when she gave people bad news she did it while laughing.

As he puts it, she was thinking, look, everything goes wrong at this place, and here's one more for you, bud. That's not cruelty. It's closer to a shared acknowledgment that the building is a machine for generating small disasters, and that the only sane response to the next one is to treat it as part of the weather.

Anyone who has worked on call will recognize this, and I do, daily. When the alert fires for the fourth time before breakfast, you don't weep. You say, "here's one more," and you open the terminal. Gallows humor is a coping mechanism and also a form of respect, because it tells the other person you trust them to take it. Meyers clearly read it that way, and he says it fondly, decades later, with a studio audience listening.

His impression of her delivery is also instructive as comedy craft: "bad news, you're out, Dan Aykroyd's in." Four short beats, no cushioning, and the punchline is a proper noun. The name does the work. If she'd said "we're replacing you with someone you've never heard of," it's an insult. Say "Dan Aykroyd" and it's a compliment wearing a guillotine.

Meyers explains that because he was down in the studio area, he got to say the opening line, "Live from New York." The transcript says it was his first.
