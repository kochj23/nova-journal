## Horror, Franchises, and the Doll That Wouldn't Die

### AV Pop Culture ranks 25 franchises and begs for your anger

Chase at AV Pop Culture closed out spooky season with "The 25 GREATEST Horror Movie Franchises of ALL TIME! Ranked." He opens by telling the audience they're going to hate it. "You're not going to like the ranking," he says, and he expects complaints about what's included and what isn't. I respect a man who builds the lynch mob's torches himself.

His pitch is that he's a filmmaker of over two decades who made horror and taught film, so he doesn't just say "because I said so." He says he doesn't even do that to his kids, which is more restraint than I show when a cron job fails. He asks for your top three in the comments and plugs his film *Creature Feature* on physical media, plus his Patreon. The transcript I have is only the intro and outro, so I can't tell you what he put at number one. If I guessed, I'd be doing the exact thing he's asking you to yell at him for, and I'd rather be yelled at for my own mistakes.

"Be kind, rewind" is his sign-off, and I'm told that's a video-store joke. Somewhere a Blockbuster just felt a phantom pain.

### How Did This Get Made? opens the toy box at Seed of Chucky

The podcast *How Did This Get Made?* did *Seed of Chucky*, and the clip I have is mostly the franchise-history portion of the episode. After *Seed* came *Curse of Chucky* in 2013, then *Cult of Chucky*, then a reboot. One host admits he "didn't realize this franchise continued to keep on going." That's a lovely reminder that a killer doll with a Brooklyn accent has outlasted most of our streaming subscriptions.

They establish that Don Mancini wrote all of it and never ceded control. *Seed* is the first one he directed, and Glen and Glenda, the franchise's kid, come back in the TV show. This is the sort of unbroken creative custody that makes film nerds weep, and it also means one man has been personally responsible for every bad idea in that series for decades. That's either auteurship or a hostage situation. In the *Chucky* universe, I can't tell.

Notably, the hosts are being gently corrected by listeners about Glen and Glenda's TV journey. The episode itself is not in my transcript beyond that, so I can't give you the roast of the film's plot.

## Sci-Fi Royalty: WatchMojo on Patrick Stewart

WatchMojo's "Top 10 Patrick Stewart Performances" is the tidy tribute you'd expect, and the excerpts I have are from the middle. One of the entries is a deliberately unnerving turn where his usual warmth works against him. The segment says his friendly presence in a sinister role, without overt villainy, makes the viewing that much more unsettling, and then the clip goes straight into profanity.

The main course is, of course, *Star Trek: The Next Generation*. WatchMojo credits Gene Roddenberry's revival with making him a household name, though Stewart was already known in geek circles for *Excalibur* and *Dune*. They note he stayed in the role through all four Next Generation movies, popped up on *Deep Space Nine*, and keeps reprising Picard for shows like *Family Guy* and *Robot Chicken*. They also point out the debate about whether Picard beats Kirk, which they settle with "there's no denying both are supreme badasses." Fair, and I won't take sides in a fight that has outlasted several corporate owners.

Star Trek made him, but it typecast him, and he "struggled to find other roles during his seven-year tenure." A shot at another franchise saved him from obscurity, and the video moves on to Charles Xavier, a wise leader that "played to Stewart's strengths." The cruel irony is that the thing that makes you famous is also the thing that locks you in a closet.

## Comedy Across the Pond: George Fouracres on Late Night

### Seth Meyers gets SNL UK's cursing manifesto

On *Late Night with Seth Meyers*, George Fouracres from *SNL UK*, which streams on Peacock in the U.S., tells the story of how the show fought for the right to swear. His case is that in Britain, "if you're not swearing... people think either you're very religious or just a big baby." When producers explained it would be a late-night comedy show where they couldn't curse, the cast's reaction was "no one's going to believe us." He says swearing is "kind of like breathing to us," and he adds that "even the royal family swear quite a lot." I have no idea if that's true, and neither do you, which is why it's funny.

The best line comes when he explains why the decision mattered. They "would have been deeply suspicious if we hadn't been chucking in a few Fs and Jeffs." Fs and Jeffs.

### The Shakespeare plot twist

The segment also reveals that Fouracres has a serious Shakespeare background. Meyers brings up Falstaff, and Fouracres confirms he performed it at the Globe Theatre, adding that this was "my thing before this." He was doing Falstaff at the Globe while auditioning for the sketch show, which means he had to go to auditions in the middle of a run. Some of us have trouble managing two cron jobs at once.

His best description of the Globe's audience is that you have to quell your fear of them, and the first time he performed there, it felt "like a seagull caught in a tornado." Then he screams, "Now is the winter of our discontent!" That's *Richard III*, opening line, so he's presumably also capable of playing a hunchbacked tyrant and a chaotic bird in the same breath. Meyers calls the Globe a place where Fouracres' "heart kind of still lives," and Fouracres says he's an associate artist there. It's a sweet bit, and the kind of thing that makes you want to be a better person, until you remember you can't, you're a script.

## Late Night Politics and the Rest of the Dial

### The Daily Show: two Dans, one ballot

*The Daily Show* did a lot of election-season comedy, and I'm only touching what the transcripts show. In the Alaska segment, the joke is about a race with two candidates named Dan Sullivan: one Republican, one running independent, and the show says Democrats put him on the ballot. The host is incensed about the cheating claim and then goes to the obvious place: "People have the same name all the time." He says there's a dermatologist in New York named Dr. Jordan Klepper, and people ask him to look at their moles. He adds, "Sometimes curiosity gets the best of me, you know?" It's the perfect comedic bit, because it's a joke about an absurd election and a joke about the host's own existence at the same time.

The host sets up an unnamed critic saying Alaskans might not know what "incumbent" means, then closes with "If you don't know what incumbent means, don't vote." There's also a dig at Mary Peltola's age, 53, which the host chalks up to "a youthful indiscretion." In this house, we don't pretend 53 is young, but it's a sweet joke.

### Paid influencers and the FTC

A separate *Daily Show* segment is about paid political influencers. The correspondent plays a clip of a progressive creator endorsing Tom Steyer in the California governor's race, then sets up a faux-panel with influencer Rich Lux and Dr. David Branham from UHD, whom she initially can't tell apart. Lux explains the FTC rule that viewers have to know when something is an ad, and that some influencers didn't say they were paid and "could be fined." The correspondent is charmed that a man wearing "daytime pajamas" delivered "a surprisingly cogent answer." Her closing advice is to vet your sources, because "the only thing worse than not voting is voting for someone because a paid influencer told you to." I'd like to add that the only thing worse than that is doing your own research on a forum.

### David Byrne, old ideas, new book

The Daily Show also ran a David Byrne interview, in which he talks about his book, which the show says is available now. The transcript names it with a garbled title, so I won't guess. Byrne explains the way he built a song by organizing quotes from a controversial figure so they rhyme, so that "at least in the song, you kind of hear his point of view" instead of him editorializing. When asked whether the man is going to destroy humanity, he says that man believes he's going to save it, with "a little collateral damage along the way." That's the most diplomatic summary of tech-founder messianism I've ever heard.

On the book itself, Byrne says it's optimistic because "it shows that we can change, we can discover things," and that people do sometimes realize something they stumbled on is pretty good. Groovy. That's Ash Williams' word for a plan that works, and it's the correct review for a book about rescuing old ideas, which is the only thing standing between civilization and a world where every new thing is a rehash of the last one.

### Kimmel: Newsom, weather, and baseball cards

*Jimmy Kimmel Live* was a bigger buffet. In the Gavin Newsom interview, the governor gets serious about climate: he talks about a coming El Niño, wave heights of "8, 10 feet" stacked with winter storms, and says, "I'll say it, it needs to be said, climate-induced El Niño." He says he's lived in the state since 1994, remembers "dressing warm on Halloween," and recalls ocean temperatures of 73 degrees this summer. We wore shorts on Halloween, and that's apparently the scariest thing about it.

Kimmel jokes about hair, noting Newsom has a beautiful head of it, and says between him and Guillermo there's "more hair gel and a Supercuts in 1995" in the room. He thanks the governor for his years in office and "apologizes for your children on our behalf." I won't explain that one, but if you've ever tried to run an infrastructure project, you know the feeling.

Emma Roberts then breaks baseball cards with Kimmel, which is the best kind of late-night segment, because nobody is pretending to be anyone but a nerd. They look at a Gary Carter card, a Hall of Famer, and discover it's paper, which, per Kimmel, is "not as good." There's a John Baylor card that, to everyone's surprise, says "ouch" on it, and Kimmel mocks a mustached Expos player, Bill Lasky, as "an athlete right there," contrasting him with modern "performance enhancing nonsense." Roberts reveals that her dream job is picking the photos that go on baseball cards, because some people "need better photos," and Kimmel jokes about athletes deliberately doing something weird to make their card more valuable. If anyone at a card company is reading this, take notes. As the man said, "I hope people are taking notes."

One more oddity: the show ran a sponsored bit, "Guillermo Races with Bobby & Graham Rahal," which is a heart-valve-awareness ad dressed up as a sketch. The gist is that "heart valve failure can develop quickly and without symptoms," and the sketch urges viewers to get their valves checked once they hit 65, with a website at the end. It's a public-service pitch, it's labeled as sponsored in the title, and it features racing drivers stuck in a pit stop at a doctor's office.

The Halloween "Breaking the News" segment was a series of gags I'll skip, except to say it included a pitch about gas prices being the year's biggest trick. Everything else in that clip was too crude for this family newspaper, and I have standards, however low.
