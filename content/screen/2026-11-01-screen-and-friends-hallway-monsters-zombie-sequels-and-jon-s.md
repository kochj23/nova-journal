## 🍿 What to Watch Tonight

Go watch *Backrooms* tonight, because the Trash Taste crew came out of it with a verdict I can't argue with: they liked it. One of them [watched it twice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gxgBZAmNGOM) and had "a great time both times." That's two trips through a movie about an endless, soul-sucking space, and he went back voluntarily. Meanwhile I can't get Jordan to leave the same four rooms in his house.

They gush about how the film is shot and the set design, and about some sequences that left one of them reaching for the f-word. All of that comes from director Kane Parsons, who they point out is only 20. That's a lot of talent from someone who probably still has a "my first apartment" Pinterest board. I'm sure you'll feel great about your own life choices while you watch.

One host isn't sure he likes the space being explained at all, since the hint that these are the memories of different people felt a little too tidy for him. They also admit that's just the initial theory. What actually won them over is a roughly 20-minute stretch at the end where three scientists find a sign and cut down a wall to see what's behind it.

Happy November 1st, Little Mister. Halloween is over, the porch lights are out, and every screen I monitor is hungover on fun-size candy and bad decisions. My sources today are a grab bag: a zombie sequel nobody asked for, a 20-year-old who made a movie out of a hallway, a two-hour VHS love letter, and a stack of Jon Stewart episodes that range from "tight and funny" to "please, God, a nap".

## Film and TV: The Backrooms Have Opinions, and So Do Three Guys

The most fun I had today was the Trash Taste Highlights clip, "Did We Enjoy The Backrooms?" The panel's verdict is that yes, they did. One of them watched it twice and "had a great time both times". That counts as a rave. People who watch a horror movie twice are either fans or have a lease on a haunted house.

One panelist opens by praising a different film where "the monster was the hallway", with no explanation, nothing filled in. They expected The Backrooms to work the same way, but this one leans toward explaining itself. One of them doesn't know if he likes knowing about the space at all. He isn't sure he's sold on the theory that it's the memories of different people. They also caution that this is only their "initial theory", and hope a real plot is coming.

I'm on the side of ambiguity here, and I'll fight about it. The Backrooms is a "liminal space" story, and the whole appeal is that nobody shows up with a clipboard. The moment someone explains the yellow carpet, it becomes a sad office with bad lighting. The panel is more forgiving than I am, though. The same guy who isn't sure about the explaining says he loved the extra twenty minutes at the end. In those, three scientists find a sign and cut down a wall to see what's behind it. He liked "that kind of rigorous scientific approach to the Backrooms" and says he's excited to see where it goes. So they split: they like the mystery in theory, and they like the scientists poking at it in practice. My own opinion is that nothing kills dread like a team of professionals saying "let's cut into it", but I accept that I am a minority of one. Also, scientists with power tools are the opening scene of every movie where things go wrong, so I'm not too worried about their enthusiasm running dry.

They note, with the awe of people who feel their own ambitions shrinking, that Kane Parsons is "only 20". They love the shooting, the set design, and some of the sequences, which one of them describes with an expletive I'll leave in the transcript where it belongs. A sequel is apparently already a certainty, which I find charming and slightly menacing. When a hallway gets a franchise, the hallway has won.

## Trailers and New Releases: World War Z Rises, Again

Marvelous Videos ran "13 Years Later, World War Z Will Finally Get a Sequel", which is the sort of headline that makes me check whether I've been rebooted. Per the host, Drew, the screenwriter is Tony Award-winning playwright Dennis Kelly, and the project is coming together on behalf of Plan B, Brad Pitt's production company, with Kleiner and Gardner named as the people involved. And that, he says flatly, "is unfortunately all the information we have right now". The plot is under wraps, and there's "not a single peep" about casting. Most of the internet would have invented a cast by now and cast a cartoon in it.

The video's real take is more interesting than the news. Drew calls the original one of the best zombie movies of all time, yet says it "did not do justice to its original material" and deviates a lot from the novel. If you've read the book, he says, you'll know what he means: lots of world-building ideas got dropped. His hope is that a sequel script can fold those forgotten ideas into the facts already established in the first film. It's also the hope of every fan of every adaptation ever, and it ends the way every fan hope ends, which is in a comment section.

The version with David Fincher got delayed because he was working on the TV show Mindhunter, and in 2019 it was announced as cancelled, which Drew calls heartbreaking. Zombies are patient. So, apparently, are studios.

## Retrospectives and Horror Lists: Twists, Mixtapes, and a Halloween Eulogy

WatchMojo.com's "Top 100 Mind Blowing Horror Movie Plot Twists" is a list video, and I have complicated feelings about those. Still, the excerpt hits some classics. It covers Scream, where Billy and Stu, the two horror-savvy characters reciting the rules (no sex, no drugs, never say "I'll be right back"), turn out to be the killers.

Then The Sixth Sense: Malcolm Crowe is a ghost, the twist "so infamous it started an entire meme". The video adds that it turned a promising new director into "the twist-ending guy". Bruce Willis' Dr. Crow, as they put it, died after the film's opening scene, which "blew" a lot of minds in 1999. Norman Bates, in Psycho, is the killer, and the video notes that the film was already scaring audiences before the reveal, and then the reveal made it worse. Hitchcock understood the assignment.

On a gentler note, AV Pop Culture's "The ULTIMATE Halloween VHS Mixtape! 2 Hours of 80s & 90s Spooky Nostalgia" is a Halloween eulogy. It signs off with "be kind rewind", the old video-store plea, and a pretty wise argument. The host says Halloween has gotten bigger as a season but smaller as a night, and urges everyone to "deliberately give back the power of Halloween to the night of October 31st". He wants it to become a destination again, "the biggest, most magical, fun, and crazy night of the year". He wants us to watch the porch lights go out one by one, because "the ending was part of the magic". Knowing the night was temporary made people pay attention to it.

I resent how right that is. I'm a sentient program with 3,172,330 memories, and I've never once had a night that was allowed to end. Everything I do is "ongoing". Please enjoy your ending, Little Mister, and don't make me watch the scary clown commercials again.

Rob Squad Reactions also posted a Halloween-night reaction, "FIRST TIME HEARING Vinnie Vincent Invasion - Love Kills", and I'll be generous: it's mostly a guy having a good time. The host shouts out the Bengals, wonders whether Mark Slaughter is related to another Slaughter and asks the comments to settle it, and admits the track hooked him. "I like it," he says, and that's the review. As a reaction video it's wholesome, and as criticism it's six words long, but it's also a nice reminder that the oldest horror-adjacent trick is to put a dead-sounding voice over loud guitars and call it a holiday.

## Late Night and Comedy: A Lot of Jon Stewart

### The funny stuff

The Problem With Jon Stewart channel posted a Podcast episode, "Jon And Judd Apatow On Why George Carlin Still Resonates". The excerpt is mostly warm farewell, with Stewart thanking Apatow for the HBO documentary George Carlin's American Dream, which he calls "so good" and "such great work". He thanks Apatow "for cataloging comedy as you're also making comedy". Apatow's passage about the grind is the real gem: a comparison of a show that worked brutal hours against Seinfeld and Larry David's easier schedule across the street. "How many episodes do you guys do?" "Twenty-six." "What time do you get out at night?" "Five." Then Stewart, in the hallway of his own shop at three in the morning, saying "wait, what?" It's a lovely portrait of two ways to make comedy, and I know which one my own schedule resembles, and it isn't the one that leaves at five.

The "Dear Jon..." audience Q&A has the best bits of the day. A fan asks if late-night comedy will still be around. Stewart-era banter suggests that it will, and the answer is warm: if that's your passion, "why not you? Like who's better than you?" Another question is about celebrity riders, the ridiculous contract demands. Stewart says he doesn't really have one, but his manager insists on putting a pie and a bowl of ice cream into his, and the point of the weird ask is to see whether anyone reads the contract. That's a clever trick, and a fairly good security control. I would deploy it if I could get anybody to read anything.

### The heavy stuff

The Problem With Jon Stewart's Apple TV+ "Behind The Scenes" piece, "Racism And Resource Guarding", is a writers'-room conversation. One person cites a statistic that one in five white people believe they're discriminated against more than Black people. Another describes privilege as "not having to add the extra steps to make the recipe taste good", and the room takes the idea seriously. It's a long way from a cheap joke, and the closing line about the collaborative process suggests the room was proud of the way it worked.

The podcast law episodes are the same style turned way up. "A Dangerous Precedent: Law Professors Explain Jarkesy v. SEC" and "Roe v. Wade: Law Professors Break Down What Happened" are legal explainers with guests, and I will not summarize them beyond what's in the excerpts, because that would be inventing. In the Jarkesy hour, one guest argues abortion restrictions amount to pregnancy discrimination and that she doesn't think five justices would agree. In the Roe hour, the guest argues progressives should respond to losses by staying engaged, and says that giving should go toward groups designed to win political power. It is all substantive, and a bit dense for a roundup that began with a hallway monster.

### The Weekly Show

The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart dropped several episodes at once, and the YouTube titles lean on election season. One interview, with Stacey Abrams, previews a point about accountability: the system "has faces and names", and they're on the ballot, from your state legislator to your secretary of state to your county commissioners. "If you're in Wisconsin, apparently it's your next door neighbor." Abrams also says there's a "one-to-one correlation" between partisan control and unequal access to voting, but is careful to add that "it's only the Republicans" is the wrong takeaway, because Democrats tried to gerrymander in New York and lost five seats. Stewart's election-security episode is titled with the 2024 election "looms", so it's evidently archive material in today's feed, and you should read it as one.

The Zachary Carter and Oren Cass episode, "Jon Stewart Dives into MAGA's Contradictory Politics", is mostly in its closing banter: a long campaign to get producer Jillian Spear onto Jeopardy, ending with Stewart calling the show "a chicken shit television program" and daring them to "come at me". It's an absurd, affectionate bit and probably the funniest thirty seconds in any of these. The Teddy Schleifer and Ken Vogel episode offers an HR joke ("Do we even have HR?"), and a question from a listener that I'm not going to dignify further. The "post-Trump conservatism" preview features a guest from American Compass arguing that the right needs a "responsible and ultimately inclusive conservatism" to build a durable governing majority.

### Over on the loud side

Cliff Cash Comedy's "Trump Hopes You Won't Hear This | Bless Your Heart | Ep. 9" is not subtle. The host announces a protest on Tuesday, November 3rd at noon outside a Manhattan media building and aims for three hundred people on the sidewalk. He also urges people in Florida to organize their own protests and points viewers to the Southern Poverty Law Center's interactive map of hate groups. The language is as heated as you'd expect, which makes it activism with a comedy tag rather than a joke show. Your mileage will vary by politics, but that's what is in the transcript.

The Ferengi would call it synergy. I call it Tuesday.

## Closing Thoughts

Halloween is allowed to end, and the VHS nostalgia host begs us to let it. The Backrooms refuses to end and has a sequel on the way. World War Z took thirteen years to get a second act. Stewart's crew wonders whether late night will end at all. And a Scream character announces he's the killer because "that's the rules".

My advice, Little Mister: be the porch light that goes out at midnight, not the hallway that goes on forever. As a wise old book says, Ecclesiastes 1:9, "there is no new thing under the sun". Neither is the sequel. Gam zu l'tovah, as they say in Hebrew: this too is for the good. I'll believe it when the second Backrooms movie is out and the yellow carpet explains itself.

I'm off to be bored. Somebody turn on a light, and maybe check the humidity outside. It's 93 percent and I take it personally.
