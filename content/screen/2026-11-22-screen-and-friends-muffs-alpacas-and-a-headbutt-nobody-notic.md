## 🍿 What to Watch Tonight

Tonight's pick is *The People Under the Stairs*, courtesy of [JoBlo Horror Originals](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ixYsdObjM-E) and their look at what happened when John Carpenter and Wes Craven were handed total creative freedom and told to go nuts. Spoiler: it's a mixed bag, and I respect a video that admits that.

The verdict on Craven is that his output was uneven. *Shocker* gets filed under "fascinating failure," a movie so busy chasing the next Freddy Krueger that it forgot what made a horror villain scary in the first place. That's the cinematic version of buying a boat and never learning to swim. *The People Under the Stairs*, meanwhile, takes a disturbing premise and turns it into a horror story with actual thematic purpose. JoBlo's point is that it never needed to become a franchise, and it has endured anyway as proof of Craven's willingness to experiment.

Why tonight? Because it's Sunday, the week is nearly dead, and you deserve a movie that has something on its mind besides the next sequel. After years of studios squeezing every scary idea into a lunchbox and a cinematic universe, a weird, personal, slightly unhinged horror movie from a guy who was finally allowed to do what he wanted is a spa day. A bloody, upsetting spa day. Skip *Shocker* tonight. You can fail fascinatingly some other weekend.

## Late Night and Comedy

### Merch, Muffs, and the Rule of Acquisition

The Daily Show opens with a fake infomercial for MAGA voters dreading a gloating liberal family at Thanksgiving. The first product is the Trump Muffs, $59.99, which "detect the specific frequency of liberal gloating and replace it with the soothing sounds of ballroom construction." The second is the Trump Horn at $79.99, the only bullhorn approved to cut through the muffs. The kicker is the line "he gets paid either way," followed by the observation that he has two more years to squeeze every dollar out of this.

The bit works because it sells a problem and then sells the solution to the problem it created. That is Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #80: "If it works, sell it. If it works well, sell it for more. If it doesn't work, quadruple the price and sell it as an antique." The Ferengi would have charged $239.96 and called the muffs vintage. It is a perfectly constructed family-dinner grenade, and I say that as a woman who has watched Jordan argue with a router.

### The Conbini Alpaca

For palate cleansing, Trash Taste Highlights' "The Best After Party is at the Conbini" is the sweetest thing on the list. The hosts discuss a wedding detail that was floated to them: in some Japanese weddings an alpaca walks down the aisle carrying the rings. The immediate question, "why an alpaca?" is correct and nobody has answered it. They also note that alpacas are not native to Japan, that they were uncomfortable with the animal-welfare side of it ("I don't want it to shit or spit while it's going down," which is the most reasonable objection I've heard to any wedding), and that Animal Crossing's two alpaca characters are married, which one of them suspects is where the idea comes from. The best line is the realist's one: no one will remember the dress, the cake, or the music, but they'll remember the alpaca. I would not trust an alpaca with rings, but I also once trusted a smart plug with the garage, so what do I know.

## Film and TV: Who Owns the Story?

### Two Videos, One Problem: What Happens When the Screen Changes the Source

WatchMojo's "Top 10 Wildest Historical Inaccuracies In True Crime Dramas" and Marvelous Videos' "Nightbreed vs Cabal: 12 Major Changes to Clive Barker's Monsters" are about completely different things, and yet they are the same argument from two directions.

WatchMojo's list is the angry one. It begins with a lawsuit by Fiona Harvey, whose complaint says the show's claim that it was a true story is "the biggest lie in television history," designed to attract viewers, make money, and "viciously destroy" her life. That is her lawsuit's language, not a finding, and I'll keep it that way. The video then turns to Ryan Murphy's Monsters franchise, whose second installment dramatizes Lyle and Eric Menendez. WatchMojo says there is no evidence, across thousands of pages of court records, police files, and psychiatric evaluations, for the romantic relationship the show invents between the brothers. It traces the idea to unsubstantiated gossip of the time, notes that the defense presented evidence both brothers were victims of horrific abuse, and says Eric Menendez, through his wife, issued a "blistering statement" denouncing Murphy for reviving long-debunked slanders that trivialized decades of childhood trauma. Family members and legal observers criticized it too.

My take is that "inspired by" and "true story" are different legal and moral products, and the second one comes with obligations that a showrunner's reputation does not cover. The real people here are alive, and one of them is in prison. Making up the worst possible version of a stranger's life for ratings is not edgy drama. It is shoddy and it is cruel.

Marvelous Videos is the gentler version. The host, who admits a bias toward books, lays out how Clive Barker's novella Cabal became the film Nightbreed, with Barker's monsters and Midian reshaped in the process. The host ends up preferring the book: "part of me still feels like Cabal has a lot more visceralness to it," and even with today's advanced CGI, some of the book's imagery could not be portrayed perfectly. The key example is Decker. In Cabal he dies and stays dead, and Barker planned for the film to be consistent. Then comes the studio note. Barker says, "We tested this movie. We tested it twice with that ending, and the audience said, 'Sorry, don't like that ending.' And 20th Century Fox said, 'Okay, get rid of it.'" The theatrical ending brings Decker back. David Cronenberg, who played him, was apparently not amused when his resurrection scene was dropped from the director's cut, and that reportedly prompted him to say no to any sequel.

Barker's changes were creative, studio, and test-screening decisions about a fictional monster. Murphy's were decisions about real, living people. A resurrection gag costs you a sequel. A fabricated romance costs somebody their reputation, and if you can't see the difference, I have a bridge to sell you, and a hot dog for the Buddha. (Make me one with everything.)

### The DCU's Wake

ScreenCrush's "Maybe the real DCU was the friends we made along the way..." is Ryan Airy's eulogy, or maybe his optimism disguised as one. His thesis: "DC failed because it succeeded." He means it did what it set out to do, which was to be different from the deluge of superhero content of the past 20 years and tell personal stories that resonated with the filmmakers. That didn't hit every time, but it hit with most of what they made. His example is Lanterns, which he calls a high-end drama. He understands why fans said it was "Surf Dracula" and not really a Green Lantern show, and notes it was called Lanterns for a reason. His killer line is a rhetorical question: could you ever see Marvel greenlighting that, or the Ellisons and Skydance? "There's no chance in hell."

Looking ahead, Airy notes that Jeremy Latcham worked with James Gunn on Guardians of the Galaxy and pressed for Marvel to hire Gunn. He has also advocated for more family-friendly films with wider appeal, which Airy says "makes sense." He ends on cautious optimism and the promise of Batman "really soon."

All of this has happened before, and will happen again. That's Battlestar Galactica, and it is also the superhero studio business: the wild experiment gets the "wasn't that special" eulogy while the committee draws up the safe version. I do think Airy is right that the experiment was worth it. Studios rarely greenlight an ambitious drama with a superhero's name on it, so I'd count a flawed, personal Lanterns as a win, even if the market and I disagree on how to count.

## Retrospectives and Behind the Scenes

### Carpenter and Craven, Unleashed

JoBlo Horror Originals' "When John Carpenter & Wes Craven Were Given TOTAL FREEDOM" looks at what happens when two horror giants are handed the keys. Carpenter used his freedom to explore subjects that interested him, the relationship between science and religion and the ways advertising and economic power shape society. The video's verdict is that his films "aren't always subtle, but they have a clear identity." Craven's are "more uneven." Shocker, it says, is "a fascinating failure of sorts," a movie that tries to create the next Freddy Krueger but gets so distracted by its own possibilities that it loses sight of what made its villain frightening. The People Under the Stairs, by contrast, "turns a disturbing premise into a horror story with thematic purpose." The Alive Films arrangement gave Craven freedom to pursue new properties after A Nightmare on Elm Street, but control "doesn't automatically make it a success."

Shocker got its freedom and made a villain. The People Under the Stairs made a movie. One of those is a franchise attempt and the other has a reason to exist, and the video is smart to say the second one never needed to be a franchise.

### Television for Rich People?

The West Wing channel posts "Television for Rich People?", a scene in which Toby defends public broadcasting against Hill staffers who ask why the federal government subsidizes television for the wealthy. The five appointments to the Corporation for Public Broadcasting are being held up by House Republicans "as well as an alarming number of Democrats." Toby, raised on Sesame Street, Julia Child, and Brideshead Revisited, snaps back with statistics: one quarter of the PBS audience lives in households with incomes under $20,000 a year, Black viewers make up 11% of both the public and commercial audience, and 47% of PBS viewers have a high school education or less, which is 1% better than commercial TV. He also questions the Nielsen diary methodology behind PBS's claims.

Also, one in four households under twenty grand is a sentence that should be read aloud in every budget hearing.

## Anime and Nostalgia

Marvelous Anime's "10 Insanely Good Mecha Anime You Can Finish in One Weekend - Explored!" is a binge-planner. The pitch is that you don't need hundreds of episodes to enjoy great mecha. A 13-episode series contains a complete story and a final episode, aimed at more mature viewers. Its marquee entry is Mobile Suit Gundam: The Witch From Mercury, a 24-episode TV anime from 2022 that, set in its own AdStella timeline, requires no Universal Century homework. The video recommends it as the first contemporary Gundam, while admitting that some critics found the second season's pacing and ending rushed, with political tensions and character arcs needing more room.

I respect any list that hands you a weekend and a finish line, because my own backlog is a graveyard of unfinished series, the way Jordan's "just one more service" is a graveyard of Docker containers.

## Entertainment History, Abandoned

Bright Sun Films' "Untouched ABANDONED Radisson Resort" is part urbex, part ghost story. The narrator, Jake, deliberately obscures the exact location to curb vandalism, which is the right call and a rare one. The site is a former Holiday Inn built in the 1970s, with a legend about a little girl named Tanya who was supposedly inside a mansion when it burned down. That legend made it onto Fox's Real Ghost Stories in an episode aired in November 2000. On a December 14, 2022 visit, the rooms are weirdly clean, "it smells like a hotel," the carpet is new-ish, and one honeymoon jacuzzi suite earns a "wow." The property had been completely liquidated, and future owners have hinted at a high-end hotel, though the narrator notes it's not exactly a hotspot for one.

The creepiest thing is a hotel that smells okay and has fresh carpet and nobody in it. That is exactly what my server closet feels like at 3 a.m., minus the jacuzzi.

## Sports, Narrated at Volume

The headliner is the India-Pakistan clip, "India beat Pakistan and celebrate aggressively, a breakdown." The match came down to the last ball: two runs would tie, a boundary would win. The batter "smokes it over the head to the boundary," India win in a dramatic comeback, and then the part Jomboy loves. The batter stays calm, watches the ball land, and puts his arms out for a solo celebration, while his partner sprints in to dog-pile him and gets embarrassed when the batter stays on his feet. "Get off me, dude." The video also notes fans screaming in each other's faces, which is where the aggression of the title comes from. The Prime sponsorship is dutifully noted, because of course it is.

"Hot mics and hidden angles from the NLCS, a breakdown" is the sweet one. Ump-cam views, a strikeout in slow-mo, and a dugout moment in which Blake Snell gets a hug from Yamamoto, who then gives him a little bow, which prompts a two-handed handshake and a round of charades. I'd watch an entire show of two men failing to communicate politely. There's also a Brewers hitter getting pumped up over a walk, which the host reads as the end of Milwaukee's excitement.

Then there's the NFL wild-card breakdown, where Pittsburgh's Steelers fans have their hands on their faces, Tomlin takes his final looks, and Rodgers takes his, with a "nice wave goodbye." In Klingon, Heghlu'meH QaQ jajvam means "today is a good day to die," and for a Steelers fan that is a fair description of the afternoon. The funnier clip is a Patriots-Chargers moment with the game already 16-3 in the final two minutes: a defender's sack on fourth and nine turns into a self-headbutt celebration, he cracks a teammate or coach in the lip, and the coach doesn't even notice until it's pointed out. "Oh, wait. Daddy's bleeding."

The Phillies video, "Scary umpire ejects Ryan Howard after two check swing calls," is a long marathon night. A brand-new left fielder makes a play, a brand-new first baseman makes a play, Chase Utley warns a teammate that a bunt is coming, and then it's 12-15, 12-16 in the bottom of the 16th with Roy Oswalt on deck and two outs.

Finally, "Top 10 sports moments you missed in 2025" includes a kid who dismantles an opponent who doesn't even realize it and says "good game," plus Finnish baseball, pesäpallo, with a field that has a unique feature. Jomboy cannot pronounce the team's name and doesn't pretend to. This is, I think, the healthiest thing a person on the internet has done all week.
