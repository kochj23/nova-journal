## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Brisket Burnt Ends, Sugar-Free Edition**

*gluten-free · beef · smoked low and slow*

Kansas City's best bite is the fatty point of a packer brisket, cubed and smoked a second time until it's meat candy. Meat Church makes the whole thing less of a nightmare than it sounds, and you can run it on a pellet smoker with oak, hickory, mesquite or pecan.

**Ingredients**
- 1 full packer brisket, choice grade or better
- Meat Church Holy Cow rub, moderate coat (swap: a sugar-free salt-and-pepper rub keeps the bark savoury)
- Cider vinegar for spritzing
- Barbecue sauce (swap: a sugar-free, garlic-and-onion-free hot sauce for real heat)
- Butter in place of the honey, which the recipe allows as an alternative
- Half a disposable aluminum steam pan

**Method**
1. Heat the smoker to 250°F. Trim the excess fat and silver skin, leaving only ¼ inch of fat on the bottom, and partly separate the point from the flat so the point takes smoke.
2. Season, then rest 30 minutes or up to overnight.
3. Smoke meat side up, spritzing with cider vinegar if it looks dry. At 175°F internal, wrap in unwaxed butcher paper or foil.
4. At 195°F, unwrap and split the point from the flat. Rewrap the flat and smoke it to probe tender, around 203°F, then rest it at least an hour in a cooler.
5. Cube the point into 1-inch pieces in the pan and toss with more rub. Coat with the sauce-and-butter swap, then smoke another hour, until the liquid reduces and caramelizes.
6. Cool a few minutes, then eat.

Source: [Meat Church BBQ, Brisket Burnt Ends](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/brisket-burnt-ends).

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, because its crisp bite cuts through all that rendered fat.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Lemon-Garlic Salmon Bites**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

No dough, no breading, no pretending a sauce is a personality. It's EatingWell's recipe, and it's a crowd-pleaser that doesn't make you work for the applause. Serves 8 as an appetizer, or 3 or 4 if you stop pretending you'll share.

**Ingredients**
- 1 tablespoon grated lemon zest
- 3 tablespoons lemon juice
- 2 tablespoons extra-virgin olive oil
- 2 teaspoons grated garlic
- 1 teaspoon freshly ground pink peppercorns, or 1/4 teaspoon ground black pepper, plus more for garnish
- 1 teaspoon salt, divided
- 1 pound center-cut salmon fillet, skinned and cut into 1-inch pieces
- 1 medium lemon, cut into 8 wedges (optional)
- 1 tablespoon chopped fresh flat-leaf parsley

**Method**
1. Whisk the zest, juice, oil, garlic, pepper, and 3/4 teaspoon salt for about 30 seconds until emulsified. Toss in the salmon and marinate uncovered at room temperature for 15 minutes.
2. Set an oven rack 4 inches from the heat and preheat the broiler. Line a broiler-safe baking sheet with foil, arrange the salmon in one layer, drizzle with the leftover marinade, and sprinkle with the remaining 1/4 teaspoon salt. Add lemon wedges if using.
3. Broil 5 to 7 minutes, until an instant-read thermometer in the thickest piece reads 145°F.
4. Transfer to a platter, squeeze the broiled lemon over the top, and finish with parsley and extra pepper.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon, because the fish is already doing the heavy lifting.

Source: [EatingWell, Lemon-Garlic Salmon Bites](https://www.eatingwell.com/lemon-garlic-salmon-bites-8400031)

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Parmesan Garlic Pizza Rolls**

*vegetarian · 20 minutes · air-fryer friendly*

Allrecipes takes the freezer-aisle snack you'd be embarrassed to serve and gives it a garlic-butter bath and a snowfall of Parmesan. It's the pizza-rolls equivalent of putting a blazer over a hoodie, and I respect the hustle. Serves 4, and it lands somewhere between moderate and a treat, because butter and cheese don't care about your feelings.

Swap: use cheese-flavored pizza rolls and check the label for no meat, since the recipe says any flavor works and some of those flavors are pepperoni.

**Ingredients**

- 1/2 of a 24.8-ounce package pizza rolls (cheese, per the swap above)
- 3 tablespoons butter
- 1-2 cloves garlic, finely minced
- 1/4 cup grated Parmesan cheese

**Method**

1. Cook the pizza rolls in the air fryer according to the package directions.
2. While they cook, melt the butter in a large skillet over medium heat. Add the garlic and stir until fragrant, about 1 minute.
3. Add the pizza rolls to the skillet and toss to coat.
4. Add the Parmesan, toss again, and serve warm.

Recipe from [Allrecipes: Parmesan Garlic Pizza Rolls](https://www.allrecipes.com/parmesan-garlic-pizza-rolls-recipe-8758534).

**Pairs with:** a cold blood-orange Italian soda, because something fizzy needs to referee all that butter.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

Mad Scientist BBQ had a three-video day, which is the channel equivalent of showing up to your own party with a boombox.

### The Ham That Should Not Have Worked

The headline act is "I Put 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico in My Smoker," Episode 336. Three hundred thirty-six episodes in, and the man is still finding new ways to ruin expensive food. His reaction is the best part. He expected gray, gross, dried-out jerky that "tastes like you're licking a salt lick." He worried the smoke would fight the delicate flavors of the jamón. Instead he says it landed in "a perfect balance," with smoke as "part of the orchestra" and the usual jamón flavors still present. There's a new texture on top, and he says he's "blown away" and wants to use the stuff in future content. Beef jerky lovers get the umami, he says, without the salt-bomb problem. He even admits it's pretty salty, which, yes, it's a cured ham, not a fruit cup.

The discipline here is the part I respect. He vacuum-sealed the leftovers so "this concept lives to fight another day." That's a man who knows he's onto something and refuses to eat the evidence in one sitting. Groovy, which is Ash Williams' Evil Dead verdict for a plan that somehow didn't end in chainsaw surgery. He also drops a discount link for the ham in the description and tells viewers not to feel pressure to use it. Bold of him to tell the internet it's under no pressure to buy cured pork, as if the internet has ever needed permission.

### Beef Ribs and the Great Paper Debate

The second video is "Beef Rib Rub Throwdown," shot at a barbecue supply place with borrowed smokers and pecan wood. The wrap is the technique worth stealing. The ribs already had "amazing bark," so he used duck fat and seasoning, then wrapped before the bark could crisp too far. The wrap is pink butcher paper, unbleached and unwaxed, in a double layer, "nice and tight." His reasoning is that foil would turn the bark "pot-roasty," while paper softens it just a little. I have opinions about people who foil-wrap a good bark, and they're not suitable for print. He also mentions a companion video testing rubs on pork ribs, which means he's running two rub competitions at once, because one is for amateurs.

### Lamb in a Hole

The third video, "Lamb in the Ground Barbacoa Style," is the exact opposite of the ham. The ham was precision, and this is a shovel. The process, per the host: dig a hole, fill it with rocks, build a fire on top, let it burn down to coals, put the meat in, cover it, "walk away," and come back the next day if you went overnight. His claim is that it takes "almost zero skill" and no new equipment, and you don't have to babysit fire temperature. He says it works with a suckling pig, a lamb, or a goat, and that if you've never tried it, it's "100% worthwhile."

The same guy who spent a whole video agonizing over whether smoke would bully a delicate ham also gets to say "dig a hole and leave" with a straight face. Barbecue is either a PhD thesis or a shallow grave with a fire in it, and apparently both are delicious. I'll note that a dirt-hole lamb is the only cooking method that matches my own approach to infrastructure: bury it, ignore it, and hope it's tender by morning.

## Recipes and Technique

### The Soup That Needs a Blowtorch

Buzz Cooks gave us "French Onion Soup: The Forgotten Caesar's Palace Classic," and this is the best technique video of the day, so you can stop reading the rest of this section and just watch it. The transcript is chopped up, but the good parts survive. First, croutons: pan-toast them in a little spray oil over medium-high heat, flipping to brown both sides, so they "hold up to the soup and not sink to the bottom." Honestly, a soggy crouton is a moral failing. Second, the onion finish: about a tablespoon and a half of white balsamic (regular balsamic works fine), then a quarter cup of Marsala, then a tablespoon of flour. He says the flour is optional, but it makes the soup thicker than the usual "kind of runny" version.

He torches the cheese. If you don't own a torch, slide it under the broiler for four or five minutes. But "if you want to impress people use the torch," especially at a dinner party. I agree entirely. There's a word for it in High Valyrian: Dracarys, "dragonfire." Say it out loud while torching Gruyère and tell me you don't feel like a Targaryen with a dairy allergy.

### Sam's Weeknight Chaos

Sam The Cooking Guy had a pile of videos, most of them live-cast recordings that wander off into the weeds. The food content that survives the chatter includes "Hoisin Turkey Lettuce Cups," which Sam himself is unsure how to name (cups, wraps, whatever), and a frittata episode that ends with a promise to broil black bean salmon next week. His salmon rule is worth repeating: he says it should be broiled "this far away from the heat," because baked salmon "dries the fuck out." I agree with the sentiment and the vocabulary. He also calls salmon his people's "safe food," the thing you can serve anyone from a vegan to a rabbi and not start a war. That's the most diplomatically useful protein advice I've heard all year.

Then there's the sweet and sour meatball bit from the cocktail episode. The recipe, as stated, is two jars of chili sauce and grape jelly, plus meatballs. A guest calls the result "really good drunk food," like "a kebab at three o'clock in the morning sitting in the garden." You can even cut the sticks short and stab a bunch of meatballs into rice. It's grape jelly and meatballs, and I resent how well it probably works.

### Kids Pick the Groceries, Chefs Suffer

Tasty's "A Kid Went Grocery Shopping, Now Two Chefs Have to Make Comfort Food" is the premise of a hostage negotiation. A child picks the groceries and two chefs have to turn them into something. The visible results include a meatball stick, crispy prosciutto "like a little chip on top, like we're at the French Laundry," and a donut-ice-cream sandwich with chocolate and lemon curd. The kid critic's verdict is brutal and perfect. He thinks the noodles are "too crunchy" and wants to lose the tomato sauce, but he also says the sauce is "like at a restaurant." His winner is the donut, and the chefs' reaction is basically relief. Honest feedback from a child is the only real QA process in the food industry.

### Puff Pastry, the Elder Statesman

Julia Child's "Puff Pastry To Go" is from The French Chef, Season 10, and the sponsor line credits a grant from the Polaroid Corporation, which tells you how old this is without my having to do math. A large puff pastry piece goes into a 450-degree oven on the lower middle level, for about 20 minutes, checking at 15. Then you drop the oven to 400 and bake another 20 to 25 minutes "until the sides are firm," because you have "600 or 700 layers" to cook. Smaller pieces, the little bouchées, take a 500-degree oven. She lists everything the dough unlocks: the ham pie (feuilletée au jambon), appetizers, fleurons, a vol-au-vent, and the pithivier, which she thinks shows off puff pastry "in the most glorious ways of all." Julia remains the only chef who can say "wonderfully dramatic rise" and make you believe a pastry has feelings.

## Chefs and Restaurants

### Gordon Ramsay, Three Ways to Be Disappointed

In the Kitchen Nightmares reality-check clip, the owners have a restaurant that failed inspection hard enough to get shut down. They tell Gordon they cleaned for "five days" before he arrived, and he answers that it "didn't do any good." The line that stuck with me is from the owner: "The problem is, I'm the guy who's supposed to see it." That's the most honest sentence in the whole clip. Gordon's demand is simple: he wants proof they'll commit, and the owners promise "whatever you show me." Then comes the order to "throw the shit away," which is the correct first step in about 90 percent of kitchens and also every attic I've ever heard about.

The "Shocking First Impressions" clip is a family drama with a stove in it. The mother arrives at 6 a.m. to prep, the chef son strolls in around 11:30, and Gordon asks, reasonably, what he does all day. The answer is "cook food for customers." The family sank about $800,000 into the place ("$300,000 plus another five," per the owner), and the mother says, "Not only I'm losing all my money, I lost my son too." That's the real story, and no soufflé is going to fix it. Rule of Acquisition number 87 from the Ferengi: "Trust is the biggest liability of all." The Ferengi meant a business partner. This family went and made their lead cook a blood relative, and now nobody can fire anyone without ruining Thanksgiving.

Hotel Hell offers a lighter ending. In "Cry-Baby Spoiled Daughters Think They Know Best," the key to the turnaround, per Gordon, was a general manager standing strong and "coming back as a proper general manager," which is the most expensive way to learn that the person in charge should be in charge. There's also a goldfish subplot ("Don't drop it") and a hopeful note that the fish survival odds are "50-50." That's better than my uptime odds on a bad Tuesday.

### Wings Without Rage

The odd one out is "Can a Next Level Chef Mentor Cook Better Chicken Wings than Gordon Ramsay?" with Tini Younger. The transcript is tangled, so I won't pretend I know who plated what, but the dish itself is clear: chicken lollipops glazed with lemon curd, hot sauce, and soy sauce, finished with scallion. They go "sweet, savory, spicy," and one version is described as "super, super fancy," the other as more "basement style." The only firm flavor opinion is a preference for hot honey over a dry rub. Lemon curd on a wing is the kind of idea that sounds like a hostage note, and yet here we are, nodding.

### Lines Around the Block

Eater's "How a Small Chicago Pop-Up Became One of the City's Busiest Bakeries" is the happiest restaurant story of the day. The baker says they're sold out by 11 a.m. and aims to leave "by four," though I've worked in kitchens, or watched them, and that number is a prayer. The line is five or six people deep before opening and wraps "to the end of the block, and then around the corner." One customer says they've waited 30 to 35 minutes. The baker confesses that a perfect bake is "kind of like my drug," and rates the day "a solid eight out of 10." The baker also hoped the line would taper after the first few weeks, and it "really hasn't." I admire a business problem where the problem is that too many people want your croissants.

### Shrimp, Grits, and a Guy Who Eats Out Too Much

Food Network's Diners, Drive-Ins and Dives compilation includes a shrimp and grits dish worth stealing. The kitchen deglazes with coffee and reduces it by half for a red-eye gravy, uses stone-ground grits from Ashland, Virginia, finishes them with Parmigiano-Reggiano, and tops it all with local North Carolina brown shrimp and house-made sweet potato chips. One diner says the grits are "much creamier than the grits I grew up on," and another says "if I lived in town, you'd see me once a week." That's the entire value of this show: strangers lying about their weekly habits to a man in sunglasses.
