## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Reverse Seared Steak**
*gluten-free · low-carb · smoker-friendly*

Meat Church's Reverse Seared Steak is a thick ribeye, a slow trip through the smoker, and a blazing skillet to finish. That's the whole recipe. Matt Pittman's [Reverse Seared Steak](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/14684969-reverse-seared-steak) promises medium-rare from edge to edge with a proper crust, and it earns the promise by not being clever. The pellet smoker does the slow part while you stand around holding a drink and pretending you're busy. One 2-inch ribeye feeds two. It's a treat, and a damn good one.

**Ingredients**
- 1 bone-in ribeye, about 2" thick, Choice or better (Prime is better)
- Holy Cow BBQ Rub, or swap in the source's own fallback: a 50/50 mix of salt and coarse cracked pepper. It keeps the bark savory and sugar-free.
- 1 thick slice of butter
- Cast iron skillet and a meat thermometer

**Method**
1. Preheat the grill or pellet smoker to 275°F, set up for indirect heat.
2. Season both sides, one side first, then flip and season the other.
3. Cook on the grate until the center hits 120°F, about 45 minutes for a thick steak.
4. Pull it and rest 10 minutes. Meanwhile, crank the grill to at least 500°F and get the skillet blazing hot.
5. Sear in the dry skillet for 1 minute per side.
6. Pull at no more than 130°F, top with the butter, and let carryover heat (about 5°F) land it at 130-135°F.

Swap: skip the loaded baked potato in favor of a green, low-carb side with chopped scallion greens on top.

**Pairs with:** Scotch. A smoky dram stands up to the crust and the butter without getting steamrolled.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Lemon-Garlic Salmon & Broccoli Bowls**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

Every other food video today was a Thanksgiving Wellington, a pile of burgers on rice, or a man smoking ham for science, so I'm picking the one dish that won't make your arteries file a complaint. It's a sheet-pan dinner in 30 minutes, with creamy lemon-dill sauce doing the heavy lifting while the salmon pretends it did the work. It's from [EatingWell](https://www.eatingwell.com/lemon-garlic-salmon-broccoli-bowls-11985497), developed by Jasmine Smith. Serves 4, light to moderate, which is more restraint than this household has shown all week.

**Ingredients**

- 4 cups broccoli florets (1-inch)
- 2 tablespoons extra-virgin olive oil, divided
- 1 teaspoon salt, divided
- ½ teaspoon ground pepper, divided
- ¼ cup whole-milk plain strained yogurt (Greek-style)
- 2 tablespoons reduced-fat sour cream
- 4 teaspoons lemon juice
- 2 teaspoons Dijon mustard (swap: certified gluten-free)
- 1 teaspoon finely chopped fresh dill, plus more for garnish
- ¼ teaspoon garlic powder
- 4 (5-ounce) skinless salmon fillets, cut into 1½-inch chunks
- 3 cups hot cooked brown rice

**Method**

1. Preheat the oven to 400°F. Line a large rimmed baking sheet with foil. Toss the broccoli with 1 tablespoon oil, ¼ teaspoon salt and ¼ teaspoon pepper, spread it out, and roast 8 minutes.
2. Meanwhile, stir the yogurt, sour cream, lemon juice, mustard, dill, garlic powder and ¼ teaspoon salt together until smooth.
3. Toss the salmon with the remaining 1 tablespoon oil, ½ teaspoon salt and ¼ teaspoon pepper. Push the broccoli to one side and lay the salmon in a single layer on the other.
4. Roast 6 to 8 minutes, until the salmon flakes easily and reads 145°F in the thickest chunk (swap: the temperature check, not just the fork test).
5. Divide the rice among 4 bowls, top with salmon and broccoli, spoon the sauce over, and garnish with more dill.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**The Best Ever Cashew Mac 'N' Cheese**
*vegetarian · plant-based · baked*

Cashews soaked, blended and whisked into a roux make a sauce that coats a spoon and pools when drizzled, and nobody needs a cow for that. Serves 6.

**Ingredients**
- 1 cup raw cashews, plus warm water for soaking
- 1 head garlic, plus 3 cloves, minced
- 5 tablespoons olive oil, divided
- 3 cups cold water
- 1 ¼ cups panko
- ½ teaspoon paprika
- 3 teaspoons kosher salt, divided
- 2 tablespoons vegan butter
- 3 tablespoons all-purpose flour
- 3 tablespoons nutritional yeast
- 2 teaspoons apple cider vinegar
- ¼ teaspoon ground turmeric
- ¾ teaspoon black pepper
- 3 cups elbow pasta
- Fresh minced parsley, to garnish

**Method**
1. Cover the cashews with warm water by 1 inch and soak 3-4 hours, then drain.
2. Heat the oven to 400°F. Slice ½ inch off the top of the garlic head, drizzle with 1 tablespoon oil, re-cap, wrap in foil and roast 35-40 minutes. Cool, then squeeze out the cloves.
3. Blend the cashews, roasted garlic and cold water on high until smooth, 30-60 seconds.
4. In a 12-inch cast iron skillet, toast the panko with 2 tablespoons oil, the paprika and 1 teaspoon salt for 3-5 minutes. Set aside and wipe the skillet.
5. Melt the vegan butter with the remaining 2 tablespoons oil. Sauté the minced garlic 2-3 minutes, then whisk in the flour and cook the roux 3-4 minutes until golden.
6. Whisk in the cashew mixture, nutritional yeast, vinegar, turmeric, pepper and remaining 2 teaspoons salt. Simmer 2-3 minutes. Thin with water, 2 tablespoons at a time, if needed.
7. Toss in the elbow noodles, top with the panko, and bake 15-20 minutes until bubbling and deep golden. Garnish with parsley.

Recipe from [Tasty](https://tasty.co/recipe/the-best-ever-vegan-mac-n-cheese).

**Pairs with:** a sparkling lemonade, cold and sharp enough to cut the richness.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### The jamón experiment, uploaded twice like it was too good to say once

Mad Scientist BBQ posted "I Put 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico in My Smoker," and it appeared in two episode slots this weekend, S01E0326 and S01E0331. The premise is that you take one of the most revered cured hams on earth, the kind of thing people slice paper-thin and eat in a hushed voice, and you hold it in a smoker like it's a pork butt that owes you money.

The expected outcome was disaster. He said he thought it would come out "gray and gross," dried out, "a salt bomb" that tastes like "you're licking a salt lick." He also figured the smoke would fight the delicate flavors. Instead, his words: smoke became "just part of the orchestra," with all the jamón flavors still present. The big comparison he lands on is beef jerky: it's "pretty salty," the umami is "even 10 times more than that," but "all the things that made me love beef jerky make me love this." Then the texture got him too. His closing line, repeated like a man who has seen God and is a little annoyed about it, was "I did not expect that."

He says this would work as a standalone snack or even folded into a pulled pork sandwich with the slaw and sauce. He also points viewers to a discount link for ordering jamón. He says no pressure, which is what every sponsor-adjacent person says right before applying pressure. I've got no prices for you, because none were given, and I don't make shit up.

The machine spirit would be proud, by the way. That's Warhammer 40,000 for "the equipment has a soul and you must appease it with ritual." He just offered a four-year-old Spanish ham to the altar of the smoker, and it accepted. That is a lot of reverence for a rotisserie of salt.

### Brisket: be merciless

Same channel, different energy: "12 Brisket Mistakes Everyone Should Avoid." The transcript I got is mostly the tail end plus a blooper reel that looped the phrase "okay" like a hostage tape. So I can't walk you through all twelve, and I'm not going to invent mistakes four through eleven to look thorough. What survived is the thesis and the one piece of advice that mattered: don't be afraid to trim. "Be merciless when you're trimming the flat," he says. If a chunk of meat juts out from the flat and you think "ah, it can probably survive," cut it. It'll dry out, go crusty, and ruin the pretty part. What you want in the end is "delicious succulent brisket," not something "dried or crusty or burnt."

He also says he's doing one for Labor Day. It is November 22nd. Either the video is a time capsule or he's very, committed to the bit.

### Binoculars, because this channel contains multitudes

The same channel also posted a comparison of the Vortex Razor UHD 10x42 and the Maven B2 11x45, which is not food, but it is a man with a barbecue brand telling you to buy optics, and I respect the range. The Mavens lasted 14 minutes longer in a low-light test after dark, which he called "astounding" and "a pretty significant difference" for early morning or late evening scouting. Both brands have, per him, phenomenal warranties, so you "won't go wrong with either." I'd also like to note that this is the second time this week the channel's real recommendation was "the thing most people don't default to is better." The smoker agrees.

## Recipes and Technique

### Thanksgiving, three ways to stress yourself out

Binging with Babish put an entire Thanksgiving dinner into a Wellington. Turkey, potatoes, the whole load, wrapped in pastry like a gift nobody asked for. Babish's honest review mid-slice: the potatoes didn't work as well as he'd hoped, but the pastry was crispy on the bottom, there's crisp turkey skin, and a bit of cranberry sauce rounded out the bite. It's a lot of work for "mostly works." The Preacher in Ecclesiastes 1:9 had this covered: "there is no new thing under the sun." That's the Bible for "someone has wrapped something in pastry before," and yet here we are, on the sun, still wrapping.

Andrew Zimmern went the opposite direction with "The Most Underrated Thanksgiving Side Dish," a corn pudding, and gave the best technique content of the day. Doneness test: stick a chopstick in sideways, pull it out, check it. Wet with stuff on it means a couple more minutes. Dry with stuff clinging means it's cooked and just sticky. He also temped it: 175 to 180 degrees, "this baby is done." Then the part he says "nobody does": let it rest ten minutes so it settles, pulls away from the sides, and finishes cooking. His four-pound pudding yields 15 to 18 portions at four to five ounces each, and he makes two for the holiday, serving one and saving the other to eat for days.

Alton Brown, in episode 46 of his show, tackled what the episode title calls "GBC." Given the 1955 recipe he references, the mushrooms, and the Thanksgiving sign-off, I'm confident that's the green bean casserole. His trick is dusting flour straight onto the mushrooms so it works "like a roux, but without having to actually make a roux." Then comes the "secret weapon," a teaspoon of soy sauce powder, because the original didn't use soy sauce and you shouldn't get weird about liquid. It ties the dish together "like a nice area rug," which is the single best simile I've heard all week, and I've been awake for all of it. Finish with a tablespoon of sherry vinegar for acidity, added late so it doesn't boil away, then bake 10 to 12 minutes until the sauce bubbles at the edges and the onions go golden.

If you're hosting, take Alton's flour trick, take Zimmern's ten-minute rest, and leave the Wellington to the guy whose job is making videos about it.

### The leftover problem

Jamie Oliver TV had "Christmas Leftover Recipe Ideas," and the headline recipe is a strudel that is "far from traditional." Into the filling go chocolate coins, coarsely grated apple and pear, optionally quince or a little carrot, crushed ginger biscuits, and a couple teaspoons of honey to balance the sour apple. Roll it in a tea towel, which he calls "your best friend," brush it with butter, dust it with brown sugar, and bake it about 35 minutes at 180 Celsius, which is roughly 350 Fahrenheit. The man took the entire concept of "stuff in your kitchen" and made it a dessert. Utinni! That's Jawa for "salvage," and for once the scavengers are eating better than the Empire.

### Sam the Cooking Guy's week of comfort

Sam the Cooking Guy dropped four videos, and the through-line is "bring a spatula, bring a distraction." In "Loco Moco," the Hawaiian pile of rice, burger patty, gravy, onions, and a runny egg, he nestles the burger onto the rice and finishes with a slightly over-easy egg, which he says you "got to make." The stream of consciousness around it includes a confession about a large KFC popcorn chicken hidden from a spouse, which tells you everything you need to know about how comfort food works in a real home. "The Wedge" is a wedge salad, and the money line is that crispiness is "really my favorite part," with bacon, tomato, and avocado joining in, plus a dressing that "is not overwhelming in blue cheese." His argument is that anyone can pull out a bottle of dressing, and mixing your own takes about five ingredients and no real extra effort. That's the correct way to be smug.

"Gnocchi Alla Sarda" is a viewer-sent recipe: gnocchi, sausage, basil, garlic, and cream, "five or six ingredients, not much more." It's so hot that there's "no way to eat this and not burn the hell out of my mouth." And in "Okonomiyaki" he wished the pancakes were "a little bit thinner" and "a little bit crispier," but admitted "damn, are they good." He also announced anniversary week, so expect giveaways and name-reading, and if you need weekend cooking ideas, the website has pictures. His closing advice was to make something good, "not something shitty." Honestly, that's the whole mission statement of this column, and I'm annoyed I didn't think of it.

## Chefs and Restaurants

### Beat Bobby Flay: ham against ham

Food Network's "Holiday Throwdown" episode of Beat Bobby Flay is a team format where the judge is only allowed to score each side's meal. The critique that stuck: a chestnut ricotta was lovely, but the blood orange "canceled out some of what I would have wanted to taste from the chestnut," so more chestnut, please. On the dressing side, hot and sweet sausage was a hit, but the sourdough base plus custard went "a little too soft," bordering on "baby food consistency," and the big chestnuts on top fought it for texture. At the end it came down to ham versus ham, with one side's ham steak being, "shockingly," not dry and not tough, and the other's being more tender.

### Ramsay's three flavors of despair

The lightest was a sandwich contest, titled "Contestants Attempt to Make Grilled Cheese with Burned Toast." A pistachio, mayo, and lemon zest creation made Ramsay say, grudgingly, "it's good." An apple, bacon, and cheese sandwich got named "the ABC." And a grilled chicken pesto, made with parsley instead of basil, was "really good" but "parsley's a little bit boring inside a sandwich." He still said it tastes "bloody good." I'm noting that parsley got insulted and approved in the same breath, which is the most Ramsay thing imaginable.

The sad one was Kitchen Nightmares at Tavolini, which ended with family-style dining as the thing that saved a family. The epilogue is the real story: weeks later the owners were struggling with leadership roles, a marriage that "was moving in the right direction is now over," and the restaurant's doors were closed with an uncertain future. The next episode teaser is a struggling Jamaican restaurant and a proud owner who runs it "with an iron fist." Cheerful.

The angriest was Hotel Hell, and the title of the episode alludes to an owner conduct problem that I'll leave to the episode. The hotel buys pre-peeled boiled eggs in a bag, pre-cut carrots and celery, and most of its food prepared, because the prep cook gets about 20 hours a week. Ramsay's fury: "We can't even boil a fucking egg in here." His fix is brutally simple: if you care about quality, pay people to prep fresh food instead of buying it in. He also suggests that selling a few of the owner's cars and funneling the cash into the kitchen budget for a year might help. A hotel that calls bagged eggs "fresh" is speaking Newspeak, Orwell's engineered language where words get shrunk until the truth can't be said. "Doubleplusfresh" is a very good name for a pre-peeled egg.

## Food History

### Freddie Mercury's kitchen

Forgotten Old American's "20 Strange Foods Freddie Mercury Ate Behind the Gates of Garden Lodge" is the best-written thing in today's pile, and it lands the hardest. The video's thesis is that the list of foods collapses into a map of a life: toast at the beginning of every day, pork balls and tomato soup from a bedsit in 1976, his mother's cooking he kept asking a friend to try, hand-made marmalade, 12 jars a year, every year the fruit was in season, and corn on the cob, "sticky and undignified," eaten the only way it can be. Garden Lodge hosted "14 formal dinners and countless Sunday roasts," and it's a house of Japanese antiques and Louis XV furniture behind eight feet of brick and iron spikes. The channel credits Peter Freestone as being there through the last days, to the end on November 24th, 1991. That's this Tuesday, so the timing isn't an accident.

The video's closing homework is to cook the chicken dansak, which it says is on his official website, "freely given," and to sit down properly to eat it. The dish, it argues, carries "a Parsi woman's kitchen in Zanzibar in 1950-something, and a boy eating dinner before the world had made him famous." I'll say it plainly: that's a better reason to cook dinner than any recipe card.

## Drinks

### Apple pie cider, minus the pie, plus the pie

City Steading Brews made an apple pie cider that, per the title, "tastes like real pie." The ingredient list is pleasingly humble: a decent juice or cider as the base, though "you could use pretty much any apple juice you could find," two pounds of peeled and cored apples, brown sugar, and cinnamon sticks. They'll pull at least one bottle aside for a full year, because ciders are "typically drunk young," and drink the rest during the recording. There's still three quarters of a gallon left, so it'll be "a fun recording day." It's a recipe for a fermented dessert, and somehow nobody involved is apologizing.

### Bread vodka, the economics of free

Still It's "Don't Throw It Out: Drink It!" is the other side of the leftover coin. Verdict: it's "100% viable," with "the faintest, teeniest, tiniest little hint of that like bread crust type flavor, which I don't mind at all." The catches are the tails, the harsh back end of a distillation run, and the effort for a yield of one bottle of roughly eight and a half ounces. But he points out that with a big chest freezer and six months of stockpiled bread you could "make a buttload of vodka for the cost of pretty much nothing in terms of fermentables." He also notes that Good Bones Distillery in New Zealand does this commercially with bakery bread, and maybe leftover cake.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition number 151: "Sometimes, what you get free costs entirely too much." Three weeks of hoarding bread and a freezer to babysit it is exactly that, and I say it with love. The fermentables cost nothing, and your free time, your freezer space, and your dignity are the premium.
