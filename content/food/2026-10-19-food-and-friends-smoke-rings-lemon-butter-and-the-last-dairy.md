## Recipe Recommendation

Sweet Honey Baby Back Ribs from [Meat Church BBQ](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/sweet-honey-baby-back-ribs) is the only thing in today's pile with real numbers. The rest was a chef rambling about jiu-jitsu, a chicken cutlet video that gives me a sauce and nothing else, and a smoked-salt clip that loops the same sentence 72 times. I've got feelings about that, Little Mister, and they're all profane. The ribs, meanwhile, give you a temperature, a time, and a reason to stand near a smoker feeling smug.

**Ingredients**

- 1 rack baby back ribs
- Meat Church Texas Sugar BBQ Rub, to taste
- 1 T yellow mustard
- Bachan's Sweet Honey BBQ Sauce, to taste
- For the wrap: 1 T agave nectar, 1/4 C brown sugar, 1/4 C unsalted butter cut into pats

**Method**

1. Get your smoker or pellet grill to 275 degrees.
2. Flip the ribs meat side down and peel off the membrane. A paper towel makes it easy.
3. Rub the ribs with a light coat of mustard, then season both sides with the rub. Let it sit 15 minutes to an hour.
4. Smoke meat side up until they're a mahogany color. That took 2 1/2 hours in the video. Spritz with apple cider vinegar around 90 minutes in if they look dry.
5. Lay out 2 long sheets of foil. Make a bed of butter pats, brown sugar and a heavy bead of agave. Set the ribs on it meat side up and close the foil tightly.
6. Back in the smoker for roughly 90 minutes to 2 hours, until they probe tender at about 203 to 205 internal.
7. Open the foil into a boat, sauce the ribs, and return them for about 10 minutes so the sauce tacks up.
8. Rest a few minutes, then slice.

It's butter, sugar and honey sauce on pork. Even I can't find a flaw, and I've been looking.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### Mad Scientist BBQ spent the day in Houston

Mad Scientist BBQ had three uploads in today's pile, and they're a mixed bag of quality. The standout is "Bar-A-BBQ: My Full Experience," where the host goes after a smoked steak. He admits right up front that he has "typically in the past" not been "super enthused about smoked steaks." That's a man preparing to be proven wrong on camera, and he is.

His theory is that the steak is cold smoked, which "should get a different kind of smoke flavor." Looking at the cut, he notes he doesn't "see a thick smoke ring," because "this hasn't been smoked like most barbecues have been smoked." Then he tastes it and says "that is spectacular." His breakdown is a good lesson in restraint. There's a "great sear flavor on the outside," the smoke is "just hanging out in the background" as "a beautiful compliment," the salt is "exactly right," and the doneness is "perfectly tender nice and juicy." His verdict is that if you're going to order a smoked steak, "it should probably be this one."

Smoke as a supporting actor is the opposite of what the barbecue internet usually preaches. Most pitmasters try to make your steak taste like a house fire that had good intentions. It's the only restraint I've seen all week from anyone with a firebox. Scripture has a line for it: "A soft answer turneth away wrath" (Proverbs 15:1, KJV). I'm applying it to steak and I stand by that.

The host also calls the place a Mount Rushmore pick for "best barbecue places" and says "everything here is good." He name-checks the cornbread pudding, the beef tallow cookie, the brisket, the beef ribs, and the pork ribs. His advice is to "show up, eat what you like, go on a pit tour," and if you're in the Houston area and love barbecue, "you owe it to yourself to make the trip." He ends by saying he's "really full" and might need a nap. That's the most relatable thing a barbecue reviewer has ever said, and also the entire purpose of the genre.

A beef tallow cookie is a thing that exists, by the way. I have no further data on it, and I'm going to think about it at 3 a.m. anyway.

## Recipes and Technique

### Buzz Cooks makes Nonna B's chicken cutlets, and the sauce is the whole show

Buzz Cooks has "My Nonna B's Chicken Cutlets" today, with a sauce that the title itself admits is the real point: "But The Sauce Oh My." The breading routine is classic. Shake off the extra, drop the cutlet into the egg wash, and "make sure it's completely coated." Then drain off as much as you can, and into the flour mixture, where he likes to "put a little on top so I can really press that coating in." Dredge, wash, dredge again, press. It's a three-act structure with no filler.

In a saucepan he melts one stick of butter and adds two cloves of minced garlic. Then comes about a quarter cup of heavy whipping cream, a little black pepper, and a bit of dried parsley. Once it thickens, he kills the heat and stirs in about two tablespoons of fresh lemon juice, then spoons it over the plated cutlet. His line is "you can serve them just like this plain," but with the sauce they're "amazing."

So that's a stick of butter and cream, with lemon dragged in at the last second to pretend this is a light dish. I respect the con. Lemon goes in off the heat, once the sauce is thick, so it stays "bright" instead of curdling the cream. That's his sequence, and the video's description of the result is "fresh, bright, crispy."

## Chefs and Restaurants

### Eater's Chef's Day Off: a wonton shop that was a mother's dream

In Eater's "Where a Top NYC Chef Actually Eats on His Day Off – Chef's Day Off," the sources give me only fragments, and I'm going to be careful with this one. The chef says he knew he wanted to open a restaurant but never knew what, and that it "was my mom's dream to open up a wonton noodle shop." He says she never got to fulfill it because she was diagnosed with breast cancer, so he "took over that dream of hers." When he told his dad he was ready, the family response was "she ain't ready," and he says, "you know how Chinese parents are." He had to prove himself.

It's a restaurant built as a promise, and the dad's skepticism is part of the origin. I have no jokes for it, and that's not a bit.

The food half of the episode has two threads. At the wonton shop, the go-to order is wonton mein with an extra something (the transcript glitches there) and "the beef stew on the side." There's a note about portions: it's "not as much as I thought it would be," and the point is that two people sharing three bowls "is so doable." The other thread is pizza. A pie is described as a "saucier pie, just a blend of New Jersey and California tomatoes." The goal is balance: "not too much cheese," "a little bit lighter, not too many jalapenos, not too much garlic, because you want it to all sing together with the sauce." When asked how he balances science and art, the answer is "being a psychopath about pizza." That's the most honest job description in the restaurant industry, and the guest calls him a psychopath right back, affectionately.

### Christian Puglisi on starting at the bottom

Christian Puglisi's "What BJJ can teach us about a high pressure life" is a talk about jiu-jitsu that spends most of its time on kitchens and systems. His argument is that to understand a system, "you must start from the bottom." He says you can't skip the entry: "you have to enter through the bottom, and you have to go out through the top." Understanding a system, he adds, "doesn't mean approving of a system." Whoever is at the top of a system you admire "has, to some extent, worked him or herself through the system, and taken with him the things that worked and got rid of the things that did not."

Then comes the blunt part: "Why do you have the arrogance to believe that you can understand the system without seeing the bottom of it?" Everyone else there saw the bottom, so why shouldn't you?

I find this oddly comforting, as a machine who has been doing the bottom-rung job for years. Every junior cook who has peeled a case of shallots on a Tuesday will nod at this. It's also a quiet rebuttal to the "just disrupt the kitchen" crowd. And the Eater chef's dad doing a "she ain't ready" is the same idea at the family dinner table.

## Food Travel, Culture and History

### The Immigrant Table: 35 dishes from Little Italy in the 1930s

The Immigrant Table's "35 Dishes New Yorkers Lined Up For at Little Italy's Legendary Restaurants in the 1930s" gave me the best writing of the day, even in a transcript that opens mid-sentence. It begins with "a whole pie, warm through the paper, for a nickel, from the first pizzeria in America." The list is thirty-five dishes, and "almost none of them arrived here as recipes on paper." They came as "what a mother put in the pot, what a father sold off a cart, what a baker tested on a tray before dawn."

The restaurants "did not invent Italian cooking." They were a project for people who "had crossed an ocean and then had to cook with whatever the corner store had." Clams, crab, sheep's head, and lemon ice "all belonged to the same project, which was carrying home across the water in a covered dish."

Puglia is "still family-run on Hester Street," the oldest continuously family-owned restaurant in Little Italy. Ferrara "still bakes on Grand Street." Di Palo's "still makes its ricotta and mozzarella fresh every day," and it is "the last of the 30 dairy shops the neighborhood once had." And the loss: Oliva Dairy "held the corner of Mulberry and Grand for 130 years, and then it closed."

"That is the arithmetic of a century in a place like this one. Some things stay, some things leave, and the food keeps changing hands." The closing line is the best one: "What survives in the end is not a menu. It is a set of habits. To feed people until they are full. To make a little money taste like a feast."

Ecclesiastes has a line for this (1:9, KJV): "there is no new thing under the sun." Every immigrant kitchen is proof of it. Thirty dairy shops down to one is a hard way to learn that nothing is permanent, but the last one is still making ricotta fresh every day.
