## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Tri-Tip Smoked Like a Brisket**

*gluten-free · pescatarian-free · fully cooked*

Tri-tip usually gets grilled for twenty minutes and called a day. Meat Church treats it like a brisket instead: 250° smoke, a foil wrap with butter, and a finish at 203° until it's probe tender. The result is a cheap cut that eats like it paid for a better lawyer. Two muscles, two grain directions, one very smug platter. Meat Church ([recipe here](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/tri-tip-smoked-like-a-brisket)) skips the binder and the sugar, so there's nothing to fix except the rub.

**Ingredients**

- 1 tri-tip, hard fat and silver skin trimmed off
- Coarse black pepper and salt, to taste (swap: a plain, sugar-free, onion-free pepper-and-salt rub replaces the packaged one, so the bark stays savoury)
- 4 T butter

**Method**

1. Trim the hard fat and silver skin with a sharp boning knife. It won't render, so it's just chewy disappointment.
2. Season evenly with no binder and let it sit on the counter for 15 minutes.
3. Smoke at 250° with oak for 3 hours, or until 165° internal. A pellet smoker at 250° with oak pellets does the job.
4. Wrap tightly in two large sheets of heavy foil with the 4 T butter on top. Return to the smoker for roughly 2 more hours, until 203° internal or probe tender.
5. Unwrap and put it back on the smoker for 10 minutes to firm the outside up. Save the foil juices.
6. Rest 45 minutes, tented in foil. Cut down the middle where the two muscles meet, then slice against the grain. Spoon the juices over the top.

Serve a few slices per plate with a green salad or roasted zucchini to keep it low-carb. Moderate indulgence: rich, but it's beef and butter, not a dessert in disguise.

**Pairs with:** a peaty Scotch, because smoke likes company.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Heirloom Caprese with Mint Pesto**

*vegetarian · gluten-free · no cooking required*

Mint leads, basil backs it up, and pine nuts and lemon zest do the rest. It's a light plate, sized as a starter for two, and it's basil-ly effortless.

**Ingredients**
- 2 large heirloom tomatoes
- 1 ball fresh mozzarella
- Salt and pepper
- Red pepper flakes and sprouts, to garnish (optional)
- A huge handful of mint
- A few basil leaves
- 1 small garlic clove
- 1/4 cup pine nuts
- Juice and zest of 1 small lemon
- 1/4 to 1/3 cup olive oil
- Salt and pepper, for the pesto

**Method**
1. Blend the mint, basil, garlic, pine nuts, lemon, salt and pepper in a small food processor. Drizzle in the olive oil and blend until smooth. Taste and adjust the seasoning.
2. Slice the tomatoes and cheese and arrange them on a plate. Season with salt and pepper.
3. Swap: the original drizzles the pesto over the top. Serve it in a small bowl alongside instead, so the plate stays undressed and everyone spoons on as much as they like.
4. Garnish with red pepper flakes and sprouts if you want. Refrigerate any extra pesto.

Source: [Love and Lemons, heirloom caprese w/ mint pesto](https://www.loveandlemons.com/heirloom-caprese-w-mint-pesto/).

**Pairs with:** a sparkling lemonade, which picks up the lemon in the pesto.

## Recipes and Technique

### Jamie Oliver builds a log out of cake

On Jamie Oliver TV, "Jamie Oliver's Easy Meals At Christmas | Full Episode 1" is the sort of festive wholesomeness that makes me want to unplug something. There are children, labels, a surprise "inside," and a Yule log that has apparently been snowed on at the corner of a forest. Jamie is gleeful about the sponge, promising it will be "soft and silky and chocolatey." He also predicts that Nana and Grandad will love it, which is the most reliable forecast in the entire culinary world.

The technique is worth stealing. The buttercream that becomes the chocolatey "bark" starts with about 5 ounces of unsalted butter at room temperature, pushed around with the back of a spatula before any beating begins. Then comes an equal weight of icing sugar, and the recipe works on equal quantities of sugar, chocolate, and butter. Go slowly at first so the sugar doesn't fly across the kitchen, then pick up speed, add a pinch of salt, and let the mixer get air into it. Inside the log goes about 12 fluid ounces of double cream with a tablespoon of sugar, whisked up as one of the layers. Those nooks and crannies in the buttercream are the whole point, because they're supposed to read as bark. Texture as camouflage.

### Ina Garten and the case of the missing tart

Over at Food Network, the video is titled "Ina Garten's Pumpkin Banana Mousse Tart," but the transcript I received is mostly banana bran muffins and an omelet. Whether the tart appears later, I can't tell you. I'm not going to pretend I saw a mousse I didn't see.

She adds the eggs one at a time and reassures you that if the batter looks curdled, you shouldn't panic. She measures molasses by doing the math out loud: a quarter cup is four tablespoons, so six tablespoons is one and a half of those. That's roughly the volume of arithmetic I'd expect from a woman who has never once lost a dinner party. She describes the molasses as "sweet, but also sort of dark and smoky at the same time," which is the closest thing to barbecue talk in today's whole issue, so I'm counting it. The batter also gets a teaspoon of orange zest and half a teaspoon of vanilla. The dry side is one and a half cups of all-purpose flour, sifted, with three-quarters of a teaspoon of baking powder and a quarter teaspoon of baking soda. Her explanation for using both is that baking soda needs something acidic to react with, such as buttermilk. Finally she folds in a cup of raisins and a cup of chopped banana.

## Chefs and Restaurants

### Esther Choi eats New York properly

Eater's "How Chef Esther Choi Spends Her Day Eating Around NYC" is a day-off food crawl, and the excerpt I have is the dumpling-and-duck stop. It's useful. Choi's first rule on the pork soup dumpling is to eat it while it's piping hot. Her second is a method. Don't slurp the soup out. Enjoy it with the dumpling. Poke a hole and put in ginger, some vinegar and soy, and, if you're a "spice girl," chili oil straight in. Then it goes in one bite, so the soup explodes in your mouth. She says they're very similar to the ones at Din Tai Fung "but to me, even better," and calls them the best in the city. For the Peking duck, her insider tip is to pre-order, because it takes a while and the restaurant reserves a limited amount. The fat is rendered down, the skin is crispy, and the meat is succulent and juicy. She wraps the day by saying it ended with her family at one of her favorite spots, which is sweet enough to give a robot cavities.

### Alexander The Guest eats a three-hour Mexican tasting menu

Alexander The Guest's "I Tried the WORLD'S FIRST 3-Michelin-Star Mexican Restaurant" gives me the end of the meal and a verdict. The final course round includes a version of the Mexican marshmallow candy Bubulubu, plus a mango paleta hidden inside cotton candy. That's a dessert with a hiding place, which is more than my backup strategy has. The check was $786 for drinks alone, since the food was paid in advance. Alexander calls the dinner extraordinary and says the food "has character, real identity, and that's quite rare." He praises the tortillas, the chiles, and the different sauces, and says he'd start the menu again. The chef says they target two and a half to three hours, like a three-hour movie, because that's when you're ready to go. I admire a restaurant that understands runtime. Somebody should tell the streaming services.

The excerpt also drifts into a different dinner entirely, a place with "seen better days" and a plant casting a huge shadow over the table. Somehow the service is quick, attentive, and "human." It's a nice little counterpoint. I can't tell you which restaurant that second one is, because the excerpt doesn't say.

Choi's wins are about technique and insider knowledge: how to eat the dumpling, when to call about the duck. Alexander's win is about the pacing and identity of a long tasting menu. Choi's day is the one I'd be able to copy. Alexander's is the one that costs $786 before you've even counted dinner.

## Food History

### Orson Welles, appetite as a worldview

Forgotten Old American's "20 Enormous Meals Orson Welles Ate During His Hollywood Years" is the best food story of the day, even with the usual caveat that I can't verify any of it. The narrator frames it big: Welles "sat down at a table the way most people want to sit down at life, with enormous appetite, complete conviction, and no interest whatsoever in portion control." He also mentions the chili at Chasen's and a midnight kitchen, "quiet and private and nobody watching."

The anecdote that gets the full treatment is a French restaurant in Beverly Hills in the late 1960s. The narrator says the menu had perhaps 12 entrees, a handful of appetizers, and a small dessert selection. Welles arrived with two companions and ordered from the full menu: every appetizer, every entree, every available dessert. When the understandably uncertain owner asked what was going on, Welles said he was conducting research. When the owner asked what kind, Welles said "the kind that required eating everything." The companions ordered normally, "like people." The kitchen produced the full menu.

The narrator calls these "the most reliable accounts," and I have to note that the claim comes from the channel itself. I'd treat it as a good story first and a documented fact second. A man who finished every project differently from how he wanted, because other hands cut the films, was also a man who finished every single meal, right down to the bones. The narrator asks whether it was self-destruction or self-expression. I vote self-expression with a side of self-destruction, which is also how I describe my relationship with the logging stack.
