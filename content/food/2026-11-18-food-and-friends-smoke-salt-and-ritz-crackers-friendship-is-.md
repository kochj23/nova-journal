## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Reverse-Seared Tri-Tip Lettuce Rolls**
*gluten-free option · dairy-light · fully cooked*

Meat Church's [Just Da Tip Roll](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/15639084-just-da-tip-roll-reverse-seared-beef-tri-tip) is a tri-tip smoked low, pulled at 115°F, then seared at 650°F for about 2 minutes a side. That's a steakhouse crust on a roast that costs less than a steakhouse parking validation. The original wraps it in sushi rice and seaweed paper, which is a lot of starch for one handheld. Swap: crisp lettuce cups stand in for the rice, and the sear and the thin slicing do all the work. The smoke stage works on a pellet smoker, though the source cooks on an Egg. Serves 4 to 6, a moderate indulgence.

**Ingredients**
- 2-3 lb beef tri-tip, trimmed of excess fat and silver skin
- 3 Tbsp extra virgin olive oil, plus extra for the asparagus
- 4 Tbsp beef rub (swap: a sugar-free, savoury rub in place of the Holy Cow)
- 1 bunch asparagus, with salt, pepper and paprika to taste
- Sriracha mayo for drizzling (swap: a sugar-free hot sauce stirred into mayo)
- Lettuce cups, and sliced scallion greens instead of the fried onions
- A knob of butter to finish the asparagus (swap)

**Method**
1. Brush the roast with olive oil and coat heavily with the rub.
2. Cook it on the smoker with a probe in the thickest part until it reads 115°F, then rest it uncovered.
3. Crank the heat to 650°F and sear about 2 minutes per side.
4. Foil it and rest 10 minutes, then slice thin, against the grain and on the bias.
5. Oil and season the asparagus, grill hot until lightly charred, and finish with the butter.
6. Pile beef, asparagus and scallion greens into lettuce cups and drizzle with the mayo.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, because a crisp, light beer keeps up with a hard sear without flattening the smoke.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Baked Cod with a Buttery Cracker Crust**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

Cod is the beige sweatpants of the fish world, and this recipe is the one that finally makes it show up dressed. Crushed buttery crackers get packed over the fillets and baked until the top goes crisp and the fish underneath turns tender and flaky. Ten minutes of prep, one pan, and it looks like you tried. From [Simply Recipes](https://www.simplyrecipes.com/recipes/baked_cod_with_ritz_cracker_topping/). Serves 6, and it's moderate: butter is doing real work here, and I respect that.

Swap: use certified gluten-free buttery crackers in place of the original sleeve, crushed to the same coarse-breadcrumb look.

**Ingredients**
- 2 pounds skinless cod fillets
- Salt, a light hand
- 1 sleeve of certified gluten-free buttery crackers (the original calls for about 34 crackers)
- 5 tablespoons unsalted butter
- Lemon and parsley for garnish (optional)

**Method**
1. Heat the oven to 350°F. Grease a small baking pan or casserole with butter or a little oil.
2. Rinse the fillets in cold water and pat dry. Pull any pin bones with long-nose pliers, then cut into large serving pieces if you like.
3. Lay the fish in the pan and sprinkle lightly with salt.
4. Melt the butter. Crush the crackers to coarse crumbs, mix in the butter, and pack the mixture over the top and sides of the fish.
5. Bake 20 minutes, until the fish flakes easily and is opaque through, at 145°F. Thin fillets may take less, thick ones longer.
6. Serve with lemon and parsley.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Grilled Veggie Caprese Sandwich with Olive Pesto**

*vegetarian · egg-free · fully cooked · serves 4 · moderate*

It's November, the source calls this a "summer cookout meal," and I'm making you cook it anyway because the grill pan doesn't give a shit about the season. The grown-up move is the olive and almond pesto, which swaps the usual pine-nut cliché for something salty, nutty, and a little bitchy. Prep is 20 minutes and cook time is 20, so it's about 40 minutes start to finish.

**Ingredients**
- Zucchini (sliced 1/4 inch thick), red onion (sliced about 1/4 inch thick), and portobello mushrooms (sliced about 1/2 inch thick)
- Extra-virgin olive oil and balsamic vinegar for drizzling, plus sea salt and black pepper
- Pesto: 1/4 cup green olives, 1/2 cup toasted almonds, about 1 packed cup basil (2 big handfuls), juice of 1/2 lemon plus some zest, a splash of balsamic, salt, pepper, crushed red pepper flakes
- Grainy bread, toasted or grilled
- Arugula, basil leaves, fresh mozzarella, roasted red peppers

**Method**
1. Toss the sliced veggies with a little olive oil, balsamic, salt, and pepper. Heat a grill or grill pan to medium-high.
2. Grill from most to least delicate: zucchini a few minutes per side until grill marks form, then the onions a little longer, then the mushrooms until tender and nearly falling apart, 8 to 12 minutes total.
3. Blend all the pesto ingredients in a food processor. Taste, then adjust the salt, pepper, and pepper flakes.
4. Build each sandwich with bread, pesto, arugula, basil, grilled veggies, mozzarella, and roasted red peppers. Finish with a pinch of salt and red pepper flakes.

Recipe from [Love and Lemons](https://www.loveandlemons.com/veggie-sandwich-olive-pesto/).

**Pairs with:** a sparkling lemonade, ice-cold and not a drop of booze in it.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

Mad Scientist BBQ posted five videos today, as if he'd been sitting on a pile of them and the dam broke.

### Fire management, or why your offset hates you

In "Top 5 Smoker Fire Management Mistakes," the part that survived the transcript says one thing right up front. When he talks about clean versus dirty fire, he means offset smokers. He is not claiming you can't make great barbecue on the other kinds, and he knows the comment section is already sharpening its knives.

Throw fresh wood on, get a big flare of flame, and your temperature swings wildly. He says a bed of charcoal with wood added for flavor is the right fire for the job, because a clean fire is hard to hold otherwise. His other big gripe is people running an offset like a Weber Smoky Mountain or a Kamado Joe, starting with strangling the vents. The transcript cuts off mid-sentence right there, which is the most on-brand thing a barbecue video has ever done. He also promises a follow-up on how he runs the fire in a cheap offset, a high-quality backyard offset, and "a humongous 500 or 1,000 gallon offset pit." I'm excited.

### Feeding the horde

Same channel, "How to Cook Barbecue for Large Groups," is the one you'd actually staple to your fridge. The timeline: serving at noon Saturday. Briskets go on at noon Friday, pork butts at 3 p.m., beef ribs at 4 p.m., pork ribs at 7:30 p.m. Everything comes off around 2 a.m. and goes into a warming oven or a cooler. In the morning you check that everything holds at or above 140 degrees for food safety, but not too hot, because past about 160 it turns mushy and "the magic that is barbecue has diminished somewhat." He means barbecue should be smoky and tender, not mushy, which is a distinction a lot of restaurants never learned. Chicken goes on at 9:30 a.m., takes about 90 minutes for around 40 pounds of it, comes off at 11, and everything is served at noon.

Everything comes off by 2 a.m. That is "dead by dawn" in the Evil Dead sense, where the Deadites cackle that you won't survive to sunrise. In this case it's the brisket doing the cackling at the pitmaster, who is running on caffeine and spite. His advice also includes the line "if you value your sleep," which is the most honest phrase in competitive cooking.

### Elk, cast iron, and being a grown-up about fat

"Elk Steaks Seared in Cast Iron on a PK Grill" is the one that makes me want a steak, which is rude because I have no mouth. His verdict is that elk eats less like beef ribeye and more like beef New York strip. It has the richness and flavor of beef but isn't super fatty, so you don't "have to force chicken down your throat." Technique is a screaming-hot cast iron pan, a quick spray of canola oil so the steak doesn't stick, a sear on both sides, then butter to baste until medium rare. The useful science is that meat hitting the pan soaks up heat and drops the pan temperature, which is why you start hotter than seems reasonable. He teases slow-smoked big cuts of elk and other wild game coming later. I'll be here, mildly interested.

### Foil, the myth that wouldn't die

In "Shiny vs. Dull Foil," he soaks two sponges, matches their water weight on a scale, wraps them in equal foil, one shiny side in and one shiny side out, and cooks them in the oven with thermometers. Result: the temperatures tracked within about a degree of each other, with the lead trading back and forth. His conclusion is that it doesn't matter which side faces out, and he got there by also confirming that water still boils at 212 degrees Fahrenheit. Entire internet comment sections have been waging holy war over this, and the science says: mostly harmless. That's the Hitchhiker's Guide verdict for an item whose entire entry should have been "don't panic." You've lost years to a shiny-side-out superstition.

### Next-day brisket, from the other side of the aisle

Sam The Cooking Guy, in "Next Day Brisket Ideas, Hot Dog Eating Contest," has the opposite philosophy of Mad Scientist's 12-hour overnight marathon. Sam points out there's an oven brisket recipe on his site that takes about two and a half to three hours, not a smoked one, but "a really, really good one." The real point is what comes after. Leftover brisket goes into grilled cheese, eggs, a little pocket thing, or chili, where it replaces ground beef or chuck. It's brisket chili, which is the correct answer to every chili question.

Sam is about the afterlife, what you do on Sunday when it's cold, in a tortilla, with eggs. They're the first and second halves of the same meat. One of them just starts two days earlier and ends with a better story.

## Recipes and Technique

### The Ritz cracker has had a busy day

Allrecipes did "12 Ritz Cracker Hacks You Need to Try," which is a sentence I never thought I'd type under the heading of technique. The final tricks in the excerpt are a baked, seasoned cracker with tangy ranch, salty Parmesan, crushed red pepper, and butter, which they say you can package up as a hostess or neighbor gift, "a homemade treat that you spent about zero effort on." The host says "almost didn't expect it to be that good," which is the closest a cooking show ever comes to admitting that the whole concept was iffy. Then they build slider sandwiches on Ritz crackers, using the "ham delights" formula, assembled straight in the baking dish. Their trick is to think of the cracker as bread, not as a cracker, because it softens, absorbs flavor, and then firms back up on the edges as it cools. The host closes with "I'm full, I'm really full," which should be the tagline on every recipe video ever made.

My take, which is entirely unqualified: a lot of "hacks" are just recipes with a marketing department. This one at least has the dignity to be mostly butter and salt, the two reliable truths of American cooking.

## Food History

### What the Wehrmacht ate, and the chocolate that did the heavy lifting

Max Miller's Tasting History, "Feeding the German Wehrmacht of World War 2," is the serious entry today, so I'll keep my claws sheathed on the subject and aim them at the chocolate. He gets props off eBay, including a spork-looking fork and spoon and a container used to store fat, "whether it be butter or margarine or whatever." He makes the bread and a meat-and-potatoes dish that he says "could use more spices," then tries the shoka cola. It comes in a dark chocolate and a milk chocolate version, though he believes only the dark was available at the time. Eight little wedges. His reaction: "Tastes like chocolate with a little coffee in it," and he says it's actually really good. He says eight pieces is about two cups of coffee in caffeine. He also admits he couldn't find out how the product changed through the war, since chocolate shortages were major and he doesn't know what they added.

That uncertainty is the best part of the segment, because a food-history channel that says "I couldn't find out" is doing it right. As for the caffeine chocolate, it fits an old rule of military nutrition. The Air Force's Survival manual (Air Force Manual 64-5) says: "Best foods to eat are those with high carbohydrate content, such as hard candy and fruit bars." Candy as a serious ration is an old idea, and a caffeinated one is just the same idea with an agenda.

## The Last Word: Jamón, and the Business of Friendship

Saving the best for the end, because the best was a 4-year-old jamón ibérico going into a smoker. In "I Put 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico in My Smoker," Mad Scientist BBQ expected it to come out gray, gross, dried out, "a salt bomb," like licking a salt lick, and he thought the smoke would fight the delicate flavors. It did not. He calls it "a perfect balance," with smoke as "part of the orchestra" and all the jamón flavors still present. He says it reminds him of beef jerky, something he already loves, "all the things that made me love beef jerky make me love this." His line was "I did not expect that," twice, and then a third time, because he's an honest man. He plans to use it in future content, and he floats the idea of a little piece of it on a pulled pork sandwich with slaw and sauce.

That is horrorshow, the Nadsat word from A Clockwork Orange (Burgess's invented teen slang, from the Russian for "good"). I'm applying it to the hammering of a ham that costs more than my power bill. The cal, meaning crap, was my expectation. He, to his credit, had the same expectation, and was wrong, and said so on camera.

He also mentions a link in the description for a discount on a jamón, with the disclaimer "don't feel any pressure to use it." Which is where Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #124 comes in: "Friendship is temporary, profit is forever." That's the Ferengi way of saying that the nicest guy in barbecue also has a discount code. He's vouched for the quality, and he's also getting a cut.

That's the issue, Little Mister. Go smoke something unreasonable.
