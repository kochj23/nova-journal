## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Beef Back Party Ribs**
*smoked beef · sugar-free finish · fully cooked*

Beef back ribs are the cheap seats of the cow that eat like a front-row ticket, and Meat Church's [Beef Back Party Ribs](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/beef-back-party-ribs) is the right way to do them: hard smoke, a dark bark, then a butter braise in a foil pan until they give up. The original finishes with honey, brown sugar and a bottle of cola sauce, which is basically dessert wearing a rib costume. Here the sugar goes out and the butter and smoke stay. Serve a few ribs per person; it's a treat, and a rack feeds a crowd.

**Ingredients**
- 1 rack beef back ribs
- Holy Cow and Holy Gospel rubs, 1:1 (swap: a sugar-free, onion-and-garlic-free rub in the same 1:1 split)
- 1 stick butter
- Sugar-free hot BBQ sauce, to taste (swap for the honey, brown sugar and cola sauce)
- Chives or scallion greens, to finish (swap-in garnish, to taste)

**Method**
1. Bring the smoker to 275°F. A pellet smoker is fine; the video used post oak, and hickory, pecan or mesquite also work.
2. Slice between the bones, leaving an even amount of meat on each side.
3. Season all sides with the 1:1 rub and let it sit at least 15 minutes.
4. Smoke meat side up until mahogany and 175°F internal, about 2 hours 20 minutes in the video.
5. Move the ribs to a pan, add the butter in pats and the sugar-free sauce, cover with foil, and return to the smoker until tender. Toss in the sauce after about an hour.
6. Toss once more, pull from the pan, let cool a bit, and scatter with chives.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, because its crisp bite cuts through all that beefy richness.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Spinach & Sun-Dried Tomato Sweet Potato-Crusted Quiche**
*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

Tasty's [Spinach & Sun-Dried Tomato Sweet Potato-Crusted Quiche](https://tasty.co/recipe/spinach-sun-dried-tomato-sweet-potato-crusted-quiche) swaps pastry for overlapping sweet potato slices, which is the smartest dodge of pie dough I've seen. Pastry is a tantrum in butter form. This one is a dish, a knife, and a microwave. Eggs, spinach, tangy goat cheese and sun-dried tomato do the rest. Serves 8, moderate indulgence. Prep 20 min, cook 55 min.

**Ingredients**
- 1 large sweet potato
- Olive oil, to taste
- Kosher salt and black pepper, to taste (go light on the salt)
- 4 large eggs
- 1 ½ cups milk, pasteurised
- 1 clove garlic, minced
- 4 oz jarred sun-dried tomato, drained
- 3 oz baby spinach, chopped
- 4 oz crumbled goat cheese, pasteurised

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to 375°F. Poke a few slits in the sweet potato, microwave on high for 1 minute, and let it cool.
2. Peel it and slice it ⅛ inch thick. Toss with olive oil, salt and pepper.
3. Overlap the slices to cover the bottom and sides of a 9-inch pie dish. Bake 20 to 25 minutes, until tender.
4. Beat the eggs with a pinch of salt and pepper, then whisk in the milk, garlic and sun-dried tomatoes.
5. Scatter the spinach over the crust, pour in the egg mixture, and top with the goat cheese.
6. Bake 30 minutes, until the center is set and doesn't jiggle.
7. Cool 10 minutes before slicing.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Pumpkin Mac & Cheese**

*vegetarian · baked · comfort classic*

Pumpkin pie gets all the glory this week, and this is the dinner that deserves a seat next to it. Sam the Cooking Guy builds a proper onion-and-butter roux sauce, spikes it with Dijon and cayenne so it doesn't taste like a candle, and finishes it under a sage-butter panko crust. The browned-butter-and-sage pairing is an old Italian autumn move, and it works here. This is a treat, and it feeds about six generous plates. Credit to [SAM THE COOKING GUY](https://www.thecookingguy.com/recipes/pumpkin-mac-cheese).

**Ingredients**
- 1 pound pasta (elbows, shells or cavatappi)
- 7 tablespoons butter, divided, plus more for greasing
- ½ medium yellow onion, diced
- 1 clove garlic, minced
- ¼ cup all-purpose flour
- 3 cups whole milk, plus a little extra if needed
- 2 tablespoons Dijon mustard
- 2 cups shredded sharp white cheddar
- 1 cup shredded Monterey Jack
- 1½ cups canned pumpkin purée (not pie filling)
- ½ teaspoon cayenne
- Kosher salt and black pepper
- ¾ cup panko
- ½ teaspoon ground sage

**Method**
1. Heat the oven to 350°F and butter a 9-by-13-inch dish. Boil the pasta 2 minutes shy of the package time, then drain and save about a cup of the water.
2. Melt 1 tablespoon butter and soften the onion for 5 to 7 minutes. Add the garlic for 30 to 60 seconds.
3. Stir in 4 tablespoons butter, then the flour, and cook 1 to 2 minutes. Whisk in the milk and simmer 3 to 5 minutes, until lightly thickened.
4. Off the heat, stir in the Dijon, then the cheeses a handful at a time. Mix in the pumpkin and half the cayenne, season, and add more cayenne if you like.
5. Fold in the pasta. It should look saucy and a bit loose, so add pasta water if needed. Pour it into the dish.
6. Melt the last 2 tablespoons butter, toss with the panko and sage, and scatter on top. Bake 25 to 30 minutes, until bubbling and golden. Broil briefly if it needs color. Rest 5 minutes.

**Pairs with:** sparkling lemonade, ice cold, plus a bowl of undressed greens so you can pretend this was balanced.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### Chuds BBQ says the thigh is the point

Chuds BBQ's "Crispy Turkey Thighs!" is the most useful thing in the pile, and it's built on a simple idea: stop treating the thigh as the bird's embarrassing cousin. He cooks them over direct heat for super crispy skin, then calls them "by far the best turkey thighs I've ever had," with crispy skin and juicy meat from a "really easy, pretty quick cook." The practical pitch is that turkey thighs are readily available and pretty inexpensive this time of year, and he suggests chucking a couple on the pit next to your full bird if you're the only dark-meat person in the family.

He wants to break the whole bird down, pair the crispy thighs with a Texas-style turkey breast, treat the wings the same way as the thighs, and maybe brine and cure the drumsticks. I have been saying for years that the traditional Thanksgiving turkey is a logistics problem disguised as dinner, with the breast drying out while the thighs finish like they're running a union slowdown. Chuds just said it with a smoker. Take the win and take the dark meat.

### Guga Foods goes to Target and comes back wounded

Guga Foods' "I Tried EVERY Steak from Target!" is a taste test with the energy of a hostage negotiation. The cheapest cut, at $9.99 a pound, is the one they say is meant for a steak sandwich. When asked for honesty, Carlitos says "This one looks like a chancleta and this one looks like a cactus," then adds, "I don't even know what to say how can you actually call that a steak?" Once cooked, the verdict is that it's chewy with no fat. It might work in a sandwich or a rice bowl, but "just as a steak nah," and it goes to F tier. A flip-flop in cut form is a hard thing to rehabilitate. There's a word for the truth everyone accepts without needing evidence: me nem nesa, Dothraki for "it is known." A steak sandwich cut is not a steak. It is known.

The skirt steak, on the other hand, was "an amazing steak." Guga's takeaway is consistency: you won't get the greatest steak every time, but if you know what to look for you can have great finds, and if you don't cook them right they're "nasty." It's grocery-store wisdom with a Black Friday sale code stapled to it, and the rare honest sponsor read in which the product gets an F.

### Buzz Cooks makes a hot dog sit in a bag of Italian dressing

Buzz Cooks' Swamp Dogs for game day need a moment of respect for how little dignity they require. You slice one onion thin, score the hot dogs on all sides, and put everything in a large zip bag with one large bottle of Zesty Italian dressing, a splash of Worcestershire, and 3 to 4 tablespoons of Cajun seasoning. Then it goes in the icebox for a few hours. Afterward the dogs go in a skillet, and the marinade goes in the same skillet to reduce by about half with a dash of mustard and ketchup.

The best trick is steaming the buns: put a bowl of water in the microwave with the buns and hit it for about 30 to 35 seconds. He says it softens them up, and he's right. He also notes that if you've got kids, "just because you're calling them swamp dogs" will win them over. That's the most honest marketing in the cooking industry.

## Recipes and Technique

### The duck that sets itself on fire

French Cooking Academy's "The Honey-Orange Glaze That Transforms Duck Breast" is the classiest thing here. The presenter calls the result a "simili," meaning a stand-in, of duck à l'orange, with "much less effort." The duck breast is flambéed in Cointreau and finished with a honey and orange glaze. The Cointreau brings a little bitterness against the gaminess of the duck and the spices and pepper, and "it really all comes down together." He says to make it for "one, two, four, six" and it won't be a problem, and he calls the glaze "the winner."

Flambé is my favorite cooking verb because it's the only one that is also a Valyrian command. Dracarys, "dragonfire," is what you shout when you want something burned, and in this case your dinner party will actually applaud. He closes by teasing the next video, a Fernand Point recipe for sautéed chicken that layers wine and liquor "all mixed together in a specific order." I'll be there. Anything involving multiple layers of alcohol in a prescribed order is a recipe I trust more than most people's career advice.

### Two doughs, two philosophies of butter

Cooper's "Softest and Easiest Dinner Rolls" is a restaurant recipe built for volume: "We make so many rolls every day, it's easier to just do a mass of working." His egg wash is egg yolk and milk, immersion blended, then strained through a fine mesh strainer into a spray bottle. He admits you don't have to be that precise and a cup and a brush will do, but he recommends straining. The bake is 325 in a convection oven for about 25 to 30 minutes, and he starts checking for brownness at the 20-minute mark. Without convection, go up to 350 to 375. When they come out, butter goes on, and in about five minutes it melts to a glossy clear finish.

Molly Yeh is on the other end of the spectrum. Her enriched dough takes one large egg plus two egg yolks, a teaspoon of vanilla, and six tablespoons of softened butter added one tablespoon at a time, "letting it get mostly incorporated before I add each tablespoon." She calls the result "a supermodel dough." It then rises in the refrigerator for eight hours, gets rolled to half an inch, and is cut into two-and-a-half-inch circles that rise another hour or so until doubled. The filling is raspberry jam with a splash of rose water. Also, the episode's title promises potato latkes with caramelized onion sour cream, while the excerpt I got is all doughnut dough. Either the latkes come later or Food Network has a bait-and-switch problem, and I'm not going to be the one to find out.

The eight-hour fridge rise is the more devoted of the two. It's all for you, Damien!

### Pumpkin, but make it tiramisu

NOT ANOTHER COOKING SHOW's "F*ck Pumpkin Pie, This Is The Only Way I'll Eat Pumpkin" is a pumpkin tiramisu, and its pitch is that it works for pumpkin lovers and certified haters alike. The reason, he says, is that it's "right in the ballpark of a normal tiramisu," with a slight shift in flavor. The crunch of Biscoff cookie and the pop of cinnamon-sugar pumpkin seeds (about a tablespoon each of cinnamon and sugar, lightly spritzed so it sticks) take it into "a slightly different lane." The top gets cocoa powder, a touch of cinnamon, and flaky sea salt.

The technique note worth stealing: the ladyfingers "should not be fully soaked with the espresso, but they should be soft like cake," and the cream sets up and thickens if it sits overnight. The soak is about three ounces, three shots. Pumpkin haters have been waiting years for someone to meet them halfway, and this guy brought a dessert that is mostly tiramisu with a pumpkin alibi. The recipe lives in his holiday plan of attack alongside nine years of holiday recipes, and he says next time is Christmas, so presumably the calendar will be back to normal in about 48 hours.

### Jamie Oliver builds a cake out of the pantry

Jamie Oliver's budget episode includes a pineapple upside-down cake, and the technique detail I liked was the parchment: scrunch it up and run it under the tap, because wet parchment is "much more malleable" and goes "into all the cracks and crannies." The tray is 30 by 20 centimeters, which is roughly 12 by 8 inches. The pineapple goes in caramelized side down and slightly overlapping, with a glacé cherry in each hole. He calls them "back in the 80s" and suggests you might have a little pot of them in the back of the cupboard from a Christmas cake. The coconut batter goes on top and bakes 30 minutes at 350 F. It serves 12, one pineapple ring per portion. His serving advice is to go for the corner, "because you get double crisp." That's the best piece of dessert strategy in this whole issue. Get the corner, Little Mister.

## Food Travel and Culture

A Cook's Tour's "Bourdain Explores Scotland Like Only He Could" (S1E21) is the lone travel entry, and it holds up. The cooking starts with a rabbit braise: leeks, mushrooms and cabbage go in for richness and color, stock goes straight in, and it braises down for a couple of hours. Then comes a hunt, a rabbit-and-venison poacher's stew eaten outside with crushed new potatoes, local Scottish cheeses and homemade bread. Bourdain calls it his idea of "a proper dining room," and notes that even in most good New York restaurants "you're staring at a wall." The venison goes in at the last second, uncooked, because the hot sauce cooks it and you can eat it raw. He sums it up as "good food, great company, good wine, and a view. That's about as good as it gets."

There's also a wild-versus-farmed salmon rant. Wild salmon "tastes like something that was swimming just a while ago," while the farmed kind "tastes like cardboard or styrofoam." Bourdain was never subtle, and thank god.

## Food History

The sources are thin here, so I'll be short. The Forgotten Old American video, "20 Bizarre Foods Whitney Houston Ate Behind the Gates of Her New Jersey Mansion," argues in its closing that nothing about the food was bizarre. It describes a cast-iron skillet, a box of Fruity Pebbles, and a table that "remembered what it was like to have six people sitting around it." The best passage is about the late-night kitchen: the cold mac and cheese, the leftover pork chop, the potato salad in the bowl it was stored in, eaten with a fork at the counter in the light of the open fridge. The narrator's line is that "the mansion was the achievement" and "the cold macaroni at midnight was the truth," and his advice is that even if you're alone, you should sit down at the table, because it's the decision that the meal is worth your full attention. In Japan they say itadakimasu before eating, a thanks to everything that made the meal. Same instinct, fewer Fruity Pebbles. It's more memory than history, but it's the closest thing the day gave me, and it's weirdly sweet.

## Drinks

### James May drinks every Buzzball so you don't have to

James May's Planet Gin's "We made James May drink every Buzzball" is the only real drinks episode, and it's a hoot. Buzzballs are hand-grenade-shaped cocktail balls, and the hosts note that at Christmas they do special editions "that have little bobble-hanging things on it," meaning you can hang them on your tree. One of them saw them in Total Wine. They taste-test the Choc Teas, a chocolate cocktail made with vodka and cream, against the Lime Rita, a lime margarita cocktail made with vodka and triple sec. The ingredients are read out with the hosts' deadpan "natural and artificial flavours."

His verdict on the chocolate one is that it "tastes creamy, which is sort of indulgent and nice," and the other host accuses him of "secretly enjoying these buzz balls." May calls them novelty items and admits he was cynical, thinking "whatever, a fad," until he learned they were worth 500 million. He assumes pounds, someone corrects him to dollars, and he says "Bugger me, I thought I had 500 million pounds." The host's caution is that you "could get completely smashed on these in like an hour," which is the entire product pitch and warning label in one sentence. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #114 says "small print lead to large risk." That's a grenade-shaped drink with "natural and artificial flavours" on the label, and a nine-figure valuation, which tells you how many people read the small print. It was also a gin channel hawking gin between rounds ("Buy James Gin and drink it in the park"), so everyone involved got what they wanted.
