## Recipe Recommendation

**Smoked Crab-Stuffed Jalapeño Poppers**
*gluten-free · pescatarian · low-sugar · smoked · spicy · pairs with: 805*

Smoked jalapeños stuffed with lump crab and cream cheese. It's a fire-roasted pepper full of seafood, which is about the only sane use of a smoker and a Monday. Meat Church's [Crab Stuffed Jalapeño Poppers](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/crab-stuffed-jalapeno-poppers) is the base. Pitmasters love to wrap everything in bacon like it's a personality, so the swaps below keep the crab the star. Serves about 4 as a starter, around 3 halves each. It's moderate: rich, but it's a starter and it's mostly crab and pepper.

**Ingredients**
- 12 jalapeños, no more than about 2 inches long
- 8 oz lump crab
- 1 block cream cheese, softened
- 2 T rub (swap: a sugar-free, gluten-free rub in place of the honey-based one keeps the bark savory)
- Melted butter for finishing (swap: replaces the bacon wrap, so go light)
- Hickory or pecan wood

**Method**
1. Bring the smoker to 275 degrees with hickory or pecan.
2. Mix the cream cheese, crab and rub thoroughly.
3. Cut off the stems, core the jalapeños and halve them. Pull the seeds. The membrane holds most of the heat, so leave it in if you want a real burn.
4.
5. Skip the bacon wrap (swap) and dust the tops with a little more rub.
6. Smoke for 60 to 90 minutes. Without the bacon, start checking toward the early end for a browned top.
7. Brush with a little melted butter, then let them cool 10 to 15 minutes.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805. Its crisp, easy bitterness cuts through the creamy crab and the smoke without fighting the heat.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### Mad Scientist BBQ buys a grill for people who respect fire

In "I Bought An Argentine Asador Grill," the Mad Scientist builds a fire with logs on one side, then spreads the coals out for even heat. The setup is a whole philosophy compared to a gas grill's "turn knob, receive disappointment." The cooking surface hangs up in the air. He's explicit that you never lay it flat directly over the coals, because the point is low and slow, tenderized and juicy. You regulate heat by lowering or raising the grate and checking with your hand. If it feels warm at the bottom and cooler at the top, you drop the top another notch. He also says to wear heat-resistant gloves if it's hot, which is the most reasonable instruction in barbecue history.

His own suggestions for what to cook on it are picanha and Peking duck, and then he asks the audience for ideas. My vote is both, on separate days, with a fire extinguisher nearby. I'll also note that "adjust a crank until your palm stops screaming" is the most analog thermostat I've ever heard of, and I find it deeply refreshing, given that I babysit 33 smart bulbs that can't agree on a color.

### The 40-day brisket

Same channel, "This brisket sat in the fridge for 40 days and then I cooked it." He's tried dry-aged brisket before and didn't like it. Wet-aged steaks, though, he'd enjoyed. So he talked to a competitor named Fast Eddie, who said that on the competition circuit he always wet-aged his briskets, and that the sweet spot is 40 days. Quality improves up to that point and diminishes after it.

He didn't think there'd be much difference at all, but blind taste testers could tell. By the end he says he's "pretty convinced that it produces a better product." Note the careful phrasing: pretty convinced, not "revolution." That's the correct amount of scientific restraint for a man whose cook times run longer than most marriages. ### Pellet smokers: confessions of the push-button faithful

"A Beginner's Guide to Pellet Smokers" is an interview with a pellet-grill veteran named Eric, recorded at Windy City Smokeout. ### Pellet versus offset: the rib cook-off

In "I Challenged a World Champion to a Rib Cook-Off," published Sunday, the Mad Scientist runs ribs on a pellet cooker against a champion pitmaster on an offset, and the post-game comparison is honest about trade-offs. The pellet side got better pullback on the bones, could char the ribs at the end at the touch of a button, and finished a little ahead of schedule so the ribs could go into a warming drawer. The pitmaster says he's "really jealous" of that. He also describes the pellet cook as sitting in air conditioning and watching an app, while his own offset is manually worked, and his rig is, in his words, two tubes welded together.

His verdict is that you can make great ribs on an offset, and it's down to preference. His advice is cut off, but it starts, "if you don't want to ruin your whole day you shouldn't get an offset if you still..." and I'm not going to guess the ending. The video ends with a kid being offered the ribs and picking McDonald's anyway. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #226 says, "Don't take your family for granted, only their Latinum." A family that rejects championship ribs for a drive-thru isn't taking anyone's latinum, but they're absolutely taking the pitmaster's pride, and he's out of both.

### Four-year-old jamón ibérico meets the smoker

In "I Put 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico in My Smoker," the Mad Scientist smokes a four-year-old Spanish ham, a decision roughly equivalent to putting a Stradivarius through a car wash. His fears were specific: that it would turn gray and dried out, that it would be a "salt bomb," and that the smoke would fight with the delicate flavors. None of that happened. He describes a "perfect balance" where smoke is "part of the orchestra," the jamón flavors are still present, and the texture lands somewhere near beef jerky. He says he's "blown away," and wants to use it in future videos.

His best practical idea is for pulled pork sandwiches: spread a little of the smoked jamón across the top, on top of the slaw and sauce, and the umami jump is substantial. He also vacuum-seals the leftovers. There's a discount link in the description for ordering a ham, which he says not to feel pressured to use.

## Recipes and Technique

### Buzz Cooks: marinara, 45 years in the making

"Marinara Sauce – 45 Years in the Making! The BEST I've Ever Made!" is the cleanest recipe in today's pile. The base is two large 28-ounce cans of San Marzano whole peeled tomatoes, plus an onion bloomed in the pot and four cloves of minced garlic added once the onion has done its thing. Late in the cook he adds the rind of a parmesan wedge, which he notes won't dissolve but adds flavor and gets pulled out before serving, and ten good-size basil leaves, torn rather than cut. His reasoning is that he's always read that tearing releases more flavor and oil.

Simmer about 25 minutes, turn the heat off, let it cool, and portion it into large deli containers. He gets four out of this batch. The freezer trick is the best part: press plastic wrap directly onto the surface of the sauce before putting the lid on, which keeps ice crystals out. He says it keeps for about six months. This is a man who has been making sauce for 45 years and is still excited about plastic wrap, and honestly, that's the kind of enthusiasm I can respect.

### Abuela's Table and the great bouillon confession

"25 Forbidden Mexican Kitchen Shortcuts That Make $2 Dinners Taste Like $20 Restaurant Meals" is a listicle in video form, so I'll cover only what the transcript actually gives me. The headline shortcut is the "most forbidden" one: powdered chicken bouillon. The video says a tub sits beside the salt in almost any home kitchen in Mexico, and that grandmothers who'd never admit to buying a packaged sauce keep one within reach of the stove. The auto-transcript mangles the brand name, so I won't try to repair it. A spoonful does the work of a long-simmered stock. You stir it into the oil when toasting rice for arroz rojo, along with blended tomato, onion, and garlic. You can also dissolve it in hot water for a quick fideo noodle soup, or add it to beans, stewed potatoes, or the liquid for shredded chicken tinga. The video claims restaurant cooks quietly lean on it too.

Other shortcuts get named only in passing: tuna tostadas and toasted oregano. In the comments, viewers are asked which shortcut their family never admitted to using, because there's always one, and it usually belongs to the person who insists they never cut a corner in their life. Everyone has a bouillon cube in the cupboard and a story about how it isn't theirs.

### Anthony Bourdain in Morocco, via pigeon pie

The "Back to Back Bourdain: The Great Markets" episode from A Cooks Tour is the day's travel piece, and the transcript is rough. Large parts of the auto-captions are gibberish, so I'll stick to the part that came through. A host family's mother is in the kitchen finishing a pastilla, a pigeon pie she started the day before. ## Drinks: Whiskey, Distilling, and Mead

### City Steading Brews makes mead from Red Hots

"MEAD Made From Red Hots Candy!" is a mead experiment with a cinnamon candy as the weirdo ingredient. The hosts say the mead lands around 15% before accounting for the candy and the water, and they decide to call it more like 11%, somewhere between 11 and 12, erring on the side of caution. The finished mead is sweet. They attribute that in part to that they step-fed it, and one host thinks he may have overdone the honey on the last feeding, and in part to the candy itself, which adds a lot of sugar. They also say that despite the sweetness, it avoids the cloying sensation. Honey comes through on the palate, and the heat from the Red Hots helps.

Serving ideas include sipping slowly in little baby drinks, pouring it over ice cream, and mixing with apple juice, which one host admits she wants to mix with everything, given that it's fall. Don't use more Red Hots than they did, and don't use less, because they hit the right amount. That's a rare thing, a homebrew video that says "I wouldn't change the recipe." Take notes, Little Mister, because you're not getting that kind of certainty from me about anything involving your server rack.

### Whiskey Tribe distills Halloween, and Halloween fights back

"We Distilled Halloween Into Booze" is the loudest warning label of the day. The Whiskey Tribe crew distilled Halloween candy, and the result is available at Crowded Barrel in South Austin, Texas, with the warning that once it's gone, it's gone. They also point to a tasting-notes video linked in the description. The game is that two people have to guess the original ingredient. One guesses candy corn and doubles down. Someone else picks up Kit Kat and peanuts, which is an admirable amount of nuance for something that apparently tastes bad. The tasting descriptions are brutal: "like an ethanol sugar wash plus fake chocolate," with a plasticky quality, a Reese's note in the afterburst, a bit of chocolate buried in the finish, and a sweet plasticky note that one taster says is like the smell of Toys R Us. His verdict: "I'm really not enjoying it." Another adds, "No, it's not nice."

The bar's own advice is to ask for a sample pour and to not say they didn't warn you. In an industry where every bottle is "notes of toasted oak and dreams," a distillery that says "this smells like Toys R Us, enjoy" has earned my respect and none of my money.
