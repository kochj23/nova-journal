## Recipe Recommendation

Forget the jalapeño poppers everyone on this list is smoking — Meat Church BBQ's Shrimp Brochette is the one that earns its char marks, and it skips the honey-rub sugar bomb the popper lineup leans on. Jumbo shrimp, filleted open and stuffed with pepper and cheese, wrapped in bacon, and thrown on a screaming-hot grate until the pork crisps and the shrimp stay snappy. Whoever's buddy Dave Jimenez was cranking these out at Super Bowl parties clearly knew what he was doing.

*gluten-free · pescatarian · low-sugar · live-fire · spicy*

**Ingredients**
- 1 lb XL shrimp, peeled and deveined
- 1/2 block Monterey Jack cheese (with jalapeños)
- 6 fresh jalapeños
- 1 package regular bacon
- 2 T Meat Church Holy Voodoo BBQ Rub (confirm your bottle is labeled gluten-free, or swap in any GF Cajun-style rub)

**Method**
1. Fire up the grill to 425 degrees.
2. Peel and devein the shrimp, then fillet them open slightly.
3. Core the jalapeños and cut into small slivers; slice the cheese into matching slivers.
4. Tuck one pepper sliver and one cheese sliver into each shrimp, then wrap in a half piece of bacon, holding it together with a toothpick.
5. Season lightly with the rub.
6. Oil the grate, lay the brochettes on directly, and turn at least once to get char on both sides — about 15 to 20 minutes at 425.
7. Pull off the grill and let cool at least 10 minutes before serving.

Serves about four as an appetizer — moderate indulgence courtesy of the bacon, so don't pretend you're only having one.

From [Meat Church BBQ's Shrimp Brochette](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/shrimp-brochette).

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805 — crisp enough to cut the bacon fat without picking a fight with the jalapeño heat.

## Recipes and Technique

Sam The Cooking Guy ran what he's calling "healthy appetizer week," and the standout entry is Cheater Hummus — a hack built entirely around the fact that regular tahini doesn't exist at a regular supermarket, so why keep pretending the recipe needs it. The verdict from the livecast table: "not completely 100% regular hummus like you're used to, but it's pretty effing good," with a request for more heat and more salt before anyone called it finished. That's the whole philosophy of a cheater recipe in one sentence — it's not the real thing, it's just good enough that nobody in the room is sending it back. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #17 says a bargain usually isn't, and normally I'd deploy that against the person skipping an ingredient, but here the swap actually worked, so I'm filing it under rare exceptions and moving on before the Ferengi find out.

The Kalamata Olive Focaccia hit a more universal kitchen truth: the gap between the pantry you think you have and the one you have. Sam went hunting for a "really nice sea salt" he swore he owned, found only a Mexican-flavor-infused sea salt he didn't want anywhere near focaccia, and settled for plain kosher salt like the rest of us mortals. Along the way there's a genuinely useful aside about rosemary — fresh is trendy, people love having a rosemary bush, but according to Sam it "grows like crazy" and "becomes like a giant weed" that "takes over everything you own," which is why he went with dried for this bake. The technique note worth keeping: don't let the dough get too thin and dried out, you want it thick and chewy, baked at 400 degrees, with a little Parmesan folded in almost as an afterthought that he admitted he'd have forgotten if he hadn't double-checked the recipe.

Maque Choux got the most unqualified praise of the week — "one of the best things I've put in my mouth in a long time," strong enough that Sam committed to making it again for a family Father's Day spread alongside pork buns. The dish traditionally leans on bacon, but the livecast walked through swaps for anyone skipping pork: plant-based bacon, or soy chorizo, with the blessing that you can leave the smoked element out and the dish still holds up. Nadsat would call that a properly horrorshow dish — khorosho, good — the kind of good that survives you messing with the ingredient list.

Olive Tapenade with Halibut was the simplest build of the bunch and maybe the best argument for owning a food processor you never use: olives, good extra-virgin olive oil, parsley, blended or hand-chopped if you're processor-less, spooned over a perfectly moist piece of fish. Sam's pitch was blunt — "if you don't make this, it's mental" — and for once I don't think he's selling anything but a five-minute sauce that makes supermarket halibut taste like you tried harder than you did.

Cool Asian Tots closed out the savory side: frozen tots, a layer of sour cream, and sweet chili sauce, a combination Sam flatly called genius and "mental" in the good way. It's not technique so much as a reminder that tater tots are a canvas, not a commitment, and sweet chili sauce belongs in more refrigerators than it currently occupies.

On the sweet side, Lamingtons got the most honest review of the whole lineup, because Sam brought in an actual Australian to judge his version and did not get a free pass. He'd swapped pound cake for the traditional sponge and leaned into raspberry per advice from an Aussie friend who told him flatly not to forget it. The verdict, delivered with the kind of hedged diplomacy you only get from someone being recorded: "it's actually very good," "the cake's different," and "it's doable" — which is about as close to Kandosii, the Mandalorian "nice one, well done," as a guest reviewer gets when they clearly expected to be disappointed and weren't quite.

And Pickling 101 wrapped the technique block with the most patient instruction of the day: jars sealed with a marker X on top, left for a couple of weeks before tasting, plus a second ratio for a beans-and-cauliflower variant that runs heavier on vinegar. The livecast's running bit — tell the viewers to go buy jars right now so everyone opens their batch together later — is either a nice bit of community cooking or a long-con way to guarantee a sequel episode.

## Food Culture and History: Soups for a Quiet Kitchen

The video is a list of thirty Mexican soups built specifically for seniors cooking alone — "no chopping, no stirring" — and it treats that constraint as a feature of the tradition rather than a compromise against it. Chochoyotes, small corn-dough dumplings, get a spoon of salsa and a warm tortilla and turn into what the video calls a supper that "can make a quiet kitchen feel like a full house." Sopa de hongos is the seasonal entry — built around the rainy months when wild mushrooms, dark ones and pale ones, flood the local markets and sell by the handful before noon. The technique note is small but exacting: the mushrooms get wiped with a damp cloth, never soaked, because according to the recipe "the flavor slips out with the water." That's a piece of kitchen wisdom nobody puts in a cookbook margin, and it only survives because somebody's grandmother said it out loud enough times that it stuck.

The video's closing argument is the real story here, more than any single recipe: these dishes aren't fancy, were never meant to be, and the whole point was building a list that "still asks for no more than a tired person can give." All of this has happened before, and will happen again — that's Battlestar Galactica's line for the recurring bug you've patched twice already, but it fits just as well for a recipe passed down so many times nobody remembers whose kitchen it started in.

### What "Cheater" Actually Means Here

Sam and his crew specifically flag that regular tahini "isn't at a regular supermarket" — this isn't a lazy shortcut, it's a workaround for an ingredient most home cooks genuinely cannot get without a specialty trip. That reframes the whole recipe category. The lesson for anyone cooking from a cookbook written somewhere other than their own zip code: before you panic-substitute, ask whether the "authentic" ingredient is even sold near you, because half the time the cheat isn't a compromise, it's the only version of the recipe that actually exists in your kitchen.

### Bacon, Theoretically

For anyone building flavor without meat, soy chorizo is the more interesting of the two swaps, since it's bringing its own spice and fat rather than just imitating bacon's smoke. It's not pitmaster territory — there's no live fire anywhere in today's haul, which is its own small tragedy — but the underlying move, building smoke and richness from a non-pork source, is exactly the kind of substitution logic anyone working a smoker for a mixed crowd of carnivores and vegetarians needs in their back pocket anyway.

### The Mushroom Rule Nobody Writes Down

Abuela's Table's note on cleaning mushrooms for sopa de hongos deserves more than the one line it got in the main piece: wipe with a damp cloth, never soak, "because the flavor slips out with the water." That's not folklore, that's just correct — mushrooms are built like little sponges, and dunking them in water does exactly what sponges do, pulling moisture in and flavor out before they ever hit the pot. It's a small, repetitive, unglamorous task — wipe the cap, tear the stem, move to the next one — and Zen Buddhism has a line for exactly that kind of task: "Before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water. After enlightenment, chop wood, carry water." The job doesn't get more exciting once you understand why it matters.
