### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Foil-Boat Beef Tallow Brisket**
*gluten-free · dairy-free · fully cooked*

Sixteen hours for one brisket. Pellet-smoker people call that a hobby, everyone else calls it a hostage situation. The payoff is allthingsbbq's foil-boat finish: the brisket gets wrapped in tallow-lined butcher paper, then sits in an open foil boat so the top stays exposed and the bark keeps setting while the flat stays juicy. Serves 15, and it's a treat. Cook it for a crowd, not a Tuesday.

**Ingredients**
- 1 whole packer prime brisket
- 1 cup beef tallow, divided
- 1 tbsp butcher-style brine (for the injection)
- Salt-and-pepper rub, generous (swap for the source's pre-mixed all-purpose seasoning, which keeps the bark savoury, sugar-free and garlic-free)

**Method**
1. Remove the fat cap completely from the point, leave a 1/4" cap on the flat, and shave off any large chunks of hard surface fat.
2. Shake 3/4 cup tallow with the brine in a small jar and inject it throughout the flat.
3. Rub the excess tallow over the whole brisket, then season generously all over.
4. Bring the pellet grill to a steady 225°F. Smoke on the second shelf until the bark is dark and the internal temperature hits roughly 165°F, about 12 hours.
5. Spread the remaining 1/4 cup tallow over butcher paper in the rough shape of the brisket, set the brisket on it and wrap tightly.
6. Double a large sheet of foil, set the paper-wrapped brisket on top, and form a boat around the bottom, leaving the top of the paper exposed.
7. Return it to the smoker until probe tender and roughly 205°F internal.
8. Rest 1-2 hours in a cooler or hot box before slicing.

Serve with a crisp cucumber salad or charred zucchini and a scatter of scallion greens as the low-carb side.

Source: [allthingsbbq, Beef Tallow Smoked Brisket](https://www.atbbq.com/blogs/recipes/beef-tallow-smoked-brisket-foilboat)

**Pairs with:** a Scotch, neat.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Garlic Butter-Roasted Salmon with Beets & Broccoli**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

One sheet pan, forty minutes, and nobody has to stand over a stove like a hostage negotiator. The garlic gets roasted soft in a foil packet, mashed into herb butter, and then slathered on everything like it owes you money. It's a dinner that looks like effort and costs you almost none, which is my favorite kind of lie. Adapted from [EatingWell](https://www.eatingwell.com/garlic-butter-roasted-salmon-with-beets-broccoli-11860300). Serves 4, and it lands at moderate: butter-forward, but there's a whole head of broccoli standing guard.

**Ingredients**
- 2 (8.8-ounce) packages refrigerated cooked beets, quartered (about 3 cups)
- 1 medium head broccoli (12 ounces), cut into bite-size pieces (about 4 cups)
- 1 tablespoon extra-virgin olive oil, plus 2 teaspoons
- 1 teaspoon ground pepper, divided
- ¾ teaspoon salt, divided
- 6 medium cloves garlic, peeled
- 4 tablespoons unsalted butter, at room temperature
- 3 tablespoons chopped fresh tender herbs (tarragon, chives and/or flat-leaf parsley), plus more for garnish
- 4 (4-ounce) skinless salmon fillets, patted dry

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to 425°F. Toss the beets and broccoli with 1 tablespoon oil, ½ teaspoon pepper and ¼ teaspoon salt on a large rimmed baking sheet. Wrap the garlic in foil with the remaining 2 teaspoons oil, set it in the center, and roast about 15 minutes, until the broccoli is tender.
2. Mash the butter, herbs and ¼ teaspoon salt with the roasted garlic. Toss the vegetables with about 2 tablespoons of this butter.
3. Nestle the salmon among the vegetables, season with the remaining pepper and salt, and brush with the remaining butter.
4. Roast 8 to 10 minutes, until the salmon flakes easily. Swap-in note: keep it going until it's opaque all the way through and reads 145°F.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon.

### The Mashed Potato Transcript Is Really About Restraint

Food Network Kitchen's Thanksgiving mashed potato video, the Yukon Gold versus russet showdown, hides its best advice in the phrase "doesn't look overworked yet." That's the real test the cook applies. It isn't "is it smooth?" It's whether the potatoes have been abused. Anyone who's turned a pot of spuds into wallpaper paste with a mixer will feel that line in their soul.

Notice the order of operations. The potatoes are "seasoned pretty heavily in the beginning," and only then does the cook taste and correct. One batch gets "just a pinch" and some pepper. The rest are called "pretty well seasoned already." So the lesson is to salt early, taste at the end, and nudge instead of dumping. Early commitment, late adjustment, and no panic-tolchocking. Tolchock is Nadsat for a good hard whack, and it should be your last move, not your first.

The video also gives you a vocabulary for the two outcomes. One batch is described as "sort of silkier." The other has "a fluffier, stiffer consistency." They're different dishes, and the cook points out "a difference in color" and "a little bit different in texture" before getting to flavor. So don't pick a potato and hope. If the plan is gravy in a well, you want the stiff, fluffy batch. If the plan is a bowl that stands on its own with butter and flaky salt, you have more room to go creamy.

One bowl gets butter and chives. The other stays plain with "a little bit of flaky salt to finish." For a holiday table, that's a free split. Put out one dressed bowl and one plain bowl and let people choose. The video doesn't say the cook does this at a real dinner, so that's my suggestion, not hers.
