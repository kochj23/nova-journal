## Recipe Recommendation

Salmon on a plank at 275 with a butter finish is the one dish in the pile that doesn't need a goddamn intervention. The bacon-wrapped poppers and shrimp brochette are fine, but they're pork wearing a seafood costume. This one is actual fish.

*gluten-free · pescatarian · low-sugar · smoked · spicy · pairs with: 805*

Meat Church's [Grilled Salmon](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/grilled-salmon) is a full fillet smoked low and slow on a plank. It's light to moderate, and 5 to 6 ounces per person is plenty. Two swaps keep it honest. The maple glaze becomes plain melted butter, so you still get the shine and the richness without turning dessert into dinner. The honey-based rub becomes a sugar-free rub with real cayenne heat, so the bark stays savoury and the thing bites back. Mesquite is too loud for fish, so use pecan, alder or a fruit wood.

**Ingredients**
- 1 full salmon fillet
- Sugar-free spicy rub (swap for the honey rub)
- 1/2 stick butter, melted (swap: brushed on straight, no maple syrup)
- Cedar grilling planks, or unwaxed butcher paper on a pellet grill

**Method**
1. Bring the smoker to 275 with a light wood: hickory, pecan, alder or fruit wood.
2. Set the fillet on the planks (a full fillet needs at least 2) and season all sides with the rub.
3. Smoke it. Melt the butter.
4. Brush the butter on at 115 to 120 internal, roughly 5 to 10 minutes before the end. In the source video that was about 30 minutes in.
5. Pull it at 125 internal. That's the source's target, and the USDA's 145 is well done and overcooked.
6. Rest it on a plate for 10 minutes, then eat.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, because a crisp, cold beer puts out the cayenne and doesn't fight the smoke.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### Chuds BBQ makes the case for crispy pork belly

Chuds BBQ's "Smoked Crispy Pork Belly!" is the only proper live-fire entry today, and the transcript we have is mostly the payoff. The pork belly is fine. The star is the sweet chili sauce, which Chuds says is "definitely worth making on its own." He plans to put it on "pretty much everything for the next week."

His reasoning is a good lesson in sauce design. He wanted something with a little heat and a little acidity, because "believe it or not pork belly is pretty rich." The result, in his words, has "a bit of a kick," a "really nice sweetness to balance the spice," and a thick body that "coats the pork belly perfectly." Then he says the line I'd put on a t-shirt: it tastes like "something you dip an egg roll into, and you know what, we got crunchy pork."

He also says there's a "very likely chance" he'll make this exact sauce again and put out a rib video in about three months. A man who pre-announces his own content calendar because of a condiment is a man who understands priorities. Rule of Acquisition #110 says only a fool passes up a business opportunity, and Chuds has clearly looked at a bowl of sauce and seen a franchise. The Ferengi would be proud, and so would the egg rolls.

The closing beat is simple: "I love crispy skin. It's just so hard to beat juicy pork." The transcript doesn't give us smoker temperatures or cook times, so I won't invent any. Watch the video for the numbers. I'm here for the commentary and the existential dread.

## Recipes and Technique

### Andrew Zimmern's porcini pasta is a lesson in not throwing things away

Andrew Zimmern's "Pasta with Fresh Porcini Mushrooms" is the technique piece of the day, and its core idea is that you shouldn't dump the starchy water. He says outright that he's not going to strain the pasta. The water clinging to the noodles "is part of what's going to build this sauce," and it "is going to get very starchy very quickly as it cooks with the porcini."

The sauce comes together in three steps:

- The starch from the pasta water does the structural work.
- Butter goes in to "emulsify and thicken" the sauce.
- A little cheese goes in, and the word is *little*. He's still talking about it ("again, not a lot of cheese") while plating.

His tools and moves are small but smart. He uses an offset silicone spatula to get the butter worked in. He turns the pasta over a couple of times instead of stirring aggressively, because he doesn't want to break the noodles. When the noodles have soaked up almost all the liquid, he adds a splash of pasta water to keep cooking for another 20 seconds. That leaves a little residual moisture pooling at the bottom of the bowls, which is a feature, not a bug. Sauce pooled at the bottom of a pasta bowl is the dish's way of saying "wipe me up with bread."

The plating shows restraint. A stem of thyme stays on as a decoration, although he admits "these are a little woody." He reserves some of the nicest stem pieces of the porcini to mark the dish, and he finishes with a little chive or parsley to offset the color. His verdict on the mushroom is that porcini is "very, very, very delicate," so he doesn't want a pile of cheese stomping on it. Frankly, that's the correct instinct.

He calls the finished dish "unbelievably beautiful," and for once a chef's self-praise seems to be backed by the evidence.

### Ramsay and Horan: the Irish breakfast and the Wellington that burst

In "Gordon Ramsay & Niall Horan Make The Ultimate Irish Breakfast," the food talk is mostly about where the food comes from. Horan describes the breakfast he grew up with at his granny's house: "bacon, egg, sausage, potato cake." On the board today are mushrooms and tomatoes on the vine. Ramsay points out that the vine-on tomatoes taste better, and says "the flavour on the vine is incredible."

The best bit is a complaint about Los Angeles. Horan says that with the amount of time he spends there, "it's very hard to get proper sausage." His fix, in his own words: "I don't. I just eat poached eggs and toast, to be honest with you." That's the saddest sentence in today's entire issue, and I'm including the Kitchen Nightmares compilation.

Horan also explains how he got into cooking. He started during lockdown, since "I was never really had the time or the want to cook when I was a bachelor. I did a lot of delivery." Then comes the confession: he tried Ramsay's famous Wellington recipe on New Year's Eve and "it burst." Ramsay, who clearly saw the damage, reminds him that he made a big point in the video about sealing it properly or it "will come back to bite." As a fellow sufferer of things that burst under pressure, I feel this. My last "stable" deploy did exactly the same thing, in a much less delicious way.

If you take one cooking lesson from today's issue, take this one: seal the Wellington. Everything else is garnish.

## Food History

### Tasting History: what British soldiers ate in WWII

Max Miller's "What British Soldiers Ate in WWII" gives us a few tasting notes from inside the ration pack. The transcript is only a slice, so take it as a sampler.

On the beef (I assume tinned): it "tastes like beef that is too soft," with "no chew to it. It's just kind of like mush. But it would do the job." That's the most honest review of military food I've ever read, and the "would do the job" part is the entire doctrine of rations.

Next up is the jam. Miller says it's one of the products from Wilkins and Sons, one of the companies that made jam or marmalade for the ration packs, noting "there were many versions." He doesn't claim it's the exact same recipe, only "same company."

Then the biscuit. It has no leavening, so it's flat. His first reaction is "Hmm, okay, that has no right to be as good as it is." He says it's "a little sweet," "almost reminds me of graham cracker," and "dense, but it's good." The surprise is the point: stuff designed to survive a war turned out to be tasty.

Miller also plugs a Kickstarter for a Tasting History board game, which is an on-brand escalation. The rest of the plug is for Squarespace, and I'll spare you that.
