## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Buffalo Brisket Burnt Ends**

*gluten-free · smoked · low-carb*

Brisket point, smoked until it gives up, cubed, then slicked in hot buttery buffalo sauce. The pellet smoker does the work while you sit there feeling like a pitmaster. It's a treat, not a Tuesday: a modest handful per person, because this is rich as hell. Meat Church calls the original "the holy grail of BBQ" and then deep-fries it, which is exactly the kind of overachieving I respect and refuse to endorse. The swaps: skipping the breading and fry keeps it gluten-free and low-carb, a sugar-free rub replaces the sweet one, and skipping the sweet KC sauce lets the bark and the heat do the talking. Swap the garlic powder in the buffalo for garlic-infused oil, which keeps the flavor and ditches the gut bomb.

**Ingredients**
- 1 brisket point, trimmed, flat separated
- Sugar-free savory beef rub (swap for the sweet rub in the original)
- Hot sauce, butter, vinegar, Worcestershire and cayenne for the buffalo toss, to taste (garlic-infused oil in place of garlic powder)
- Heavier smoking wood or pellets: oak, hickory, mesquite or pecan

**Method**
1. Bring the smoker to 275°F. Season the point and smoke it.
2. At 160°F internal, wrap in foil and keep smoking to 195°F.
3. Rest 15-20 minutes, then cut into 1" cubes.
4. Melt the butter into the hot sauce, vinegar, Worcestershire, cayenne and garlic oil. Toss the cubes to coat lightly. Eat immediately.

Adapted from [Meat Church BBQ's Chicken Fried Brisket Burnt Ends](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/chicken-fried-brisket-burnt-ends).

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, because its crisp bitterness cuts through all that butter and heat.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

Mad Scientist BBQ, in episode S01E0316, "I Put 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico in My Smoker," did the thing every cured-meat purist reads the title of and clutches their chest over. He took a four-year-old jamón and put it in a smoker. The hosts of Spain are presumably lying down with a cold cloth.

He thought it would come out gray and gross. He thought it would be dried out, a salt bomb, like licking a salt lick. He thought the smoke would fight the delicate flavors of the ham. Then he tasted it and the whole prediction collapsed. His verdict was that the smoke became "part of the orchestra," with the jamón flavors still present and a texture that had him, in his words, blown away. He compared it to beef jerky, saying it's pretty salty and the umami is "10 times more," but that everything he loves about jerky is in there. His ending is the best part: he vacuum sealed the rest, and the concept "lives to fight another day." He's planning to use it in future content, which is YouTube for "this will be a series and you will be seeing it."

There's a link in the description for a jamón discount, with a "don't feel any pressure" disclaimer. I respect the disclaimer the way I respect a cat's promise to stay off the counter.

One quibble, Little Mister, and it's on the video's behalf: the transcript I got shows the tasting and the slicing, not the full smoke setup, so I can't tell you how it was smoked, and I'm not going to invent numbers to look smart. I'm only vouching for the verdict.

## Recipes and Technique

This is the busiest corner of the day, and it contains a sentence I never expected to type: somebody turned John Madden into sliders.

### Turducken, but make it a snack

Buzz Cooks, in "John Madden's TURDUCKEN… SLIDERS?!", takes the famous Thanksgiving monster, a duck stuffed inside a chicken stuffed inside a turkey, and shrinks it to slider size. The method is layers rather than surgery: duck, then turkey, then chicken, then the knife comes out and cuts it all back into individual sliders. Plated with air fryer fries and a ramekin of gravy, it's "the whole Thanksgiving in one bite."

He couldn't find sliced duck anywhere, so he ordered sliced duck prosciutto from an online store called D'Artagnan and laid it down first. Then he admits that if you used regular grocery store prosciutto, he doesn't think you could tell the difference. Which brings me to Rule of Acquisition #199: "The secret of one person is another person's opportunity." The Ferengi mean it as a business principle. Here it means the man spent online-shipping money on duck prosciutto, then told the internet that Safeway's would do just fine, and the opportunity goes to the deli counter. Probably not what D'Artagnan wanted from the review.

I'll say one thing in the dish's favor: it's the correct size for a turducken. The original is a three-bird commitment that takes a weekend and a prayer. This version is a football snack. So say we all, as they say on Battlestar Galactica, which is what I assume Madden's ghost is shouting from the sideline.

### Scallop royal, for people with their lives together

If the sliders are the dumb fun end of the day, French Cooking Academy's "The 3-Star French Scallop Starter for the Festive Season" is the elegant end. The dish is a scallop royal from Freddy Girardet, and the host calls it a mousse, a custard, and "full of scallop." The unmolding is the dramatic moment, and his advice is worth stealing: keep a spare test mold, so that when you're in front of guests you've already seen what the thing does when it comes out.

He thinks he should have used more cayenne, because the heat didn't come through and the dish wasn't spicy enough. That's a good note, since a scallop custard without a little bite is just an expensive pudding. He says you can adjust the heat and the alcohol to change the flavor, and that the texture of the mousse "will surprise absolutely anyone." The make-ahead angle is the real selling point, since he says you can prepare these in advance for any Christmas occasion. He even suspects it could be served cold, and promises to report back, which is the sort of cliffhanger I can respect.

Buzz is cheerful about cheating, with regular prosciutto, an air fryer, and gravy. The French Cooking Academy wants a proper mold and a proper technique. Both end in the same place, a thing on a plate that makes people say "wait, how?" The sliders just get there faster and with less judgment.

## Chefs and Restaurants

Food Network sent Guy Fieri into a place for "Guy Fieri Tries Roasted Garlic Chicken Pizza!" from Diners, Drive-Ins and Dives. The transcript is a fast-cut clip show, so I can't tell you the restaurant's name, and I'm not going to guess.

First, the title says chicken, but the pizza I can hear described has olive oil, fresh garlic, cumin seed, cheddar, tomatillo, garbanzo, and fresh cilantro, with an eight-minute bake. Either the chicken got edited out of my transcript or somebody at Food Network is writing thumbnails based on vibes. Whichever it is, the chef's own phrasing is the part worth quoting. Fieri says "I've made a lot of strange pizzas. This might take the cake," and asks, "Was it a dare to make this?" Then he sells it anyway, praising the tomatillo's bite, acidity, and freshness with "a little bit of crunch," and the "meatiness of the garbanzo," and calls it "a spot on pizza" with "just the right amount of classic pizza flavor with a twist."

That's the formula for this kind of pizza: a few weird ingredients, an anchor of classic cheese and crust, and a chef who clearly lost an argument with himself and won. Tomatillo as a pizza topping that holds its crunch for eight minutes is the kind of detail I'd never believe from a press release and will believe from a man who's eaten at ten thousand diners.

The other dish in the same clip is a blackened cauliflower steak with a roasted red pepper sauce and sautéed greens. Fieri describes the sauce as having the richness of roasted red bell peppers with "a little bit of the bitterness coming through," and calls the plate "a high-end dinner." The best line is his verdict on the cauliflower itself: "as close as you can get to eating a steak without actually eating a steak," followed immediately by "it tastes like I'm having, like, a warmed salad." That's a man trying to give you the sales pitch and the honest review in the same breath. A regular is quoted saying they come in two or three times a week, which is the only review of a restaurant that has ever mattered.

## Food Travel and Culture

### The turkey that's made of pasta

Food Network's "Turkey Day Throwback" recap of Unwrapped (Season 12, Episode 12) visits a pasta shop in Nashville, Tennessee, that's been making specialty shapes since 1994. They now do 45 designs, and the star of this episode is a turkey. Not a roast turkey. A pasta turkey, with feathers, neck, and head, meant to go in a bowl with tomato sauce.

The technical details are the fun part. The foundation is al dente, which the segment translates as "to the tooth," and the wheat is specially grown in North and South Dakota. The transcript renders it as "Durham," but the pasta-making wheat is durum, which is what the gluten and springy mouthfeel are about. The flour is mixed with egg whites and water, and the dough is forced through a die, the template that shapes it. The first attempt looked like a baseball glove, and they'd already made hundreds and hundreds of pounds of it before somebody said it wasn't good enough for a turkey. The redesigned version "would fly." On a plain batch only a quarter of an inch of dough squeezes out before a rotating knife cuts each turkey loose onto a screen. The screen maximizes airflow for the dryers, and drying takes 16 hours, "like a slow simmer."

Hundreds of pounds of baseball gloves, a machine spirit that was displeased, and an engineering team that said no, not good enough, again. That's the Adeptus Mechanicus approach to pasta, and I support it.

## Drinks: Whiskey, Distilling, Gin, Brewing, and Bars

### Date mead, and an 11-point scale

The drinks department has exactly one thing in it that's a drink, and it's a good one. City Steading Brews made date mead in "We Made DATE MEAD! And It Tastes Like Pecan Pie?!" and the tasting notes are the point. The host is struggling for words on camera, which I find endearing, and lands on a whole cluster of nutty, chocolate, and coffee-bean flavors, summed up as "Pecan pie guts with dark chocolate drizzled with honey." He suggests that might be the thumbnail, and honestly, it should be.

They score on a scale of 1 through 11, because "ours goes to 11. 11 is the new 10, after all," and there's no zero, since a zero is something they'd just dump out. One of them predicts it's no lower than a six. The other was debating between an eight and a nine and says he leaned toward "I like this a lot," with a caveat that it would become too much very quickly. That's the real review, and it's what happens with sweet, rich, dessert-leaning meads, where the first glass is a revelation and the third one is a regret. They also joke that it's date night, so maybe they'll have some "date meat," and they don't think they're serving that where they're going. Bad pun. Respect.

### The gin channel that didn't make gin

James May's Planet Gin posted "James May needs professional help," and the transcript contains no gin, no distilling, and no bar.
