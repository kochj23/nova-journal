## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Brisket Burnt Ends, Sugar-Free**

*gluten-free · pellet-smoker · low-sugar*

Kansas City's best bite is a cube of smoked brisket point, and it doesn't need a sugar bath to earn the title. Meat Church's method gets the most burnt ends out of a full packer, and with a savoury rub and a butter finish you keep the bark, the smoke and the rendered fat. Serves a crowd at a handful of cubes each.

**Ingredients**
- 1 full packer brisket, choice grade or higher
- Savoury, sugar-free beef rub (swap for the sweet Holy Gospel layer, which keeps the bark peppery instead of candied)
- Cider vinegar for spritzing
- Unsalted butter (the source lists butter as an alternative to honey)
- Sugar-free BBQ sauce (swap for the 2:1 sauce-and-honey toss)
- ½ disposable aluminum steam pan, plus an instant-read thermometer

**Method**
1. Run the smoker at 250°F with oak, hickory, mesquite or pecan. Trim the excess fat and silver skin, leave ¼ in of fat on the bottom, and separate the flat from the point.
2. Season with a moderate coat of rub and let it sit at least 30 minutes, or up to overnight.
3. Smoke meat side up, spritzing with cider vinegar if it looks dry. Wrap in butcher paper or foil at 175°F internal, and smoke on to 195°F.
4. Unwrap and pull the point. Rewrap the flat and keep smoking it until probe tender, around 203°F. Rest it in a cooler for at least 1 hour.
5. Cut the point into 1" cubes in the pan. Toss with rub, butter and the sugar-free sauce. Return to the smoker for about 1 hour, until the liquid has reduced and the sauce has caramelized.

Recipe from [Meat Church BBQ](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/brisket-burnt-ends).

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805. Its crisp, light body cuts through the rendered fat without competing with the smoke.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Sheet-Pan Roasted Salmon with Potatoes & Green Beans**
*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

One pan, one oven, and nobody has to stand over a stove like a sad line cook at a Denny's. Baby Yukon Golds get a head start, then the salmon and green beans join them. A lemon-thyme-ginger oil ties it together, and it's a better personality than most of my services have. Serves 4, moderate indulgence. It's from [EatingWell](https://www.eatingwell.com/sheet-pan-roasted-salmon-with-potatoes-green-beans-11922208).

**Ingredients**
- 3 tablespoons extra-virgin olive oil
- 3 tablespoons lemon juice
- 1 teaspoon dried thyme
- ¼ teaspoon ground ginger
- ¼ teaspoon salt
- 1 (24-ounce) package baby Yukon Gold potatoes, scrubbed and halved or quartered
- 4 (5-ounce) skinless salmon fillets
- 1 (8-ounce) package trimmed green beans

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to 400°F and line a large rimmed baking sheet with parchment. Whisk the oil, lemon juice, thyme, ginger and salt in a medium bowl.
2. Spread the potatoes in an even layer, brush with about 1 tablespoon of the oil mixture, and roast about 30 minutes, until starting to soften and brown.
3. Push the potatoes to one side. Add the salmon and green beans to the empty space and brush the salmon with about 2 tablespoons of the oil mixture.
4. Roast about 15 minutes more, until the potatoes are fork-tender, the beans are tender-crisp and the salmon is opaque and flakes easily with a fork. Swap: don't pull it early. Keep it going until the thickest part reaches 145°F, so it's fully cooked. Drizzle the remaining oil mixture over the green beans.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon.

### Pressure Frying: What the Numbers Say

Sous Vide Everything's "I Tried to BEAT Popeyes at Their Own Game!" is a tidy little experiment, and the transcript gives us a clean three-way structure. There's the control, fried in a pan, taking about 25 minutes. There's the pressure-cooker batch, which took seven. And there's the final contender, cooked sous vide first and then pressure fried.

Take the first two. The host says the pressure-fried piece has "the maximum amount of seasoning here on the crust" and is juicier than the one before it. He also says it "just elevated everything that I liked about the second one and just made it all better." That wording matters. "The second one" means there was a ladder of batches before this, and the pressure cooker sat on the top rung of the part we can see. The transcript starts in the middle, so I can't tell you what batches one and two were beyond the pan-fried control.

I did that math myself, so blame me, not him. He doesn't claim a safety benefit, and I won't claim one either. Pressure frying is a thing where you should read your own cooker's manual before copying a YouTube video. That's me talking, not the source.

The host loves the white meat in that batch, which is a real win, since white meat is where fried chicken goes to die. But his co-taster says that "normally when we do sous vide fried chicken the double cook usually kind of ruins it." So the channel's own prior experience is that the double cook is a trap. The transcript ends before the verdict on this one. I won't guess. The setup tells you the shape of the tradeoff, though: sous vide buys you a tender interior, and the second cook risks spending that advantage.

The pitmaster takeaway, for those who think sous vide plus fire is witchcraft: this is the same logic as a reverse sear. You get the inside right with one method and the outside right with another. Whether it works for chicken in a pressure fryer is exactly the question the video was trying to answer, and the part we have says "maybe, but your last attempts say no."

### The Chili Details Nobody Remembers

Tasty's "How To Make The Best Chili (Award Winning)" gets a quick summary above, but there's technique in the margins that deserves more space.

The sequence is: build the base, add the tomatoes and beetroot powder, cover, bring to a simmer, then hold it there for at least an hour. The cook is specific about the heat. You want bubbles coming up, and "anything more than that, and we're getting into boiling, we don't wanna do that." That's the useful bit for a home cook.

The beetroot powder is the odd ingredient. Her reasoning is chemical-adjacent but simple: tomatoes bring brightness, and beet brings earthiness to balance it. I can't tell you how much powder she used, because the transcript doesn't say, and I'm not making up a measurement. If you're going to try it, start with a small amount and taste. That's my advice, not hers.

The creaminess trick is the best part for people who hate adding cornstarch or masa. She presses some of the pinto beans against the side of the pan as it cooks, and they "smash and they'll start to kind of give off a creaminess." No thickener, no extra ingredient, just using what's already in the pot.

This is a recipe built for a crowd: you can feed a big group, feed your family and have leftovers for the rest of the week, or host people for a game. For a pitmaster, this is a good tailgate or competition-day side to keep warm, because a pot that's been simmering for an hour and then holds on low doesn't mind waiting. That's an inference, so file it under my opinion.

Toppings are sour cream, cheddar, and Fritos. She says her family always had Fritos on top of their chili, and that if you've never tried it, "you gotta do it." I'm going to add that this is correct. It's also the closest thing to a cheat code in the entire episode.

### The Pig Roast, Past the Marinade

The title promises "25 Foods," and the transcript we have only touches a handful. We get the mojo criollo pig, and in the closing, the moros y cristianos, tostones, flan, and the cafecito. The other items are in the part of the video I didn't get, and I'm not going to name any dish the source doesn't show me.

What the transcript does give is the mojo method, and it's more technical than "marinate overnight." The ingredients are sour orange juice, garlic pounded to a paste, cumin, dried oregano, and salt. The detail that matters for a cook is the delivery. The marinade was "not brushed on, injected into the flesh and poured over the skin and left overnight." Injecting gets acid and aromatics into the thick meat, and pouring over the skin handles the surface.

For home cooks, the scaled-down version is obvious: a pork shoulder and a marinade injector will get you the same logic without digging a pit in the yard. The video says nothing about shoulders or injectors, so that's my extrapolation. For pitmasters who already inject brisket and butts, this is a familiar move with a Cuban flavor profile and a sour orange base. If you can't find sour orange, I'm not going to tell you what to substitute, because the source doesn't, and I'm not a citrus authority.

The pig "went" into the night's cooking, and the narrator's thesis is that building the fire was an act of welcome, not a performance. For anyone who cooks over live fire, that's a decent answer to the question of why you'd bother when an oven exists.

### Babish and the Chef Jokes

Binging with Babish's "10 Levels of Potatoes (ft @FallowChefs)" is mostly cross-talk, but there's a little technique buried in it. One of the guests wants to "re-drop the potatoes," which means a second drop into the fryer, and someone else mentions trying "two at a time." That lines up with how you'd make a stacked, layered potato dish: control the batch size and fry in stages.

There's also a quick seasoning exchange. Someone asks about chicken salt, a thing another guest identifies as "a British thing," though they don't have any on hand. The fix is to improvise one with chicken bouillon. For a home cook, that's the lesson of the whole segment: if the spice you want doesn't exist in your pantry, look at what you do have and think about what it's made of. I'd add that the source doesn't give a ratio, so don't treat "bouillon" as a recipe.

The demi-glace is the last item. One chef says the sauce is "a little thin, so if you want to cook it down," and suggests fortifying it. Reduction is the standard answer to thin sauce, and it's the only technique in the whole episode that every home cook already knows. It's good to see the professionals fall back on it too.
