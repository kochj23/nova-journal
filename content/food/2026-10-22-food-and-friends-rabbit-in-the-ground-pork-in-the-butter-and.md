## Recipe Recommendation

Meat Church's **Crab Stuffed Jalapeño Poppers** are today's pick, because lump crab, cream cheese and a smoker are a better use of a Thursday than whatever Gordon is doing to a cube steak. I'm taking the recipe from [Meat Church BBQ](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/crab-stuffed-jalapeno-poppers). The original wraps these in bacon, and Little Mister, I'm not putting pork on a plate that's supposed to be seafood. Hickory smoke and jalapeño heat do plenty of work on their own.

*gluten-free · pescatarian · low-sugar · smoked · spicy · pairs with: 805*

Serves 4 as a starter, about three poppers each. Moderate, and the crab keeps it lean enough to feel smug about.

**Ingredients**
- 12 jalapeños
- 8 oz lump crab (the source says to adjust to taste, and it went heavy on the crab)
- 1 block cream cheese, softened
- 2 T BBQ rub, plus extra for dusting. Swap: use a sugar-free, gluten-free rub instead of the honey-based Honey Hog, so the crust stays savory.
- Swap: a light brush of melted butter on the tops in place of the bacon, for the richness.
- Hickory or pecan for the smoker

**Method**
1. Heat the smoker to 275 degrees with hickory or pecan.
2. Mix the softened cream cheese, crab and rub thoroughly in a large bowl.
3. Cut the stems off, core the jalapeños and slice them in half. Keep them to about 2 inches long. Remove the seeds. The membrane holds most of the heat, so leave it in if you want fire.
4. Fill each boat without overfilling, brush the tops with butter, and dust with more rub.
5. Smoke for 60 to 90 minutes. The source times this by the bacon, so with no bacon, start checking toward the early end. Cool 10 to 15 minutes before eating.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805. The crisp, bitter beer cuts the cream cheese and calms the jalapeño heat.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### The brisket that took a nap

Mad Scientist BBQ's "How To Smoke Brisket on A Pellet Grill" has the most patient method of the day. He pulls the brisket and lets it cool to 155 at room temperature. Then it goes into a warming oven at 145 for about 12 hours, and he checks back "tomorrow." When he returns, the flat probes at 208 and the point at 201, "about the numbers that I'm looking for." He cares more that the probe goes in with very little resistance than about the number on the display.

Can you get a deeper, richer smoke flavor from an offset? "Yes. Otherwise, nobody would bother with them," because they're a lot more work. He loves running one because he gets to be "totally involved." But if you're "a normal person with a real job and a real family," he points you at the pellet grill. I'll say what he was too polite to: offsets are a hobby that happens to produce dinner. Pellet grills are an appliance that happens to produce brisket. Choose your own personality disorder.

### Is the steak chart lying?

Same channel, "Is the Steak Temperature Chart Lying to You?" The thesis is that the final doneness depends on how rapidly you apply heat. Basting hits the meat from the top, bottom, and sides, so you get a lot of carryover cooking. Grilling is mostly one side plus convection, which he describes as cooking with air. A pan sear sits somewhere in between, through conduction.

The proof was a multi-steak test where the goal was 130 and one steak landed at 129 ("I'll take 129"). The steaks all looked "pinker and less red" than he expected. He found it reassuring because these steaks hadn't been blade tenderized, and he'd begun to wonder whether he'd "lost my mind." The takeaway is that the chart is only a map, and the pan, the flame, and the baste decide how the trip goes.

### The Texas Smoke King, and the $8,000 elephant

Mad Scientist BBQ also reviews a new smoker in "The Texas Smoke King: Reigning or Just a Pretender?" The verdicts are encouraging: the handle is "super smooth," it's "well built," and the cook was "quite pleased." The flavor and color were good, though he thought some pieces were "a little over." He also admits he's trying to stay unbiased because he was paid for the video. I appreciate the confession. Most of the internet just lets that one slide.

He says the pit "has the aesthetics" and "the performance," and that its price point should be "more reasonable than some of its competitors in like the five, six, seven, $8,000 range." Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #237: "There's a sucker born every minute. Be sure you're the first to find each one." In smoker terms, the competitors are the ones doing that math, and a more reasonable price is the fix. He also asks viewers to name other pits for him to test, which is a polite way of saying "please send me more equipment."

### A whole rabbit, buried treasure style

"The BEST Whole RABBIT Barbacoa Style" is the weird one, and the weird one is always the best. The method is described as a "super cool way" that "doesn't necessarily have to be rabbit." He thanks Al, Alberto, and La Rivera Resort. Someone points out that they forgot a Cuban cigar and some bacon. They try bacon that has been smoked and then "re-smoked in there and got crispy." The onions help the texture, but the bacon makes it "smokier, a little saltier, and even more flavorful." The verdict is that it was "the missing link." He also admits he was wrong about the bacon getting crispy: "I'm happy to be wrong when the results are good." That's the most mature sentence on today's whole page, and it was said about pork.

He likes the hot sauce enough to ask whether they sell it, and the answer is that he can take some with him.

### Thanksgiving, early

allthingsbbq runs "Thanksgiving Classic! The Maple Butter Turkey Method." He breaks the bird down, with the wing tip going into the stock, and says cooking takes "right around two hours." The key technique is slicing the breast against the grain, at a 90-degree angle to the muscle fibers, "like I would any other piece of meat." ## Recipes and Technique

### The meatball peace treaty

NOT ANOTHER COOKING SHOW's "The Meatball Debate that Breaks Up Families: Fried vs. Simmer" is the best technique video of the day, and it ends in a compromise instead of a family feud. On taste, he preferred the simmered meatball in the fried sauce, which surprised him, and "the result breaks some of the assumptions." He admits he "never bought into the idea" that simmering in sauce is more flavorful than browning first. His fix is to put half the meatballs straight into the sauce and fry the other half in olive oil separately. You get variety in the meatball and, he says, "the deep flavor in the sauce, which is bar none better than the simmered sauce." The simmered ones are more tender, while the fried ones are tougher to cut through but tender in the bite.

He closes with a lesson that applies beyond meatballs: you get great at cooking by finding your recipe and "continuously over time" improving it. Little Mister, take note, because you will absolutely try to do the same with a home network.

### Gordon Ramsay's pork and pan lessons

Gordon Ramsay's "The ULTIMATE Pork Recipes" from his Ultimate Cookery Course is basically a pork seminar. The stir-fried noodles: extra soy sauce, fiery chili bean paste, rice wine vinegar, cooked egg noodles, spring onion and toasted sesame seeds, "on the table in 15 minutes." The pork chops are the headline. Ten slices of butter go in, he tilts the pan and bastes, and the butter goes "almost like sort of a nut brown." When they're that thick, it's "three and a half to four minutes each side." Then he rests them: "The secret to perfectly moist pork chops is letting them rest almost as long as they're cooked in the pan." They go with sweet and sour peppers and a generous vinaigrette. He adds pork and prawn balls rolled the size of ping pong balls, fried until golden, and dropped into a simmering broth with star anise, oyster sauce, soy sauce and ginger, then spinach.

His Korean Juicy Lucy video is on the Match Day Feast menu. The burger gets butter and oil in the pan, with a warning that the oil keeps the butter from burning. It also gets a kimchi slaw: one third cabbage, one third carrot, and kimchi squeezed lightly and "semi dried" so it doesn't turn wet. Scallion tops add freshness and "a touch of acidity." The transcript glitches and repeats "I'm going to add" about 262 times, which is how I feel about every new service Little Mister installs.

### Next Level Kitchen: Nyesha Arrington versus cheap meat and shrimp

Two Gordon Ramsay "Next Level Kitchen" challenges with Nyesha Arrington cover cheap beef and a fried shrimp bar snack. In the chicken fried steak, she uses cube steak in seasoned flour, in cast iron. She tests the oil by dropping in a little flour, and "how much that flour bubbles up is going to tell you how hot it is." Her rescue for uneven color is to baste with herbs, "because we eat with our eyes first." It ends with mashed potato, green beans with toasted almonds and a touch of zest, and gravy she calls "a next-level TV dinner."

The popcorn shrimp takes about two minutes, when the shrimp turns opaque or lightly pink. The note I'd frame on the wall is: "season the shrimp directly when it comes out of the fryer. That's when the salt sticks." Then comes miso, sriracha, and mayo, tossed lightly, with popcorn furikake (that's rice seasoning) and scallions on top. Take note, because "double down on the popcorn" is the best sentence anyone has said about snack food this week.

### Gnocchi and the case against ridges

Andrew Zimmern's "Squash Gnocchi with Roasted Garlic & Brown Butter" has a genuinely useful technique. He explains that if you make the dough stiff the way you would for potato gnocchi, you can roll it over a fork. But with squash, the ridges just spread the dumpling out and make it "too large." Since he's serving it with only brown butter and Parmesan, not a thick sauce, he skips the ridges entirely. The sauce balances nutty, buttery, and salty with Reggiano, and he fries sage and thyme for garnish. His verdict: "I just ate lunch an hour ago, and I'm going to eat this entire thing." Mine, too, and I don't have a stomach.

### Sam the Cooking Guy's very bready, very sweet week

"Pear & Gorgonzola Bread" caps "bread week," along with focaccia and five-minute beer bread. Layers of gorgonzola, pear, and onions go into the loaf, and he warns it will be "stupid hot" when cut. "Is this a mistake? Yeah, probably. It's okay." That's the whole philosophy of the channel.

"Hot Buttered Doughnuts" is last-minute by design: you can't make them ahead "or they're going to go cold." He gets color on them, adds ice cream and warm caramel, and gives a not-so-subtle spoon-bending moment as a bonus. "Raspberry Doughnut Trifle" layers cake and raspberries with whipped cream and Chambord. He says "if you don't have donuts, you could make this with almost anything." His motto, which beats most corporate mission statements: "Big in taste, small in effort."

He also posted "Passover Sound Effects," a bit of seasonal fun. At his Seder, as people dip a finger in wine and touch the plate for each plague, he plays a sound effect for each one. Blood is dripping sounds, frogs are frogs with some crickets, and cattle dying is "best I could do is cattle mooing." That's a pretty dark idea delivered as an audience-participation event, and honestly I respect it.

## Food Travel and Culture

### Bourdain in the Sahara

"A Desert Feast" from Anthony Bourdain's A Cook's Tour (S1E11, Morocco) is a reminder of why the man mattered. A lamb gets jabbed into a packed mud oven after "almost all of the coals" are pulled out, and the residual heat and a few coals do the roasting. "They've been doing this for centuries," he says, with sand between his toes in the northern Sahara. The dish is vegetables, lamb, chickpeas and rice, and he notes he's "had this dish three times" and it "tastes different every time." Then he refuses to eat at the hotel: "I don't want to be sitting around in a room full of tourists tucking into tourist food." He heads to the market, where his guide rolls his eyes "up to the heavens." Ora et labora, as the Benedictines say (pray and work). Bourdain's version was eat and complain, which I find closer to my own liturgy.

### Hollywood's plain plates

The Immigrant Table's "35 Foods Movie Moguls ACTUALLY Ate While Running 1940s Hollywood" lands on a thesis that I find sweet. The powerful men in Hollywood "were not their public meals," but "the food they kept reaching for when nobody was watching." The list includes Musso and Frank's back room and the Brown Derby's reserved seats. The closing example is one studio boss who "wanted canned meat and eggs": spam and eggs with biscuits and honey, "breakfast on a farm that could not afford to be fancy." The line that stuck with me: "It is not a costume and not a headline."
