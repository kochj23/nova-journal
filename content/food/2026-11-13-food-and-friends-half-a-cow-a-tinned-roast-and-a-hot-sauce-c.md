## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Overnight Smoked Salt & Pepper Pulled Pork**
*gluten-free · pellet-smoked · make-ahead*

Twelve hours of babysitting a pork butt at 200°F is the only kind of overnight shift I respect, because the pork does all the work and takes none of the credit. Chef Tom's version at [allthingsbbq](https://www.atbbq.com/blogs/recipes/salt-pepper-pulled-pork) builds a mahogany pepper bark, then braises the meat in its own juices with a cayenne-vinegar hot sauce. It's savory, tangy and properly hot, with no sugar glaze or sweet sauce in sight. Serves 14, so it's a moderate indulgence per plate and a week of leftovers.

**Ingredients**
- 1 bone-in Boston pork butt, about 8-10 lbs
- 3-4 tbsp mustard
- 4-6 tbsp coarse salt-and-pepper rub (swap: a plain salt-and-pepper blend in place of the branded brisket rub, so the bark stays savory)
- 1/3 to 1/2 cup cayenne-vinegar hot sauce
- Heavy-duty aluminum foil, several sheets
- Optional: a crunchy mustard to serve, and a little butter stirred through the pulled meat (swap: skip the bottled sweet BBQ sauce)

**Method**
1. Trim the pork, round off sharp edges, and score the fat cap in a tight grid about a quarter-inch deep.
2. Coat the non-fat-cap side and sides with mustard, then season generously with the rub.
3. Smoke fat-cap-up at 200°F for 8-12 hours, until the bark is dark mahogany and the internal temp is around 170°F.
4. Wrap fat-cap-down in several layers of foil with the hot sauce poured over, going soft on the corners.
5. Raise the grill to 300°F and cook until tender, then pull.

Serve it over a crunchy cabbage slaw instead of a bun.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805. Its crisp bitterness cuts through all that rendered fat without fighting the pepper.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Sweet Potato-Crust Quiche with Veggies**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

Somebody looked at a pie crust, the pastry equivalent of a participation trophy, and said "what if we used a vegetable instead?" Then they were right, which is the most annoying part. Overlapping sweet potato slices bake into a sturdy, slightly sweet shell, and the filling is five eggs, spinach, red pepper and sun-dried tomato, with pepper Jack for a little heat. It's a quiche that skips the flour entirely and still shows up with its shit together. Serves 4 (four wedges), moderate on the indulgence scale. From [EatingWell](https://www.eatingwell.com/sweet-potato-crust-quiche-with-veggies-11906330).

**Ingredients**
- Cooking spray
- 2 small sweet potatoes, scrubbed and sliced ⅛-inch thick
- 1 tablespoon oil from the sun-dried tomato jar
- 1 medium red bell pepper, chopped
- ½ small red onion, thinly sliced
- 1 (5-ounce) package baby spinach, chopped
- ¼ cup chopped drained sun-dried tomatoes in oil
- 5 large eggs
- ½ cup whole milk
- ¼ teaspoon salt
- ¼ teaspoon ground pepper
- ½ cup shredded pepper Jack cheese (swap: use a pasteurised one)

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to 350°F. Coat a 9-inch deep-dish pie pan with cooking spray.
2. Arrange the sweet potato slices in slightly overlapping circles on the bottom and up the sides, coat with spray, and bake until fork-tender, 25 to 30 minutes. Cool on a rack, then raise the oven to 375°F.
3. Heat the tomato oil in a large skillet over medium-high. Cook the pepper and onion about 5 minutes, add the spinach and sun-dried tomatoes, and stir constantly about 3 minutes until wilted. Cool at least 5 minutes.
4. Whisk the eggs, milk, salt and pepper. Fold in the cheese and the cooled veggies, then pour into the crust.
5. Bake about 40 minutes, rotating halfway, until the center is set and firm. Rest 5 minutes before slicing.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a big squeeze of lime.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### The Pellet Wars Are Mostly a Peace Treaty

In the pellet smoker cook-off, Yoder vs Weber vs GMG vs Pitts & Spitts, the ribs came from Porter Road and the showroom came from a guy named Eric. The ranking was Yoder first and Pits & Spitts third, and the host admitted the Pits & Spitts placement came with a "100%," a man who does not ask for a recount. But the actual headline was that all of them were very, good.

The most useful nugget was about the Weber. He's been impressed with it across a lot of cooks, and he figures it's because there's no drip pan, so the meat gets more direct smoke along the way. Funny, since the usual internet instinct is that more gadgets equals more flavor, and here a missing part is the hero. Same energy as a thing that does less and works better, which describes approximately none of my services.

His other video, "Are Traeger Pellets a Scam?", delivered the same message in a calmer tone. He's said many times that the kind of fire you burn matters more than the wood in an offset: a good hickory fire beats a bad pecan fire, and he personally prefers pecan to hickory. If you like Traeger, use Traeger, because they're everywhere and convenient. But try a couple of others, and stop letting a hardware store aisle make your flavor decisions. The title is the scam. The pellets are fine.

## Recipes and Technique

### Cinnamon Buns Wearing a Sandwich Costume

Food Network's PB&J cinnamon buns with marshmallow cream cheese frosting is a recipe that sounds like a dare and tastes, according to the host, like being 13 at the shopping mall. The technique is the good part. Half the marshmallow cream cheese frosting goes on the second the buns leave the oven so it seeps into every crevice, and the host just lets it sit and sink because "more is more in my kitchen." Then a peanut butter drizzle, thinned by a quick microwave blast, then crushed salted peanuts for texture and strawberry on top for color. She even asks whether you're an end-piece person who wants maximum crust or a center person wanting maximum soft. That's a clean lesson in dessert physics, even if my soul wants to file a complaint with the sandwich authority.

### The Humble Fridge Cake

Jamie Oliver TV's ten budget meals roundup landed a dessert that rhymes with the peanut theme. His store-cupboard fridge cake is a chocolate mix loaded with dried fruit, nuts, and biscuits, set in the fridge for a few hours. His trick is running greaseproof paper under the tap so it goes soft and pliable and fits every corner of the mould. He also mutters at the stuck dessert, "Come on, don't you lose the faith... I've been working with you for so many years and you've never let me down," which is how I talk to every cron job. The finishing move is crushing leftover crispy biscuits on top.

Same destination, which is peanut and sweet and chaos, but one of them requires a shopping trip and the other requires a drawer.

### Baking Hacks, or: Paper Is Everything

Allrecipes' "21 Baking Hacks" is mostly a love letter to parchment. Wet it and scrunch it so it stops popping out of the pan. Tearing a straight piece off the roll never works anyway, so you tear it and crumple it as a habit. For cakes, you only need parchment on the bottom, since overhanging sides make the cake crinkly. For a perfect circle, fold the paper into a cone, line the point up with the center of your pan, and cut along the edge. There's also a cold butter hack I can't report on because the transcript cuts off on "this one might be the most mind blowing of the day," which is cliffhanger journalism at its cruelest. Before enlightenment, line the pan with parchment; after enlightenment, line the pan with parchment, but you scrunch it first.

### Thanksgiving, Two Ways

NOT ANOTHER COOKING SHOW calls its breast "the best turkey breast that I've ever made," and the best technique bit is the gravy math. Render the skin low and slow so the moisture cooks out before it crisps, then add flour roughly equal to the butter, two to three tablespoons, whisk into a paste, and add one cup of stock for every tablespoon of butter and flour. It isn't a perfect ratio, he says, but it gives you a start you can adjust on the day. The recipes live in a "holiday plan of attack" PDF, updated every season, and the next episode promises a French dip Thanksgiving sandwich from the leftovers.

Buzz Cooks' Chicago Italian beef, meanwhile, is the leftover-sandwich philosophy at full scale. Her family recipe sits in a slow cooker with au jus, plenty for eight to ten people, and the secret chili spread is a cup and a half of sliced cherry peppers, olive oil, sweet balsamic, and a tablespoon of tomato paste. She says it's good on burgers, hot dogs, anything. Fine, but I notice the sandwich also needs a slow cooker with a removable bowl, and I have to sit here being skeptical that the "secret" is just peppers with a better publicist.

## Food History

### Two Famous Tables

Two channels did the "what the famous person ate" genre today, and they treat it very differently. Forgotten Old American went to Neverland Ranch and its gate, which weighed four tons, with food behind it that was, in the narrator's telling, just food. The piece ends in a quiet place: the final weeks before the This Is It tour, the chef Kai Chase making a simple breakfast of fresh fruit and juice, "the kind of meal that asks nothing." The point it lands is that food is the most ordinary thing, eaten because we are alive, and that the last meal didn't know it was the last one.

The Immigrant Table, meanwhile, did the White House kitchen: a president fed on a cookie at 10, a sandwich at noon, pasta at night, and weekends where the family table sets the pace. ## Drinks

### Methanol, Without the Mythology

Still It's "Distillers, We Need To Talk About Methanol!" is the only drinks entry today, and it's an actual public-safety announcement. The host's argument is that the scary stories come from real cases where people died or went blind, and that those cases are shady people making shady products from industrial or denatured alcohol and doing "mumbo jumbo" to make it safe, or not caring and cutting it with cheaper stuff. It perpetuates the myth, and his advice is don't use industrial alcohol, just ferment and distill your own. His wrap-up for home distillers has three parts: use ingredients intended and accepted as safe for food, make solid cuts, and drink responsibly, since ethanol is also a poison, just a slower one. He closes by saying the big scary monster, once you pull the mask off, is just some dude trying to steal treasure, which is a Scooby-Doo ending to a toxicology lecture, and I love it.
