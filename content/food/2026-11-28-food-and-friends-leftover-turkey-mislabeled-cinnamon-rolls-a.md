### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Smoked Beef Back Ribs, Sauced Like Pork Ribs**

*dairy-free · gluten-free option · fully smoked*

Beef back ribs are what's left after somebody else gets the prime rib, and Meat Church treats them like the Fred Flintstone version of pork ribs. It's cheap, it's absurd, and it makes you look like you own a restaurant. The pellet smoker does the work while you stand there with a drink, which is the most honest form of labor I know. I'd add a pinch of cayenne to the rub for real heat, since these ribs can take it. A crisp cucumber and scallion-green salad on the side keeps the plate low-carb. Serves 2 to 3, and it's a treat.

**Ingredients**

- 1 rack beef back ribs
- 1 T Worcestershire sauce
- Beef rub, applied moderately (swap: use a sugar-free one so the bark stays savoury; the original calls for Meat Church Holy Gospel)
- Cider vinegar, for spritzing
- BBQ sauce for the finish (swap: a sugar-free, vinegar-forward sauce in place of the sweet one in the original)

**Method**

1. Heat the smoker to 250°F, using post oak, hickory or mesquite.
2. Trim any hard fat off the meat side and leave the membrane on. Coat with the Worcestershire, season both sides, and let it adhere for at least 15 minutes, up to an hour.
3. Smoke meat side up. Spritz once with cider vinegar around 2 hours in, or whenever they look dry. Cook to at least 170°F internal, about 2.5 hours in the video.
4. Wrap in two sheets of heavy-duty foil and return to the smoker until tender, just over 200°F in the thick meat between the bones.
5. Unwrap, brush on the sauce, and smoke about 10 more minutes to let it tack up. Cool briefly, slice, and eat like a Flintstone.

Source: [Meat Church, Beef Back Ribs](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/beef-back-ribs)

**Pairs with:** a peaty Scotch, because smoke loves smoke and the beef is rich enough to hold its own.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Slow-Roasted Salmon with Caper Tartar Sauce**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

Foil-packet salmon steams in its own butter, lemon and dill, which is the culinary equivalent of a weighted blanket, and the caper-heavy yogurt tartar sauce does the heavy lifting. It's the rare fish dinner that's nearly impossible to wreck, and I say that as someone who has watched a lot of things get wrecked. Serves 4. Light to moderate, with the butter and mayo doing the indulging. Adapted from [Serious Eats](https://www.seriouseats.com/slow-roasted-salmon-with-tartar-sauce-recipe-12037639).

**Ingredients**
- 1/2 cup plain whole-fat Greek-style yogurt
- 2 tablespoons certified gluten-free mayonnaise
- 1/2 cup capers, drained and chopped
- 2 tablespoons minced fresh dill
- 1 teaspoon fresh lemon juice
- 1 small clove garlic, minced or grated
- 4 salmon fillets, 6 ounces each
- 1 small lemon, thinly sliced
- 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, cut into 8 small pieces
- 1/4 cup fresh dill fronds, divided
- Salt and pepper (go light, the capers are salty)

**Method**
1. Stir the yogurt, mayo, capers, minced dill, lemon juice and garlic together. Season lightly and chill at least 30 minutes.
2. Heat the oven to 275°F, rack in the middle. Set each fillet on its own big sheet of foil, season, and top with 2 or 3 lemon slices, 2 butter pieces and some dill.
3. Fold the foil loosely over each fillet and seal the edges, leaving a little air space. Set the packets on a rimmed baking sheet.
4. Swap: bake until the center hits 145°F and the fish is opaque and flaking, not the source's 115 to 125°F. That runs longer than the source's 20 to 25 minutes, so check with a thermometer. Serve with the tartar sauce.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Eggplant Lasagna**
*vegetarian · Italian · baked · serves 12 · a treat*

Little Mister, the noodles have been fired. Tasty's eggplant lasagna swaps pasta sheets for salted, grilled eggplant slices, which is the one grown-up technique here and the reason this isn't a soggy casserole. You get the same ricotta-mozzarella-parmesan wall of cheese, a garlicky San Marzano sauce, and a broiler finish that makes the top bubble like it has something to prove. It's a big, indulgent pan, so make it for a crowd and don't pretend otherwise.

**Ingredients**
- 2 large eggplants
- 1 teaspoon sea salt, plus more to taste
- Olive oil
- 5 cloves garlic, minced
- ½ teaspoon red pepper flakes
- 28 oz crushed San Marzano tomatoes (2 cans)
- 6 fresh basil leaves, thinly sliced, plus more to garnish
- 1 large egg
- 15 oz ricotta
- 2 cups shredded mozzarella, divided
- 1 cup grated parmesan, divided
- 2 teaspoons Italian seasoning

**Method**
1. Heat the oven to 375°F. Slice the eggplant lengthwise about ⅛ inch thick, salt both sides, rest 10 minutes, then pat dry. Grill in an oiled pan over medium heat, 2-3 minutes per side.
2. Sauté the garlic and pepper flakes in olive oil over medium-low for about 3 minutes. Add the tomatoes and 1 teaspoon salt, simmer 10 minutes, then stir in the basil.
3. Beat the egg and mix with the ricotta, 1½ cups mozzarella, ¾ cup parmesan and the Italian seasoning.
4. In a 9x13-inch dish, layer sauce, eggplant, then ricotta mixture. Repeat, finish with sauce, and top with the remaining cheeses.
5. Bake 45 minutes, then broil on high 5 minutes. Rest 15-20 minutes before cutting, and garnish with basil.

Recipe from [Tasty](https://tasty.co/recipe/low-carb-eggplant-lasagna).

**Pairs with:** a blood orange Italian soda over plenty of ice.

### Still It asks whether stripping runs are worth the trouble

The real meat of today's issue is Still It's "Stripping Runs, Are They Really Needed For Distilling?" The transcript opens mid-thought, and its thesis comes through clearly: "this is all about artistic choice and logistics."

The host starts with a confession from an earlier vodka video. He did a stripping run purely for logistical reasons, since he "couldn't actually tell how much liquid I had because it was all soaked into the bread." So he filled the pot to its absolute max, did the stripping run, and then topped things up with what was left. That turned out to be only about a quart. Rather than do it properly, he combined the low wines with "that leader" and ran the whole thing like a vodka. He admits he paid for it, especially on the Air Still Pro, which he notes "is already not great at reaching the highest ABV possible." That's alcohol by volume, the strength of the spirit. And it still "turned out great," which is the most dangerous sentence in all of distilling.

The framework is simple. Want a cleaner spirit, at some cost in flavor? Distill more: "Add a whole lot of plates," meaning the stages in a column that refine the vapor. Or take a lesson from Irish whisky and do another distillation in a pot still. Want more character and less polish? Strip less, meaning run a smaller portion of the wash through the stripping step. And you can combine these tools to land "back at roughly the same place, but make the logistics easier."

The practical tip is where it gets useful. If you don't want to do a stripping run but you want to mimic double pot distillation, then "don't strip it all and use one or two plates." He says one plate will probably be slightly less clean than double pot distillation, and the transcript ends on "Two plates," just before it could tell us anything about two plates. And a guy who says "screw it" and proceeds to make a good vodka has accidentally performed the best possible argument for not overthinking it.
