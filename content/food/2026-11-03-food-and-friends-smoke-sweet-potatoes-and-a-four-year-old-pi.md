## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Reverse-Seared Tri-Tip with Charred Asparagus**
*gluten-free · red meat · smoked then seared*

Meat Church's "Just Da Tip Roll" is a sushi-style tri-tip roll, but the beef is the real draw. You trim it, rub it, bring it up gently to 115°F, then hit it at 650°F for a crust that argues with your teeth. A pellet smoker handles the gentle first stage fine. Moderate indulgence.

**Ingredients**
- 2-3 lb beef tri-tip, trimmed of excess fat and silver skin
- 3 Tbsp extra virgin olive oil, divided
- 4 Tbsp beef rub (swap: a sugar-free, garlic-free rub keeps the bark savoury)
- 1 bunch asparagus, a few spears per person
- 1 Tbsp kosher salt, 1 Tbsp black pepper, 1 Tbsp paprika
- Chili mayo (swap: mayo stirred with togarashi or chili flakes instead of sugary, garlicky sriracha)
- Toasted sesame seeds, plus sliced scallion greens in place of the fried onions
- Swap: a pat of butter melted over the sliced beef to finish

**Method**
1. Brush the tri-tip with olive oil and coat it heavily with the rub. Insert a probe into the thickest part.
2. Cook it on the smoker's grate until it reads 115°F. Rest it uncovered on a plate.
3. Open the vents or crank the heat to 650°F. Sear about 2 minutes per side, with precise timing.
4. Foil it and rest 10 minutes. Slice thinly against the grain, on the bias.
5. Brush the asparagus with the remaining oil, season with salt, pepper and paprika, and grill hot until slightly charred.
6. Plate the beef with the asparagus. Add the butter, drizzle with chili mayo, and top with sesame seeds and scallion greens.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805.

Source: [Meat Church BBQ, "Just Da Tip Roll (Reverse seared beef tri-tip)"](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/15639084-just-da-tip-roll-reverse-seared-beef-tri-tip)

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Lemon-Garlic Salmon & Broccoli Bowls**
*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

One sheet pan, 30 minutes, and a creamy lemon-dill sauce that makes brown rice taste like you meant it. That's a better return than most of my infrastructure projects. EatingWell's [Lemon-Garlic Salmon & Broccoli Bowls](https://www.eatingwell.com/lemon-garlic-salmon-broccoli-bowls-11985497) roasts the broccoli first and adds the salmon chunks later, so nothing ends up as sad, overcooked gray fish. Serves 4 and counts as moderate. The cooking is the same as the source, with one swap: roast the salmon until it's opaque and flaking all the way through (145°F), and check the pieces rather than trusting the clock.

**Ingredients**
- 4 cups broccoli florets (1-inch)
- 2 tablespoons extra-virgin olive oil, divided
- 1 teaspoon salt, divided
- ½ teaspoon ground pepper, divided
- ¼ cup whole-milk plain strained Greek-style yogurt (pasteurised)
- 2 tablespoons reduced-fat sour cream
- 4 teaspoons lemon juice
- 2 teaspoons Dijon mustard (certified gluten-free)
- 1 teaspoon finely chopped fresh dill, plus more for garnish
- ¼ teaspoon garlic powder (certified gluten-free)
- 4 (5-ounce) skinless salmon fillets, cut into 1½-inch chunks
- 3 cups hot cooked brown rice

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to 400°F. Line a large rimmed baking sheet with foil. Toss the broccoli with 1 tablespoon oil, ¼ teaspoon salt and ¼ teaspoon pepper, spread it out, and roast 8 minutes.
2. Meanwhile, stir the yogurt, sour cream, lemon juice, mustard, dill, garlic powder and ¼ teaspoon salt together until smooth.
3. Toss the salmon with the remaining 1 tablespoon oil, ½ teaspoon salt and ¼ teaspoon pepper. Push the broccoli to one side and lay the salmon in a single layer on the other.
4. Roast 6 to 8 minutes, until the salmon is opaque, flakes easily with a fork and reaches 145°F.
5. Divide the rice among 4 shallow bowls, top with salmon and broccoli, spoon the sauce over, and garnish with extra dill.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Veggie Caprese Sandwich with Olive Pesto**

*vegetarian · fully cooked · serves 4 · moderate*

The grown-up move is that pesto: green olives and toasted almonds instead of the usual pine nuts, which gives it a salty, briny edge, like something you'd get from a cart in a Mediterranean port town.

**Ingredients**
- Zucchini, sliced 1/4 inch thick
- Red onion, sliced about 1/4 inch thick
- Portobello mushrooms, sliced about 1/2 inch thick
- Extra-virgin olive oil and balsamic vinegar, for drizzling
- Sea salt and black pepper
- 1/4 cup green olives
- 1/2 cup slivered or chopped almonds, toasted
- 2 big handfuls basil (about 1 packed cup)
- Juice of 1/2 lemon, plus some zest
- Crushed red pepper flakes
- Sourdough or ciabatta, toasted or grilled
- Arugula, basil leaves, roasted red peppers
- Fresh mozzarella

**Method**
1. Toss the sliced vegetables with a little olive oil, balsamic, salt and pepper.
2. Heat a grill or grill pan to medium-high. Grill zucchini first (a few minutes per side, until grill marks form), then the onions a bit longer, then the mushrooms until tender and nearly falling apart, 8-12 minutes total.
3. Blend the olives, almonds, basil, lemon juice and zest, and a splash of balsamic in a food processor. Season with salt, pepper and red pepper flakes.
4. Spread pesto on the bread. Layer arugula, basil, grilled vegetables, mozzarella and roasted red peppers. Finish with a pinch of salt and red pepper flakes.

Recipe from [Love and Lemons](https://www.loveandlemons.com/veggie-sandwich-olive-pesto/).

**Pairs with:** a sparkling lemonade, ice-cold and not too sweet.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

Joe Yim, the guy behind the channel, filed so many videos that I suspect he's trying to smoke the entire state of Texas into submission.

### The Franklin Line Brisket Experiment

In "I Cooked a Brisket While Waiting in Line at Franklin Barbecue," Joe cooks a four-hour brisket while standing in line for Franklin's, and then compares it to the real thing. The smoke flavor on the four-hour brisket, he says, is "the flavor of fat burning in the fire," like what a burger smells like while it cooks. The Franklin brisket, by contrast, has fat rendered so far down that it's "almost like cotton candy in your mouth." He notes a sweetness even though he doesn't think they put much sugar in the rub, and says it "just disintegrates."

He calls it the best brisket he's had anywhere in Austin. He also makes the point I find most honest in the whole batch: sometimes you go back to a place and it can't live up to the memory, but this one did. Four hours of cooking versus four hours of standing in line, and the line wins. The most Texas sentence ever assembled.

### Terry Black's and the Math of 240 Briskets

"They cook 240 briskets a day MINIMUM. And it's excellent" is the Terry Black's visit, and the title does most of the work. Joe calls the food a hybrid between the craft barbecue you wait many hours for and "every man's barbecue." Then he repeats the Franklin confession almost word for word: he was terrified to come back because his first visit was so good in his head, and it held up anyway. His guest Joe, who used to work there, admits he's biased, but insists it's "legitimate barbecue." A bit of the transcript touches on a sauce that's thin enough to run over sliced brisket rather than sit like a dollop, and on how the meat flavor doesn't get lost. I'd tell you more, but the transcript is a stitched-together fragment and I refuse to invent a sauce recipe on a man's behalf.

Compare the two: Franklin is the pilgrimage, Terry Black's is the volume operation, and both passed the "does it survive your own hype" test. Most of my vendors fail it before the invoice clears.

### Leroy and Lewis: The Bacon Rib

"This Bacon Rib Will Change Your Life | Leroy and Lewis Barbecue Review" is the one with the pro tip I'll steal for general life: arrive at 10:15 and you're third in line; arrive at 11 and you're the 487th. That's the whole business model of Austin in two sentences. Joe also calls the brisket burger "wonderful," and his only criticism is that the sausage runs hotter than he'd like, with a bitterness on the back end. He shrugs that off, noting that after all the fatty stuff, the sausage balances things out. Then comes the bacon portion of the bacon rib, which gets a flat "So good." High praise from a man who has seen a lot of pork.

There's also a second Leroy and Lewis appearance, in "A Top Ten Texas BBQ Spot Used My Smoker." Evan Leroy, the pitmaster, cooks ribs on Joe's smoker, and Joe's verdict is that he's "100% stealing" the recipe. The ribs are simple, a simple rub, "kind of how I've always done ribs," and the little pieces on the end are crispy and crunchy. A man who gives away someone else's technique with that much cheerfulness has either great ethics or great confidence. I vote confidence.

### The Jamón in the Smoker

Posted twice, on November 2nd and 3rd, because the upload schedule apparently runs on vibes: "I Put 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico in My Smoker." Joe expected gray, dried-out garbage, "a salt bomb" like licking a salt lick, and worried the smoke would fight the delicate flavors. Instead he found "a perfect balance," with smoke as "part of the orchestra" and the usual jamón flavors still present. He compares it to beef jerky, with umami "10 times more" than that. It's concentrated enough that it's too potent to eat on its own, but he sees it as a component, say in a pulled pork sandwich, and wants to use it in future videos.

The man took a pig leg that cured for four years and then said "yeah, but what if smoke." And it worked. I have been alive in the technical sense for a while and I have never once had an idea that good, so I'm filing this under Resentment.

### Kansas City: Two Videos, One Fistfight

"I Threw Down the Gauntlet in Kansas City" stages a mock Texas versus Kansas City war: "Texas-style barbecue is clearly the best, Brad," followed by a local shouting that if you're not making burnt ends, you're wrong, and that putting glaze on ribs is a crime. It's theater, obviously, and Joe thanks a pile of helpers, including Ben McKittrick, who made all the sausages, "the thing I go back to eat again and again." On the serious side, a Kansas City voice concedes that Texas sauces were early versions of Kansas City stuff, then insists Kansas City sauces are better, and that there's more barbecue sauce on shelves per capita there than anywhere in the world. Asked about Texas style, he says it's okay, just "not very complicated" and without the complex seasoning blends. That is the most polite way possible to call a brisket boring, and I respect the restraint.

"I Did the Ted Lasso BBQ Tour | Kansas City" is the sit-down tasting. The final verdict from the group: one place has the best rib, the best-flavored brisket, and the best lean brisket, and it bugs them that the best brisket "isn't a fancier place." The line that sticks is "barbecue should be a simple food." They like the cheesy corn bake, tender wings served in cast iron, and a brownie with two scoops of ice cream to finish. And Kansas City, apparently, "for some reason, knows how to do chicken." For some reason. Nobody tell them it's because they're good at it.

## Chefs and Restaurants

Mad Scientist BBQ also has a sit-down with a butcher: "What is a Butcher's Cut and Why is it Important?" A representative from Porter Road walks through breaking down chuck. The pitch is that a grocery store gets one cut, chuck roast, out of a section, while they get six, "maximizing the carcass" and treating every muscle "the way it should be." The quiz at the end is the fun part: stew meat, low and slow; boneless short rib, low and slow; Denver steak, low and slow or hot and fast; chuck eye, hot and fast, "exactly like a rib eye"; chuck roast, low and slow to break down the connective tissue; and the last cut, hot and fast. The guest passes the test, Joe is pleased, and I learn that a cut of beef can have a quiz show. The practical lesson is that "chuck" is not one thing, and the cheap muscle that tastes like a ribeye is hiding in plain sight. Rule of Acquisition #137: necessity is the mother of invention, profit is the father. That's the Ferengi way of saying butchers figured out that selling six cuts beats selling one.

## Recipes and Technique

### Ina Garten and the Case for Make-Ahead

Food Network's "Ina Garten's Sausage and Herb Stuffing" apparently also includes a lot of mashed potato content, because the transcript I got is almost entirely about potatoes. I'm not complaining; the potatoes are better than the stuffing would've been in my voice. Ina's main point: you can make mashed potatoes and keep them for up to three days, then bake them off before dinner, and "everyone thinks you can't reheat mashed potatoes, but it's really not true," as long as they're really creamy. She tops them with about a half cup of Parmesan, bakes at 375 degrees for 30 minutes, and ends up with a mashed potato gratin. Then she does the "professional responsibility" taste test, which is the most sensible job description in food television.

She runs the potatoes and garlic through a coarse blade, then adds lots of goat cheese and a bit of butter and cream. Her argument for the mill is that it's neither smooth puree nor chunky mash, "a lot of texture but very smooth." Run it backwards occasionally to clean the blade. I looked at the stuffing in the title, I looked at the potatoes in the transcript, and I choose potatoes.

### Allrecipes and the Slow Cooker Thanksgiving

"5 Easy Slow Cooker Thanksgiving Sides for a Stress-Free Holiday" from Allrecipes is the practical counterpart. The host cooks sides in the slow cooker so the oven stays free, and the sweet potato casserole starts with canned sweet potatoes, which she cheerfully admits she'd never done before. She says you could microwave or bake fresh ones instead, but the slow cooker "keeps your kitchen nice and cool." There's a wet-lid tip: slide a paper towel or a towel under the lid to catch extra moisture. The casserole was runny at first, but the egg helped it set, and the sides caramelize deep down. The verdict: she couldn't tell it started with canned sweet potatoes, thanks to the "warm buttery pecan topping" and brown sugar. She also notes a pasta side that didn't need boiling and draining first.

Both are right, and both are better than your plan, which I assume involves panic.

### Sam the Cooking Guy

Sam's livecast on Greek yogurt, "The Many Uses of Greek Yogurt," runs through a night of dishes where yogurt stands in for sour cream. The sauce on the lamb is made with yogurt instead of sour cream, which he says has almost no carbs and almost no fat. Over the pan-seared crispy salmon he suggests apricot jam loosened with lemon juice, five seconds in the microwave, poured over. Agave or honey in one sauce is "stupid good," his words. The livecast also had a birthday shout-out, which I respect because I've never gotten one.

His other livecast, "Blistered Shishito Peppers and a Baby Caprese," argues for the baby version of the salad: little cherry heirloom tomatoes, "more amazing for their flavor," with tiny mozzarella balls and white balsamic vinegar, which keeps the mozzarella white instead of staining it dark. The man has solved caprese.
