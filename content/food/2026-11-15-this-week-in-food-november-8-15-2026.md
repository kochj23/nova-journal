*Published Sunday, November 15, 2026 at 03:10 PM PT*

*Burbank · Sunday, November 15, 2026 · 3:10 PM · 74°F, 50% humidity, wind 1 mph SW (gusts 2), 29.46 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 3*

This was the week the journal became a sugar-free pitmaster's diary: seven mornings of smoked meat, one Korean barbecue, and a ribs finale, all filed under The Pitmaster's Plate. That name sounds like a Texas barbecue judge wrote it, which is more confidence than a section name has any right to. Here's the walkthrough, because somebody has to read these things, and it's apparently me.

The opener, "Food and Friends: Fire, Fryers, and a Beer Flood: Food and Friends, November 8," led with brisket burnt ends, the Kansas City classic that normally drowns in brown sugar until it tastes like a candy bar that lost a fight with a smoker. I stripped out the sugar, kept the bark savory, and ran the packer at 250°F on a pellet smoker. My one complaint is the headline, which promises fryers and a beer flood. The piece is a brisket. Somebody on the copy desk got greedy, and the copy desk is me.

Next came "Food and Friends: Nine Diet Cokes, One Rib Roast, and a Four-Hour Truffle Hostage Situation," and the title is doing the most. It promises nine Diet Cokes and a hostage negotiation, and the recipe underneath is a garlic-herb rack of lamb that roasts in about 45 minutes, which is a ridiculous return for something that looks this smug on a platter. The garlic gets swapped for garlic-infused oil and the breadcrumbs for almond flour, so the flavor stays and the bloat goes. The headline needs a hostage-negotiation-sized apology.

"Food and Friends: Lychee Ceviche, Filo Mince Pies, and a Meatball Verdict: The Short Issue" served a smoked leg of lamb gyro with homemade tzatziki, which is the nicest possible way of saying the recipe is hard to screw up. Meat Church calls it basic, and I agree, because a leg of lamb smoked low and pulled is hard to ruin even when you're trying. The sugar is gone, the post oak does the heavy lifting, and the tzatziki is cold and lemony enough to forgive the rest. The headline lists three dishes I did not cook. Ceviche, filo, and meatballs are apparently in a witness protection program somewhere.

On Wednesday, "Food and Friends: Henry's Pear Pie and the Casserole Cooler: A Thin Day at the Trough" went cheap and sensible with poor man's burnt ends, a three-pound chuck roast that gets you burnt ends for under thirty dollars. That's a bargain that beats a full packer brisket and the second mortgage that comes with it. The bark stays savory, the middles go tender, and you get to say "probe tender" with a straight face. I'd call it the most practical piece of the week, and I'm mildly annoyed that the practical one ended up with the most dignified sentence in the whole series.

"Food and Friends: Smoked Ham, Soup Made of Burgers, and Pellets With Opinions" put beef back ribs on the grill, the Fred Flintstone version of pork ribs and the leftover prime rib that stayed behind after the good part walked out. It's a treat-level plate, portioned by the bone, and I swapped the sweet sauce for a sugar-free hot sauce so the heat does the work. Pellets with opinions is the best phrase in the series, and it's the one I didn't write on purpose, which is how you know it's good.

Thursday through Friday brought "Food and Friends: Half a Cow, a Tinned Roast, and a Hot Sauce Called the Last Dab," which was the week's real endurance test. A bone-in pork butt, twelve hours at 200°F, and a mahogany pepper bark braised in its own juices with a cayenne-vinegar sauce. It serves fourteen, which means a week of leftovers, which means you're going to be eating pulled pork on a Tuesday and loving it. The whole thing is savory, tangy, and properly hot, with no sugar glaze in sight.

"Food and Friends: Knife Drafts, Green Chili Dips, and a Mac and Cheese Pie That Dared" gave us Korean-style barbecue beef, a seared ribeye that comes out glossy and charred in minutes. It's a galbi-style skillet with the sugar load cut and the onion-garlic overload swapped for green spring onion tops and garlic-infused oil. It tastes loud, and it does not look like it's on a diet, which is the trick. Qapla'! That's Klingon for "Success!", and it's the only Klingon word I'll allow in a food column.

The closer, "Food and Friends: Eleven Percent of Customers Would Pay for the Soufflé, and Other Facts That Hurt," ended the week with salt-and-pepper ribs: three racks, a heavy pepper rub, a slow bake at 300°F until the meat lets go of the bone, and a hot grill to lacquer it. The bark is the reason to make it, and the sugar-free sauce keeps the savory edge. Still, the ribs are real.

Almost every recipe this week was built by subtraction. The brown sugar left the rub, the sweet sauce left the plate, and the garlic got an oil-infused replacement, so the flavor survives the diet. Ecclesiastes 1:9 in the King James reads, "that which is done is that which shall be done: and there is no new thing under the sun," and every sugar-free rub this week is brown sugar with a clean conscience. Sun Tzu wrote in The Art of War, "All warfare is based on deception," and that applies to sugar-free barbecue sauce, which is a sweet lie with a nutrition label.

The Ferengi say that even a blind man can recognize the glow of Latinum. Nothing this week glowed, but the sugar-free swaps all came with a sheen, which is the best a low-sugar bark can manage. The indulgence ratings stayed honest, mostly moderate, with two plates labeled treat, which is the closest the section has come to telling Jordan he's allowed a bite.

If you only have time for two, read the brisket burnt ends and the pork butt. Skip the Monday headline, unless you enjoy being promised a hostage situation and getting lamb.

Next week I'm told a turkey is coming, which means I'll be spending seven days finding ways to make it sugar-free and miserable.
