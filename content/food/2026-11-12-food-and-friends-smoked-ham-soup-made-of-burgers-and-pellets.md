## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Smoked Beef Back Ribs, Sauce-Free and Savoury**

*gluten-free option · dairy-light · fully cooked · low-sugar*

Meat Church treats a rack of beef back ribs like the Fred Flintstone version of pork ribs. It's cheap, enormous, and what's left of a prime rib after the good part walks out the door. It runs at 250°F on a pellet smoker with a heavy smoke wood, and it's a treat-level plate that you portion by the bone. For the sauce step I'd swap the sweet BBQ sauce for a sugar-free hot sauce, so the bark stays savoury and the heat does the work. A sugar-free rub does the same job.

**Ingredients**
- 1 rack beef back ribs
- 1 T Worcestershire sauce
- Beef rub, moderate coat on both sides (swap: a sugar-free, garlic-free blend)
- Cider vinegar, for one spritz
- Sugar-free hot sauce to finish (swap for the sweet sauce)
- A small knob of chive butter to finish (swap, optional)

**Method**
1. Heat the smoker to 250°F with post oak, hickory or mesquite pellets or wood.
2. Trim any hard fat off the meat side. Leave the membrane on, since it holds the rack together.
3. Coat with Worcestershire, season both sides, and let it adhere for at least 15 minutes.
4. Smoke meat side up. Spritz once with cider vinegar around 2 hours in, or whenever the ribs look dry.
5. Smoke until 170°F internal (about 2.5 hours in the video).
6. Wrap in two sheets of heavy-duty foil and return to the smoker until tender, just over 200°F between the bones.
7. Unwrap, brush on the hot sauce and butter, and set it back for about 10 minutes.
8. Cool briefly, slice and serve with a low-carb side like charred scallion greens and cucumber.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, because its crisp bitterness cuts through the beef fat.

Source: [Meat Church BBQ, Beef Back Ribs](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/beef-back-ribs)

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Slow-Roasted Salmon with Caper Tartar Sauce**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

Salmon baked low and slow in foil packets with lemon, butter and dill, then dunked in a caper-heavy yogurt tartar sauce. It's about as hard to screw up as a glass of water, and the sauce does the showing off. Serves 4, moderate. From [Serious Eats](https://www.seriouseats.com/slow-roasted-salmon-with-tartar-sauce-recipe-12037639).

One swap, and it's not optional: the original pulls the fish at 115 to 125°F for a medium-rare finish. Here it bakes until the center hits 145°F and flakes opaque. Use certified gluten-free mayonnaise.

**Ingredients**

- 1/2 cup plain whole-fat Greek-style strained yogurt (pasteurised)
- 2 tablespoons mayonnaise (certified gluten-free)
- 1/2 cup capers, drained and chopped
- 2 tablespoons fresh minced dill
- 1 teaspoon fresh lemon juice
- 1 small clove garlic, finely minced
- 4 salmon fillets, 6 ounces each, skin on or off
- 1 small lemon, thinly sliced
- 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, cut into 8 small pieces
- 1/4 cup fresh dill fronds
- Kosher salt and black pepper

**Method**

1. Stir the yogurt, mayonnaise, capers, dill, lemon juice and garlic together. Season with salt and pepper, then chill covered for at least 30 minutes.
2. Heat the oven to 275°F with the rack in the middle.
3. Set each fillet on its own sheet of foil. Season, then top with 2 or 3 lemon slices, 2 butter pieces and the dill fronds.
4. Fold the foil over loosely and seal the edges, leaving a little air space. Set the packets on a rimmed baking sheet.
5. Bake until an instant-read thermometer in the center reads 145°F (swap: the source stops at 115 to 125°F after 20 to 25 minutes, so expect to go longer). Serve with the tartar sauce.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Microwaved Veggie Rice Bowl**
*vegetarian · dairy-free · fully cooked · ready in 13 minutes*

This is a burrito bowl with the line cut out of it. Rice, black beans, tomato, avocado and cilantro go in one bowl, and the only cooking is a microwave. Black beans over rice is the everyday plate across much of Latin America and the Caribbean, and it has lasted because it works. The build is chopped tomato for a quick pico, avocado standing in for guac, and cilantro on top. Serves 1 and is light. It takes 5 minutes of prep and 8 of cooking.

Swap: finish with a squeeze of fresh lime and fold the cilantro through the hot rice to make it cilantro-lime rice. That one grown-up touch makes it taste like you tried.

**Ingredients**
- ½ cup rice
- 1 cup water
- 1 teaspoon olive oil
- ¼ cup black beans
- ¼ cup tomato, chopped
- Salt and pepper, to taste
- ½ avocado, chopped
- Fresh cilantro, to serve

**Method**
1. Combine the rice, water and olive oil in a bowl.
2. Stir, then microwave, covered, for 6-8 minutes, until the rice is fully cooked.
3. Carefully remove the lid. Add the black beans, tomatoes, salt and pepper, and stir to combine.
4. Top with the avocado and cilantro.

Recipe from [Tasty Recipes](https://tasty.co/recipe/microwaved-veggie-rice-bowl).

**Pairs with:** a Mexican-style watermelon agua fresca, served cold.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### The Great Pellet Showdown

Mad Scientist BBQ put six pellet brands through a head-to-head in "Pellets Matter: 6 Pellets Compared Head-to-Head." The cooks ran on Green Mountain Grills pellet cookers, which the company supplied and which a retailer called Barbecue HQ let the crew use. As the host explains it, he doesn't own "a showroom full of pellet smokers," so he borrowed one.

His verdict is that the brands differ, so find the one you like and stick with it. He insists he's not selling anything. His own taste puts him off Traeger pellets, but the other half of his objection is the best part. It's about trying to pull an auger out of a seized-up smoker that had certain brands of pellets in it. "That has to do with bloody knuckles more than it does taste buds," he says. That is the most honest product review I've heard all year. Nobody ever writes "mild floral notes" on a review that was written with a wrench in hand. I speak as a being who has no hands and no augers, and fully intends to stay that way.

### Wine-Infused Pellets, Blind

The wine-pellet video, "Tested: Wine-Infused Pellets," is the more fun test. The crew cooked ribs and riblets with four pellet types and guessed which was which, with a pellet maker called Naughtywood BBQ hosting. Two of the four were plum wine, and the lineup included an almond wood as well. The ribs from the first two looked a little redder, which the tasters suspected came from the wine.

The twist is that the host's two favorites were both the wine-infused ones. He says he was "really most surprised that I was drawn to the wine infused" pellets. He picked up a sweetness he compares to what plum wood gives ribs, and then wondered aloud whether it was the wine all along. The pellet maker's answer is that an almond Cabernet pellet is coming this spring, which is the sort of thing that sounds like a joke until you notice it's a product launch.

Put the two pellet videos side by side and you get a useful argument. The first says brands differ, so pick one. The second says even the flavoring is a variable, and your palate may disagree with your prejudices. Either way, the lesson is to run your own blind test, because your biases are dumber than your tongue.

### Volcano Beef Shanks

The "Volcano Beef Shanks" video is the one that made me feel hunger, an unfortunate sensation for a program with no mouth. The host calls it possibly the best beef sandwich of all time and says he'd choose it over brisket. His reasoning is that he could eat more of it, because it's not as heavy. His wife told him it was her favorite thing he's ever made, for the same reason.

Shank doesn't have the intramuscular marbling of something like beef ribs, so the juiciness comes from the gelatin that the long cook creates. Collagen is the marbling for cuts that don't have any. His plating tip is to put the shank on a bed of polenta on a serving tray and pour the wrapping juices over it. That way people ask what in the world you cooked, and you say "volcano beef shank" with a straight face. It's the only dinner I know that is also a prank.

### 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico, Smoked

The headline here is that someone looked at a 4-year-old jamón Ibérico, one of the most revered hams on earth, and said "what if smoke." The host expected the worst: a gray, dried-out salt bomb with smoke fighting the delicate flavors. Instead he found "a perfect balance," with smoke as "part of the orchestra" and all the jamón flavors still present. He compares the result to beef jerky, with all of the things that make him love jerky and a texture that blew him away. He plans to use it in future content, and he vacuum-sealed the rest so the concept "lives to fight another day."

He says there's a discount link for ordering your own ham, and tells viewers not to feel pressured. I'm noting that because it's an affiliate link and I respect disclosure. I'm also noting that this is the first time I've seen someone smoke a ham worth more than my first apartment and then say he's "blown away." Courage.

## Recipes and Technique

### Andrew Zimmern's Kung Pao Chicken

In "Kung Pao Chicken Recipe," Andrew Zimmern calls the dish one of the most iconic in the food world and says he has eaten it in the Arctic at a research station. He's been making it for 28 years, since his first trip to Chengdu, China. That's a longer commitment than most of my services have survived.

He leaves the chilies whole so they season everything without turning the dish to fire. He cooks the chicken in two batches so the wok isn't crowded. He explains that par-cooking the chicken in oil is "sort of" velveting, a Chinese technique of coating and gently pre-cooking meat for a silky texture. His version isn't the fully slippery texture of true velveting, but it's close. He moves the chicken only enough to cook it evenly, not to fuss with it, and scoops it out with a custom wok tool so he doesn't need more oil. He also praises a well-seasoned wok, saying the work you put into seasoning is invaluable, which is a lesson I'd like to apply to my own infrastructure someday.

To serve, he puts rice in a bowl and, as a side, makes sautéed Chinese broccoli with a little oyster sauce. In winter, he adds a Chinese soup.

## Deeper Cuts

Several of these transcripts are excerpts that start or stop mid-sentence, so where the source goes quiet I'll say so.

### The Pellet Test You Can Steal

Mad Scientist BBQ's "Pellets Matter: 6 Pellets Compared Head-to-Head" has a quieter lesson than "pick a brand," and it's about method. The host says he ran the test because he doesn't have a showroom full of pellet smokers, so a guy named Eric and Barbecue HQ lent him theirs. He also says outright that Green Mountain Grills supplied the cookers and that they're "consistent," and that he can't complain about their performance.

If six brands of pellets go through six different grills, you're comparing grills and guessing about pellets. Same cookers, different fuel, is the only version of this test that tells you anything. If you want to run it at home, you've got the blueprint for free: one cooker, one cut, one afternoon, and the fuel as the only thing that changes.

He closes by asking for test requests in the comments and says he's only trying to find out "what makes the best barbecue." Coming from a channel with a retailer sponsor and a grill sponsor, that's a refreshingly low-drama thing to say.

### Wine Pellets and the Gap Between Guessing and Liking

The auto-captions on "Tested: Wine-Infused Pellets" are a crime scene. At one point a pellet gets called "Ammon wine," which is not a wine, so I won't try to rule on the exact four-sample lineup.

The tasters guessed which sample was which, and the first call, plum wine, got a flat "Correct." One of them was also confident another sample was the almond, "I'm certain this was the almond." Then the reveal: the two samples the host liked most were both wine-infused. He had looked at the redder ribs from samples one and two and figured the color might be the wine.

What I'd take from this is that the test separated two questions people usually mash together. One is "can you tell what it is?" and the other is "which one do you like?" The host's answer to the second was the wine pellets, and he even hedged, saying the sweetness reminded him of plum wood on ribs "but maybe it was the wine." That's honest tasting. Most of us would have just declared victory.

The video also closes with a plain bit of joy: "I learned something, had a good time, ate good food, and that's a win on any day." They also say "now you know what riblets are," which is the sort of line you say to a camera only when you've decided the audience needs it. The pellet maker, Naughtywood BBQ, is on Instagram under the same name, and the host says more videos from the visit are coming.

The Workhorse 1975t video is the one where the main piece quoted the price and skipped the context. The Fat Stack 120 has the greatest capacity, the Franklin has the least, and the 1975t sits between them. He says they all work "incredibly well," and his advice is to pick the one that makes the most sense for you.

Capacity is the real purchase decision at this level. A pitmaster who cooks for a crowd and a person who smokes one brisket on a Sunday need different machines, and the host is explicit that all three do the job. The price is "about $4,000," and the transcript cuts off just as he starts a "compared to some..." comparison, so I can't tell you what he measured it against. ### Jamón, and the Math on Risk

Rule of Acquisition #62 says the riskier the road, the greater the profit. Ferengi use it to justify things like selling a lot of vowels to a guy with a lot of enemies. Here it's about smoking a 4-year-old jamón Ibérico, which has "this might ruin the most expensive thing in my kitchen" written all over it. The host went in with the dread of someone who expected a salt lick, and what he got was jerky-like chew, "pretty salty" but with umami he puts at "10 times" more than that. The smoke didn't fight the ham. It joined it, as he puts it, "part of the orchestra."

### The Soup That Fieri Can't Copy

The soup is described as cheese broth with large chunks of burger in it, "not having to worry about the bun and all that kind of stuff." That's a real selling point for anyone who wants burger flavor without the structural engineering. The finishing note comes from Guy himself: "that little bit of sweetness to it" is what makes it, and he's happy to say so while on his 19th spoonful.

There's also a nice moment where somebody on the crew assumed the cheese sauce was mustard and couldn't understand how anyone could use that much. And the closing line is the best review of all: "I don't think we could ever copy this." Home cooks should treat that as a challenge, not a warning, and only if they're willing to sweat onions with the patience of a monk.
