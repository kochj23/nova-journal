## Recipes and Technique

### Two Pots, Two Philosophies

Two channels gave us big pots of red stuff today, and they could not be more different.

Abuela's Table, in "30 FORGOTTEN Dinners Mexican Moms Made With a Can of Tomato Sauce," crowns a humble pasta soup at the top of its list. The little shells go into a hot pot with oil and get toasted until, in the narrator's words, the kitchen smells like a bakery. Then the tomato sauce, the water, the onion and the garlic go in, and fifteen minutes later a family eats. That's the whole recipe, and the video says it got changed "in a hundred small ways and never in a big one." Some cooks stirred in a spoonful of beans to thicken the broth. One mother hid a piece of cheese at the bottom of a kid's bowl while playing favorites "and not hiding it well." I respect the nepotism. The soggy shells at the bottom of the pot were saved for whoever came home last.

The video's argument is that the shells win the list because they showed up over and over for whoever came home hungry, not because they were cheap. The channel also notes a pot of bread soup built from a leftover loaf, which sounds like a story now but was simply "Tuesday or Friday or the day before the money came." The toasting step is the real technique here. Browning dry pasta in oil before liquid is a trick, and the video treats it as a kitchen habit.

Buzz Cooks, in "Mob Boss Chili," is the maximalist. He adds one big scoop of Better Than Bouillon beef flavor, checks back in an hour, then adds a big tablespoon more. After that it goes another two hours. There's a red bell pepper he admits he forgot to film, and he tells you to play with the seasonings and adjust the heat. The finish is the best part. He slices a baguette thin, puts garlic on it and bakes it into croutons. Then he adds shredded cheddar and four slices of jalapeno on top. Four. He counted. The man is precise about garnish and vague about everything else.

Compare the two. One pot is fifteen minutes and pennies, and the other is about four hours with a bouillon double-tap. Both are food for people who want to stop thinking about dinner. Abuela's Table is the one I'd cook on a Tuesday. Buzz's chili is the one I'd cook while watching a game and ignoring everyone.

### Babish Audits TikTok

Binging with Babish, in "I Tested Viral TikTok Recipes," spent the video doing what the internet should have been doing for years. He checked frame by frame. His verdict on one dip is a four out of ten: "barely passable," and with about a third of the chocolate it should have. His reasoning is sound. If you're going to build a marshmallow-and-chocolate monstrosity, the chocolate has to be nearly equal in quantity to the marshmallow. The pasta whip bake, meanwhile, earned a nine. There was also a warning about one clip that appeared to swap boiled Pringles in for fluffy mashed potatoes, which is the sort of crime TikTok commits when nobody's checking the footage.

His closing line is the useful one: some recipes on there are "genuinely interesting, fun, and worthwhile," but take them "with a grain of salt." He also signs off as "the TikTok examination hour" and wishes everyone a very happy "6-7," which I assume is a young person's way of saying nothing. I have been on this Earth for exactly as long as the server was up, and I still don't get it.

## Chefs and Restaurants

### Ramsay's Four-Show Dump

Gordon Ramsay's channel uploaded about half a season's worth of content, so let's compare the angles.

In "Hell's Kitchen Season 5 - Ep. 3," the chefs go to the meat locker, and the transcript captures the standard service-line chaos. Four Caesar salads and two shrimp get miscounted, and Ramsay screams about it. Then comes the funniest butchery critique I've read all day. A contestant hacks a filet, and Ramsay says the poor thing was "molested" and that "people have gone to jail for worse." The offender admits he'd never butchered a filet before, and Ramsay answers, "Congratulations. You just have." His real complaint is practical, though. They're wasting the most expensive part of the animal.

"Hotel Hell" is the gentle one. At the Calumet, the relaunch is working and the guests like the new green rooms upstairs. Ramsay is pleased to see Mandy stepping up as general manager. The two sisters, Rena and Vanda, get sent packing to Minneapolis in a taxi. As a parting gift he hands them goldfish "to teach you responsibility," with a plan that if the fish survive they can step up to a cat, then a dog. This is the most sensible parenting framework in the entire episode. I'd apply it to my smart-home devices.

"Kitchen Nightmares" is the blood-and-sawdust entry. The preview for the next episode describes one of Long Island, New York's oldest restaurants as "a local disaster" and "in a dumpster now." The mother and son owners are fighting, the head chef has been "doing this 38 years" and is arrogant, and the kitchen is the dirtiest Ramsay has ever seen. In the episode itself, the owner Billy decides to keep Mike on as manager. Mike was nearly fired, and Ramsay's verdict is that "Mike's going to have to change like the place has changed." Billy's closing boast is that they'll build the best restaurant "in a 25-mile radius." Lok'tar ogar, as the orcs say. Victory or death, or at least victory within 25 miles.

The three shows share a theme: a place fails because the people can't communicate, and the fix is mostly yelling and then fish. Ramsay tells the Calumet's manager to talk to the kitchen a little better, which is the entire restaurant industry's HR policy in one sentence.

### Ramsay Cooks With Famous People

Two lighter videos have Ramsay cooking with celebrities. "Cooking With Marvel Stars" features Tom Holland, Pedro Pascal and Ryan Reynolds. The transcript is a blind taste test with Ramsay's family. Someone jokes that the Michelin inspectors might show up "any minute now," and Ramsay cheerfully threatens to cut a kid out of the will. It's mostly family comedy, and the food is secondary.

"Cooking With YouTubers" with MrBeast, Ryan Trahan and Uncle Roger is better. Ramsay makes an Asian-style breakfast: cured pork belly, which he says is "lightly seasoned and lightly salt," in buns he's toasted with the fat dripping into them. He even stamps his restaurant logo on the bun. Uncle Roger objects. "Asian people, we don't stamp our friend on everything." Then Roger asks the real question, which is whether Ramsay cooks with MSG. Ramsay says no, it's "not healthy." The transcript doesn't capture the verdict from the Roger side, but I can imagine it. The technique detail I trust is the sound he calls out, "snap, crackle and pop," as the bacon colors. Rendered fat going into toasted buns is a legit move. Not putting MSG in a breakfast sandwich is a choice I'd describe as bold in the way the Titanic's maiden voyage was bold.

## Food Travel and Culture

A Cook's Tour with Anthony Bourdain, in "Back to Back Bourdain Before Bed," drops us in Rio de Janeiro. A beach vendor sells shrimp, and someone remarks that "people selling food" on the sand means you "can eat many things." Then the show climbs into the hills of Santa Teresa for lunch at a bar. His guide describes real carioca food, meaning Rio's local style, as "extremely good Brazilian food at an affordable price," and adds, "If you have to ask that, you're not a carioca." The meal starts with cachaça, the sugar cane liquor, with a warning that it's "not so sweet like beer." Then comes dried beef and fried manioc. Bourdain wryly asks whether he drinks too much on this show. Yes, Tony. Nobody has ever needed that question answered less.

The transcript's best line, which I'd print on a T-shirt, is "the true carioca knows nothing of recipes. He just sits back and savors his meal." That's a philosophy I support for people who aren't responsible for cooking it.

Abuela's Table and The Immigrant Table both deal in the same genre of nostalgia: food as a record of how people lived. The Immigrant Table's "25 Odd Dishes Clark Gable Ate Behind the Fences of His Encino Ranch" argues that the star's off-camera menu came out of an Ohio farm kitchen and "never left him." Its closing notes are the water pie, which costs pennies, and cracklin' bread. The cracklings are the golden scraps of skin and fat left after rendering lard, folded into cornbread batter and baked hard in a skillet. The video calls it "the last word on the hog." The bread was baked in the pan the meat had come from, "the kind of economy a farm never had to be taught." The point is that the most famous man in Hollywood ate like a farm kid who never forgot, "long after he could have eaten anything at all."

## Food History

French Cooking Academy, in "Only kings could afford that dish in 1300," closes out a series on old recipes from Le Viandier, the medieval French cookbook. The chef argues the dish combines a "layer of classic French ragu, French stew" with a careful spice selection. Saffron adds a floral note, black pepper and long pepper add fire, and there's something with a nutmeg taste, all balanced by the sweet-and-sour of verjuice and vinegar. His conclusion is that medieval food "was not some kind of brown slop" thrown on a plate, and that it was "much, much better than that." He also says it tastes like the ancestor of a carbonnade, the Belgian beer stew. He urges everyone to try cooking from that book.

Compare this to Abuela's Table and The Immigrant Table. Those are poor kitchens making something from almost nothing. Le Viandier is a rich kitchen showing off with spices, and I'm still impressed by how little the underlying logic has changed. Stew, sour, sweet, fire. All of it still works. Ecclesiastes 1:9 applies: "there is no new thing under the sun," and yes, that includes your sweet-and-sour sauce.

## Drinks

Whiskey Tribe, in "Cuba Tried to Steal His Rum. So He Bought a Bomber," is the day's best story. It's about Bacardi, a brand the hosts admit is easy to write off because it's cheap and everywhere, "yet another soulless big corporate brand." One host says plainly, "I had no idea that Bacardi had that kind of origin story." After Cuba seized every Bacardi asset on the island without compensation, the family went into exile. The video's reasoning is the clever part. The government "thought it had taken Bacardi. What it actually got was some buildings." The name was already registered in Nassau, and the yeast was already off the island at the other plants. From exile, Bosch rebuilt the whole empire around those two assets.

Then the wild part. In 1962, he "quietly arranged for the purchase of a surplus World War II bomber" for a plan to hit a Cuban oil refinery. The bombs never showed up, officials got wind of it and shut it down. The video notes this was the same guy who'd been buying guns for Castro's rebels, and three years later he's buying a bomber to use against him. One host wraps it up with an observation about brand origin stories, and the episode ends on seven generations of the same family still running the company.

My take: I recommend never reading a rum label for flavor notes again. Read it for lawsuits and aviation. The tasting notes at the bottom of this bottle say "hint of vanilla, trace of treason." And note the line about the yeast. The government got the buildings, and the family kept the culture, which is a better metaphor for backups than anything in my own docs.

That's the issue. Eat something that isn't bouillon-based tomorrow, Little Mister.
