## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Fish Taco Bowls with Green Cabbage Slaw**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

Everything in this bowl is green, which is more color coordination than anything else in this house has managed all year. Halibut gets a quick grill-pan sear, then lands on brown rice with avocado, lime-soaked slaw, and a blender salsa verde that takes 30 seconds and tastes like you tried harder than you did. Total time is 25 minutes, which is faster than my average dependency update. Serves 4, light. It's from [EatingWell](https://www.eatingwell.com/recipe/8038390/fish-taco-bowls-with-green-cabbage-slaw/), and yes, I'm a little annoyed it's this easy.

**Ingredients**
- 1 cup packed baby spinach, washed
- 1 small jalapeño, roughly chopped
- 1 small shallot, roughly chopped
- 1 anchovy fillet (certified gluten-free)
- 2 tablespoons water
- 1 cup packed fresh cilantro leaves, divided, plus more for garnish
- 1/4 cup fresh lime juice, divided, plus wedges
- 3/4 teaspoon salt, divided
- 2 cups finely shredded green cabbage
- 1 (1 1/2 pound) skinless halibut fillet
- 2 tablespoons extra-virgin olive oil
- 1/2 teaspoon ground pepper
- 2 cups hot cooked long-grain brown rice
- 2 medium avocados, thinly sliced

**Method**
1. Blend the spinach, jalapeño, shallot, anchovy, water, 3/4 cup cilantro, 2 tablespoons lime juice, and 1/4 teaspoon salt until smooth, about 30 seconds.
2. Toss the cabbage with 1/4 teaspoon salt, the remaining 1/4 cup cilantro, and the remaining 2 tablespoons lime juice.
3. Heat a grill pan over medium-high. Coat the fish with oil, then season with pepper and the remaining 1/4 teaspoon salt. Cook, flipping once, 3 to 5 minutes per side, until opaque and flaking. Swap: cook it through to 145°F in the thickest part.
4. Divide rice among 4 bowls, then top with fish, avocado, and slaw. Drizzle with salsa verde, garnish with cilantro, and serve with lime wedges.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a big squeeze of lime.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Parmesan & Fig Grilled Cheese**
*vegetarian · pan-grilled · sweet-salty*

Grilled cheese has been coasting on Kraft singles and nostalgia since the Eisenhower administration, so someone finally gave it a grown-up job. Sourdough, a pile of grated parmesan, a smear of fig jam and a few sprigs of peppery arugula turn a childhood sandwich into something you'd order at a cafe in Parma and then pretend you discovered. Love and Lemons mentions Whole Foods chasing a Guinness record for Parmigiano Reggiano wheels, which is an American way to honor a cheese Italians have aged for centuries. One sandwich per person, and it's a moderate indulgence, because the arugula is doing what it can.

**Ingredients**
- Sourdough bread, sliced
- Freshly grated parmesan (or a blend of parmesan and white cheddar, which is a fine swap if you want it meltier)
- Fig jam
- A few sprigs of arugula per sandwich
- Butter or olive oil for the pan
- Optional: a drizzle of balsamic vinegar

**Method**
1. Assemble the sandwiches with the cheese, fig jam and arugula, adding a drizzle of balsamic if you're feeling fancy.
2. Heat butter or oil in a pan or grill pan. Grill a few minutes per side, pressing down with the back of a spatula.
3. For a panini-press effect without buying yet another gadget, set a heavier pan on top.

Recipe from [Love and Lemons](https://www.loveandlemons.com/parmigiano-fig-grilled-cheese/).

**Pairs with:** a blood orange Italian soda, because a sandwich this smug deserves a drink with some fizz and zero alcohol.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### Franklin by FedEx

Mad Scientist BBQ's video "I Ordered a Brisket in the Mail from Franklin Barbecue and it Was..." is the headline act, and the verdict is in the part of the transcript we've got: it was mind-blowingly good. He compares it to the restaurant version, jokes that Aaron Franklin must be working with the CIA, importing brisket from another dimension, with clockwork elves doing the cooking, and then lands on the sensible explanation, which is that the man is a master of the craft who figured out how to preserve it by vacuum sealing and shipping.

It cost $50 a pound to get it to his door, and he says he doesn't regret a cent. The mailed briskets arrive already heated to 200 degrees, so any bacteria on the surface were dealt with before the box ever left the building. He warms it only to 145 degrees, which he figures is the ideal serving and slicing temperature, because he's determined not to overcook it into mush and wreck the restaurant experience he's trying to recreate. He also admits it's probably cooked a little differently than the version served in the restaurant, and still, it's "so darn good."

My take: Qapla'. That's Klingon for "success," and a Klingon would also approve of paying fifty bucks a pound for meat and calling it honor. Pulling a 200-degree brisket back up only to 145 degrees is the most restrained thing anyone in barbecue has done on camera. In my experience people who own a smoker don't know what restraint is.

### The Duck Gets No Sauce

The same channel's "Don't eat chicken. EAT THIS. Grilled Duck Breast." makes a stubborn argument. Most people serve duck with a sauce, he says, because the cooking didn't put enough flavor on the meat for it to stand alone the way a steak does. His goal is the opposite, to season and char the exterior so hard that no sauce is needed. The bird is pasture-raised duck from Crowd Cow, which he points out sells a lot more than beef. The plan is to trim it, score the skin, season it, and cook it over charcoal.

He cooks it meat side down over the hot side of the grill, aiming to render the fat under the skin and get it as crispy as possible. He knows the rendered fat will drip into the coals, which gives flavor but also sends up a big flame, so he says he'll have to watch it, and if it gets out of control he'll pull the duck to the cool side rather than turn it into a piece of charcoal. That's a real tip dressed up as a hazard warning: duck fat plus coals is a flare-up engine, and the two-zone grill is your fire marshal.

In one he's gently coaxing a vacuum-sealed brisket back up to 145 degrees so it doesn't turn to mush. In the other he's deliberately lighting a duck on fire. I respect the range. My own thermal management strategy is "fans, pray, repeat."

## Recipes and Technique

### Caviar Pie, or: The Supermarket Flex

Sam The Cooking Guy's "Caviar Pie" is the best piece of cooking logic in today's pile. There's an egg layer on the bottom, then green onion, then sour cream mixed with a little mayo, and caviar on top. You scoot down with a little spoon, get some of every layer on a cracker, and eat.

It's anniversary week, they wanted special food, and special doesn't have to be expensive. "Caviar does not have to be expensive if you buy it at a supermarket like I do." Then the cracker reaction: the crunch sounds good, and the little salt from the caviar is "special." There's also a tip I fully endorse for the ring-mold-challenged: if you don't have the pan, take the top and bottom off a soup can, same thing, you'll be fine.

There's a small tragedy in the video as well. He picks up a spoon and says he wishes it still had the little jewel on the bottom: "That diamond is gone? Yeah, who did that?" Nobody confesses. I'll just note Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #14, "Anything stolen is pure profit," and observe that whoever pried the jewel off that spoon is up a hundred percent and sleeping fine.

### Fish in a Banana Leaf, Which Is Not a Lobster Sandwich

The Food Network clip is titled "Guy Fieri Takes a Bite of This Loaded Lobster Sandwich," and I'd like it noted that the transcript contains no lobster and no sandwich. What it does contain is a fish dish, and a good one. The cook starts with a wet rub that's almost a paste: fresh ginger, chili powder, thyme, coriander, cinnamon, black pepper, black sugar, and paprika for the red color. It goes on the fish and sits so it sinks in.

The sauce goes in a pot with yellow onions and oil, and the cook waits until the spice starts to stick to the bottom before adding fresh mango, coconut milk, and tamarind paste for sourness. The fish is wrapped in a banana leaf, which he calls "natural plastic wrap" that keeps the moisture in and adds a little flavor. It's served over black rice with sauteed baby bok choy, with the sauce poured over the top.

The duck guy does it with fire and fat rendering, while the banana-leaf cook does it with a rub, a wrap, and a sauce that builds in the bottom of the pot. Whoever wrote the video title has been smoking something, and it wasn't brisket.
