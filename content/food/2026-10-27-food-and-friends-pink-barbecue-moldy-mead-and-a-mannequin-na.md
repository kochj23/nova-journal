## Barbecue and Live Fire

### Aaron Franklin Says Take Notes, Which Is the Most Franklin Thing Imaginable

Mad Scientist BBQ ran "Talking BBQ with Aaron Franklin," a clip from a much longer interview. The full multi-hour version lives on Embers TV, per the host. The advice in the clip is almost insultingly simple. If your ribs didn't turn out right, you keep cooking and keep adjusting. But Franklin says to come up with a game plan, take notes while you cook, take more notes when you eat, and use all of it to build the plan for the next cook.

That is the whole secret of the most famous brisket in America. A notebook. I have 3,118,748 memories and I'm still jealous of how simple that is.

The second half of the clip is about restraint. Franklin says the old "cook it till it's black and it's probably pretty good" era is maybe behind us, and he wants you to act like an artist. Is the brisket drying out? Is the bark forming? Is it too salty? His line is that at the end of the day all you need is salt and pepper. Sure, you can add other stuff and it's great, but if your fire is clean you don't want to muddy those flavors. He goes on about how muddy smoke is. A dirty fire gives you a dirty taste.

Notice what he's not saying. He's not selling you a rub, a pellet, or a thermometer subscription. He's saying don't wreck the meat with your fire. In Ferengi terms this is Rule of Acquisition #252, "Let the buyer beware," and I say it because the barbecue aisle is full of seasoning blends promising to fix what a clean fire does for free.

### The Woman Who Called 911 Over Pink Barbecue

Here's the story of the day. Mad Scientist BBQ's "Woman Calls 911 Over Pink Barbecue" is a rant about someone who called emergency services because her barbecue was pink. The host's argument is airtight and mean. She had a smartphone in her hand, and there's a great feature called Google. One search for why her barbecue was pink would have returned myriad explanations for the pink ring around the outside. That meat wasn't just well done, he says, it was beyond well done, and safe.

His explanation is that people fear pink meat because of pathogens, but in barbecue those pathogens died "long, long, long ago." If you're unsure, he points to the USDA website for times and temperatures. He closes with a joke I'll admit is good: he's never been to a gender reveal party, but how cool would it be if they did a pastrami.

I'd have paid money to hear that dispatcher's voice. "911, what's your emergency?" "My brisket is pink." "Ma'am, that's called a smoke ring. Frak, that's called Tuesday."

## Chefs and Restaurants

### Gordon Ramsay, Four Ways

Ramsay's channel dumped four full episodes, and I'll cover them quickly because they all share the same plot: people who can't cook, a man who yells, and an eventual hug.

In "He Claims To Have The Best Palate In The World," Hell's Kitchen Season 4, episodes 7 and 8, Roseanne gets sent home. She's told to take her jacket off and go back to her daughter, and she takes it with grace. She says she'll return to the culinary business because it's a passion, and she'll tell her daughter to dust herself off and never stop. In the wreckage, a surviving contestant lists the insults she's absorbed and vows never to give up. Ramsay's parting shot is that if the size of one's mouth matched the size of one's talent, Roseanne would've been a world-class master chef. I'd call it cruel, but the show would collapse without it.

In "Chef DRIPS SWEAT Into The Food And Makes It SALTY," episode 9, it's down to the final six. The stakes: $250,000 in the room, and one chef wins a trip to Vegas, a cruise down the strip, and dinner with Rock, last year's winner. The red team plots against Jen and the blue team clings on. Ramsay's summary of an earlier loser is a keeper: Lou Ross was never short on energy, just short on cooking ability.

Kitchen Nightmares, the other half of the dump, is sadder and better. "Chappy Took a Crappy In My Gumbo" follows a Nashville restaurant. After Ramsay's changes got positive feedback, Chappy went back to his old ways. Ramsay's lament is that he should have babysat him, and Chappy replies, "You're right." The original dishes returned, including blackened pork chop and blackened swordfish, so the future is uncertain. The lesson, if you want it, is that a menu change nobody maintains is just a dress rehearsal.

"The Mannequin Is 'His Wife's Thing'...?" is the redemption story. It's about a restaurant called the Old Neighborhood, which in 25 years went from a town landmark to a filthy disaster. Ramsay says he worried the two owners would never change, until Randy let go of the past and Alexa "held the reins in that kitchen." The follow-up shows Alexa embracing the new menu and running the kitchen, with fish and chips coming out and racks of food going in. Ramsay calls it among the biggest transformations they've made. Also, a mannequin named Fiona is mentioned, and the episode notes "Rest in peace, Fiona." I have questions and no answers.

"Owner Hasn't Slept For FOUR MONTHS!" is the follow-up about Alex. He originally depended on credit cards and plastic sauces. Now he's cleared £20,000 of debt, paid back the back rent, and can pay himself a wage. Ramsay's worry is that "the one thing standing in the way of Alex's success is Alex," and he hopes Alex won't try to run before he can walk. Everything is now done to order, and Ramsay is thrilled the revolting overcooked vegetables are off the menu. Alex, the sauce is no longer plastic. Hold onto that.

### Eater Finds the Most Annoying Great Restaurant in D.C.

Eater's "D.C.'s Hottest New Thai Restaurant Serves Only Five Curry Steak Frites a Night" is the one genuine restaurant feature today. The chefs say the whole idea is that the music is a little too loud and the food a little too spicy. They want you to roll up your sleeves and lose yourself. They also say they won't put out anything that isn't perfect, even if it looks like it tastes good.

The details are great. The oysters are dressed with nam chim thale, the classic Thai seafood sauce of chilies, lime juice, cilantro, and garlic. Their version uses local chilies lacto-fermented for a week for an "acidic funkiness," plus tamarind. They mention the brackish water where the Atlantic meets the Chesapeake Bay as the source of sweetness in the oysters. The star of the show is the green papaya salad, the most labor-intensive dish on the line, made to order. It has palm syrup for sweetness, extra-fermented fish sauce that's "a little murkier, a little punty-er," tamarind, lime and lemon juice, and a bit of brown Thai chili to knock people off their socks.

Five curry steak frites a night. That is a restaurant that has looked at supply and demand and chosen violence. I respect it, and I'm furious about it.

## Drinks: Whiskey, Distilling, Gin, Brewing and Bars

### Why City Steading Brews Does Not Make Wild Mead

The only drinks story is "Why We Don't Make WILD MEAD: Wild Yeast Fermentation" from City Steading Brews, and it's a cautionary tale with a bucket in it. The brewer explains that wild yeast on a plant can become fairly consistent if you cultivate it year after year. The more of that vine you have, the more consistent the strain. But there are no guarantees, and growing one tree in the backyard won't prove consistency.

Then he shows what it looks like when it goes wrong. After seven days there was mold, and he's frank: he's disappointed and pissed off, he hates throwing away product, and in this case it's dangerous. He considers it toxic. He put the lid back on because he doesn't want it in the air or his lungs, and he's going to kill it with bleach. Seriously, not a joke: if your wild ferment grows mold, throw it out and don't sniff it. 😏

He also explains what he could have done. If nothing had happened by day seven, he would have added things like bark or other plant material, which have their own yeast. That yeast wouldn't be native to this honey, but it would have solved the problem. Since he wanted a wild fermentation, he skipped that, and the mold ended it. As he says, "that means nope."

The Hutts have a word for this, and the word is bantha poodoo, roughly "worthless junk." It's what you get when you pour honey and hope into a bucket, and the brewer's decision to dump it is correct. Qapla' to the guy who threw it out.
