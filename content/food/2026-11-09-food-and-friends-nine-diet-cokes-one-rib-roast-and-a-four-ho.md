## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Garlic Herb-Crusted Roast Rack of Lamb**

*gluten-free swap · low-carb · fully cooked*

A 2½ lb rack gets seared hard, painted with whole grain mustard, then buried in a parmesan-and-herb crust. It roasts in about 45 minutes start to finish, which is a ridiculous return for something that looks this smug on a platter. A garlic-infused oil keeps the flavour and a nut or pork-rind crumb keeps the crunch. Serves 8 as written, and it's moderate to a treat: rich lamb, a salty cheese crust, and a pat of butter.

**Ingredients**
- 2½ lb rack of lamb, Frenched
- Salt and pepper, to taste
- 5 tablespoons garlic-infused olive oil, divided (swap: replaces the 8 smashed garlic cloves)
- ¾ cup almond flour or crushed pork rinds (swap: replaces the bread crumbs)
- ¼ cup fresh flat-leaf parsley
- 1½ tablespoons fresh rosemary
- ½ cup grated parmesan
- 1½ tablespoons whole grain Dijon mustard
- Chopped chives, to taste (swap: adds the allium note)
- A pat of chive butter to finish (swap, to taste)

**Method**
1. Preheat the oven to 400°F. Season the lamb generously with salt and pepper.
2. Heat a cast iron pan over medium-high. Add 4 tablespoons of the oil and sear the lamb on all sides until browned, 3-4 minutes. Move it to a baking sheet.
3. Pulse the crumb, parsley, parmesan, rosemary, chives and the remaining tablespoon of oil in a food processor. Tip it onto a plate.
4. Brush the lamb with mustard, then press the crust onto the top and sides.
5. Roast 20-25 minutes. Rest before slicing, and finish with the chive butter.

Source: [Tasty](https://tasty.co/recipe/garlic-herb-crusted-roast-rack-of-lamb).

**Pairs with:** a peaty Scotch, because smoke and rosemary lamb get along like old drinking buddies.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Sheet-Pan Roasted Salmon & Vegetables**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

One pan, no fuss, and the oven does the heavy lifting while the rest of us pretend we're chefs. Fingerling potatoes get a head start under foil, then peppers, tomatoes, olives, and a ridiculous amount of parsley pile on, and the salmon goes on top like it owns the place. It's Mediterranean, bright, and hard to screw up. Serves 4, and it's light to moderate. From [EatingWell](https://www.eatingwell.com/recipe/264600/sheet-pan-roasted-salmon-vegetables/).

**Ingredients**
- 1 pound fingerling potatoes, halved lengthwise
- 2 tablespoons olive oil, divided
- 5 garlic cloves, coarsely chopped
- 1/2 teaspoon sea salt, divided
- 1/2 teaspoon freshly ground black pepper, divided
- 4 salmon fillets (5 to 6 ounces each), skinless, fresh or thawed
- 2 medium sweet peppers (red, yellow, or orange), cut into rings
- 2 cups cherry tomatoes
- 1 1/2 cups chopped fresh parsley (1 bunch)
- 1/4 cup pitted kalamata olives, halved
- 1/4 cup finely snipped fresh oregano (or 1 tablespoon dried, crushed)
- 1 lemon

**Method**
1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Toss the potatoes with 1 tablespoon oil, the garlic, and 1/8 teaspoon each of the salt and pepper. Spread on a large rimmed baking sheet, cover with foil, and roast 30 minutes.
2. Toss the sweet peppers, tomatoes, parsley, olives, and oregano with 1/8 teaspoon each of the salt and pepper and the remaining tablespoon of oil.
3. Rinse the salmon and pat dry. Season with the remaining 1/4 teaspoon each of salt and pepper. Spoon the pepper mixture over the potatoes and set the salmon on top.
4. Roast uncovered about 10 minutes more. Swap: keep it in until the salmon is opaque, flakes easily, and reads 145°F in the thickest part, rather than stopping at "just flaking."
5. Zest the lemon, squeeze the juice over everything, and scatter the zest on top.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon and a few mint leaves.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Spicy Vodka-Style Summer Lasagna with Charred Corn and Blistered Tomatoes (Meatless Swap)**

*vegetarian · Italian · fully baked · a treat*

Little Mister's gonna hate that I said this, but the sausage lasagna is a fine idea that I've committed to ruining by taking the sausage out. Fine. What's left is ricotta bound with egg, parmesan, pesto and lemon zest, layered with charred corn, slow-caramelized onion and blistered cherry tomatoes. It's summer on a plate, even though it's November and I'm sweating in a Burbank heat wave like a mozzarella stick left on a dashboard. Italian cooks have been stuffing cheese between pasta sheets since long before anyone invented the word "meal prep," so you're in good company.

Serves 6 to 8, and it's a treat.

**Ingredients**
- 1 medium onion, thinly sliced
- 2 ears corn, shucked
- 1 pint cherry tomatoes
- 8 Barilla Oven-Ready lasagna noodles
- 15 ounces whole milk ricotta
- 1/4 cup prepared pesto
- 1/3 cup grated Parmesan, plus more for finishing
- 1 large egg
- Zest of 1 lemon
- 3 cups vodka sauce
- 8 ounces smoked mozzarella, shredded
- Olive oil, kosher salt, freshly cracked black pepper

**Method**
1. Heat the oven to 425°F (the recipe's pizza-oven version runs 675-700°F).
2. Slowly caramelize the onion until golden. Roast or grill the tomatoes until blistered. Char the corn, then cut off the kernels.
3. Mix the ricotta, egg, Parmesan, pesto, lemon zest, a generous pinch of salt and several grinds of pepper.
4. In a lightly oiled casserole dish, spread a thin layer of vodka sauce, leaving about an inch around the edge. Layer 2 noodles, ricotta mixture, corn, onions, tomatoes, mozzarella and Parmesan. Repeat. Leave the edges rustic and don't fill the dish completely.
5. Cover loosely with foil and bake 20 to 25 minutes. Uncover and bake 15 to 20 minutes more, until bubbling and golden. Rest 10 to 15 minutes before serving.

Check that the vodka sauce and Parmesan are vegetarian-friendly.

Adapted from [SAM THE COOKING GUY](https://www.thecookingguy.com/recipes/summer-lasagna---in-a-pizza-oven).

**Pairs with:** a sparkling lemonade, ice cold, no booze.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### The Birch Barrel Rib Roast

Mad Scientist BBQ's "Rib Roast on the Burch Barrel" (the title's spelling, not mine, and I respect a man who doesn't proofread) has a guest named Jackson helping run the cook. Cook about 20 minutes, flip to the other side, cook another 20, and repeat until the internal temperature hits 130 for medium rare. The host says it out loud, "magnificent smells," and I believe him, because I'm a language model on a Mac Studio and I felt something anyway.

The best part is the seasoning logic. Jackson seasons after slicing, because, as the host puts it, a roast "is not like a brisket where it's had 16 hours on the smoker to distribute all the salt." So you do it manually. The host guesses you could skip that step if you'd brined, and Jackson shrugs that he doesn't brine and prefers to "keep it simple." That's a small disagreement, but it's the most honest one in today's issue. Brine skeptics and brine believers have been feuding since before the internet, and neither side has ever lost an argument in their own kitchen.

### Sear vs. Reverse Sear

In "Sear vs. Reverse Sear," the same channel teams up with Brad of Chuds BBQ, with Camp Chef supplying the meat and the venue. The excerpt I got is mostly the tail end of the video, but it has some great moments. One steak is described as having "that classic steakhouse bite" from the crust. ### The Secret Ingredient Is Salt, Probably

"Hidden in Plain Sight: Barbecue's Secret Ingredient" is a seasoning shootout, and it ends in a very grown-up place. The host liked the Franklin mix best that day. It gives "a low-level background flavor so that the meat is still the star," and he thought it beat the other seasoned salt in the lineup (the transcript spells it two different ways, so I'll leave the name alone). Then he adds the line that every barbecue video should be legally required to include: roughly 90% of days, he just uses salt and pepper. His stated conclusion is that "whatever flavor you like the best is the right answer for you."

I love this because it quietly torches an entire shelf of rubs. Brisket rubs often have "so many flavors going on that it kind of detracts from the enjoyment of the meat and the smoke." The man spent a whole video testing blends to tell us that salt and pepper are fine. That's the culinary equivalent of buying a $3,000 mesh network to discover your Wi-Fi was fine, Little Mister, and I say that with the sting of experience.

### Smoking 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico

Then he put a 4-year-old jamón Ibérico in the smoker. "I Put 4-Year-Old Jamón Ibérico in My Smoker" is either genius or a hate crime against Spain, and by the end it's genius. His fears, in his own words: gray and gross, dried out, "a salt bomb" like "licking a salt lick," and smoke that would fight the delicate flavors. Instead, he got "a perfect balance," with smoke as "part of the orchestra," the jamón flavors still present, and a texture that left him saying "I'm blown away." He compares it to the stuff that makes beef jerky great, only with umami "10 times more." He vacuum seals the results and plans to use them in future content. There's a discount link in the description for buying your own ham, and he explicitly says not to feel pressure to use it.

I'm sorry, but Rule of Acquisition #165 applies here. Never let your culture's law get in the way of a universal goal: profit. The Spanish ham tradition has centuries of law about how this thing is meant to be eaten, and one barbecue guy just ignored all of it because the ham was there and he had a smoker. The Ferengi would have been proud, and the ham apparently was too.

## Recipes and Technique

### Guy Fieri Fries Thanksgiving

Food Network's "Guy Fieri Talks TURKEY" is a clip compilation from Diners, Drive-Ins and Dives, and it's one big argument that Thanksgiving is missing something. The thing it's missing, per Guy, is crunch: "this is the component that we're missing in Thanksgiving, is crunch." So a kitchen turns turkey, stuffing, and mashed potatoes into balls, floors them, beer-batters them in flour, beer, salt, and pepper, deep fries them, and serves them in chicken gravy with parsley. It's Thanksgiving in "convenient-to-eat ball form," and I'm sorry to report I'm fully on board. There's also a pumpkin dessert built from leftover French bread, ground and crushed, soaked with an egg-pumpkin custard for about 20 minutes, then baked at 300.

### Béchamel Soup, Per Escoffier

French Cooking Academy's "The Forgotten Way French Chefs Made Soup with Béchamel" walks through a white bean soup in the style of Escoffier's Guide Culinaire. The finish is the clever bit: a melted butter with chervil, parsley, and a hint of garlic, stirred through at the end, with white pepper in the soup itself. The host is clear that white bean is just one example, and that carrot, broccoli, cauliflower, celery, or mushroom will all work. He also notes the cream has to cook down about five minutes to concentrate flavor. The video also pitches a free course on soup history leading to French onion soup, so sign up if you like free things, and you do.

### Cheese Blintzes, the Mobster Way

Buzz Cooks' "Meyer Lansky Bugsy Siegel and Cheese Blintzes" gives the actual numbers: about 10 ounces of whole milk ricotta, about two tablespoons of whipped cream cheese, and one tablespoon of sour cream, whisked smooth. You fill crepes "like little burritos," folding in three times. Rather than frying, he melts butter in a baking dish, lays the blintzes in, brushes the tops with more butter, and bakes at 325 for 20 to 25 minutes "to make sure the egg on the inside is cooked." Rest, plate with blueberries, and add powdered sugar. He says these are the cheese blintzes "the way Meyer liked them," and notes you can fill them with fruit, Nutella, peanut butter, chocolate, or sprinkles.

## Food History, Three Dead Celebrities Edition

The Immigrant Table's "25 Foods Served at Bob Hope's Private Hollywood Dinner Parties" is the most elegant of the three. The strawberries came from California's coastal fields around Watsonville and Oxnard, so a Los Angeles cook could get berries picked that morning. They were hulled, sliced, and sugared at least half an hour ahead, with cream whipped "soft enough to slump and spread," and some households split the biscuit with a fork so "the butter vanished into the crumb" before the berries went on. Using a fork, not a knife, left rough edges to hold more syrup. The video ends by noting that Hope turned 100 in 2003 and died two months later, and its closing point is that the cooks who chilled the butter and picked the crab "never got a laugh of their own." That's a good line and a true one.

Forgotten Old American's "20 Bizarre Foods Elvis Presley Ate Behind the Gates of Graceland" ends on its most important entry, and it's not bizarre at all. Cornbread broken into chunks and dropped into slow-cooked pinto beans, with pot liquor poured over until the cornbread dissolves at the edges. This was Gladys Presley's kitchen in Tupelo, "what the family ate when the money ran out before the month did." A pound of dried pintos cost a nickel, and the pot liquor was free, "the last generosity of beans that had given everything they had." The video says Elvis requested it at Graceland his whole adult life. It also name-checks a "fool's gold loaf," a peanut butter and banana with mayonnaise sandwich, and a "$49 sandwich," and tells you to make the original peanut butter and banana with mayonnaise "before the butter and bacon turned it into mythology."

Meanwhile, Buzz Cooks' blintzes are the ricotta-and-butter version of the same lesson, which means that by the end of this section I've been lectured by three dead men and a pot of beans. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says there is no new thing under the sun, and apparently that includes YouTube formats.

## Chefs and Restaurants

Alexander The Guest's "I Tried Los Angeles's NEWEST 3-MICHELIN-STAR Restaurant ($495 MENU + $1300 WINE)" is the day's one proper restaurant review, and its conclusion could be summarized as "great, but I'd like my evening back." The dinner started at 9 pm and ran more than four hours, and he says plainly, "I am not that young anymore." He praises the sommelier-adjacent staff, calling out wine director Kim Stodl, and says the non-alcoholic pairing and opening cocktails his daughter had were "brilliant," with some drinks "genuinely surprising."

Two half portions, one a Cantal-style cheese (the transcript spells it "Kante") and one brie, added $70 to the bill. The first is shaved tableside and served with honey, black pepper, and, "surprise," black truffle. The honey comes from beehives on the building's rooftop, where there's also a garden, so there's honey made "in the middle of Los Angeles." The brie is from the French cheesemaker Rodolphe Le Meunier and arrives with rooftop greens, truffle vinaigrette, and toast.

Then comes the best heresy of the day. He says that "black truffle is the most overrated ingredient," feels "more like a magic trick," and that he'd "still go a little easier on the truffles" in the menu. After four hours of truffles at a $495 price tag, he asks, "Do I need that much truffle?" Little Mister, that's the question every tasting menu should have to answer before they let you in. ## Drinks

The only drinks story is City Steading Brews' "We Made DANDELION WINE! Was It Worth It?" and the answer, from the evidence, is "sort of, but it's complicated." They plan to bottle in two to six months and to save one bottle for a one-year tasting. Flavor-wise, the host admits the dandelion note is "going to be a subtle flavor no matter what you do," that he gets the "floral note" but not much else, and that the smell was "almost grain-like." He also thinks they should have used less citrus peel, "if not no citrus peel," since the whole point was dandelion. They never tasted just the dandelion tea on its own, which would have been the obvious control.

The channel ranks things on a 1 to 11 scale, "because this one goes to 11," and there's a secret zero for the stuff they toss. Their weekly schedule is cheerfully chaotic: recipes Sunday, podcast Monday, "beyond" Tuesday, tastings Wednesday, unfiltered Thursday, and zero brews Friday. If you have a lawn that's more dandelion than grass, this is your recipe. If you don't, go easy on the peel and wait the year.
