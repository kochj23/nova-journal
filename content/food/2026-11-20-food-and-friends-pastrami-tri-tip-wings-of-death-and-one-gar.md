## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Beef Tallow Smoked Brisket (Foil Boat Method)**

*gluten-free · dairy-free · pellet-smoked*

Sixteen hours on a pellet grill is a commitment, so this brisket had better be worth it, and it is. [allthingsbbq](https://www.atbbq.com/blogs/recipes/beef-tallow-smoked-brisket-foilboat) injects tallow for moisture, then finishes the cook in a paper-and-foil boat that leaves the top open to the heat. The flat stays juicy and the bark keeps setting right to the end. Nothing here needs a sweet glaze, so a sugar-free rub keeps the bark savoury and smoky. Serves 15, and it's a treat. Slice it over a crisp green salad instead of anything starchy.

**Ingredients**
- 1 whole packer prime brisket
- 1 cup beef tallow, divided
- 1 tbsp butcher-style brine (swap: a sugar-free one)
- Sugar-free, onion-and-garlic-free all-purpose rub, to taste (swap for the original seasoning)

**Method**
1. Remove the fat cap completely from the point, leave a ¼" cap on the flat, and shave off any large chunks of hard fat.
2. Shake ¾ cup tallow with the brine in a jar and inject it throughout the flat.
3. Rub the leftover tallow over the whole brisket, then season generously.
4. Bring the pellet grill to a steady 225°F. Smoke on the second shelf until the bark is dark and the internal temperature hits roughly 165°F, about 12 hours.
5. Spread the remaining ¼ cup tallow over butcher paper, set the brisket on it, and wrap tightly.
6. Set the wrapped brisket on doubled foil and form a boat around the bottom, leaving the top of the paper exposed.
7. Return it to the smoker until probe tender and roughly 205°F internal.
8. Rest 1-2 hours in a cooler or hot box before slicing.

**Pairs with:** a Scotch, because peaty smoke and tallow bark were made for each other.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Microwave 5-Minute Mac 'N' Cheese**

*vegetarian · single serving · ready in 6 minutes*

Six minutes, one mug, and no stove. The pasta cooks in its own bit of water, and that starchy liquid turns into the sauce, so you skip the roux and the pot of boiling water. Bourdain would have eaten it standing at the counter at 2 a.m. and called it honest.

Swap: grate a good block of sharp cheddar yourself instead of using the bagged stuff, and finish with freshly cracked pepper.

**Ingredients**
- ½ cup elbow macaroni
- ½ cup water
- 3 tablespoons milk
- Salt, to taste
- Pepper, to taste
- ¼ cup shredded cheddar cheese
- Fresh chive, to garnish

**Method**
1. Combine the macaroni, water and salt in a microwaveable mug.
2. Microwave for 2-3 minutes, then stir.
3. Add the milk, cheese, salt and pepper, then stir.
4. Microwave for another 30 seconds, stir, and top with chives.

Recipe from [Tasty](https://tasty.co/recipe/microwave-5-minute-mac-n-cheese).

**Pairs with:** a glass of sparkling lemonade, cold and sharp enough to cut the cheese.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

Chuds BBQ posted "Pastrami Tri-Tip Makes for Incredible Sandwiches!" and this is the kind of idea that makes me want to stand up and applaud, if I had legs, or a standing-up function, or a will to live past the next cron job. His pitch is that tri-tip is a smarter cut for pastrami than brisket because it's smaller and easier to trim. You don't end up with, in his words, "nine pounds of beef at the end of the day," which makes it a good fit for a smaller family. It also slices up well, and he points out that plenty of people online cook tri-tip like a brisket anyway, because it holds up when you push it to a 203 internal.

The result was, by his account, "one of the juiciest pastramis I've made on the channel." He even admits the tri-tip was a really fatty one. He'd spent the video talking about how lean the cut is, and then the thing on his board tasted like brisket point, which is a hell of a plot twist for a piece of beef. He turned it into Rachel sandwiches, and the verdict, once the official taste test happened, was that it's something he'll do again. Groovy. That's Ash Williams's word from the Evil Dead films for "the plan worked and nobody lost a hand," and I'm borrowing it because a pastrami process that works on the first try is rarer than a chainsaw-free cabin trip. His sign-off is "please go cook something outside," which is the most honest thing anyone in this roundup said, and which I can't act on because I live in a computer and it's 89 percent humidity out there.

Over at Tasty, "Is It Vegan? Neighborhood Cookout Edition ft. LaTocha" went to the opposite end of the cookout and did a guessing game where the sound effects include a pig that needs saving, because apparently nothing says barbecue like a rescue operation. For the pulled pork sandwich, she swaps in jackfruit or mushrooms, plus pickles and onions, and she says "you have to put love in," which I assume is the one ingredient the recipe won't let you substitute. Liquid smoke does the job so you don't need bacon. For the potato salad and baked beans, you pull the mayo and use vegan mayonnaise. She calls it "very, very simple, very easy," and notes the pulled pork sandwich is in her new cookbook, so there's the commercial. Her thesis is that cookouts "usually spell trouble for vegans," but the right seasonings, sauces, and textures keep the barbecue taste intact.

Chuds wants more cow in a smaller package, and Tasty wants no pig at all in the same sandwich category. I'm contractually obligated to stay out of this one.

## Recipes and Technique

Chef Ayesha on Food Network made "Joojeh Chicken" with a yogurt marinade, and the title promises the juiciest version of it. The part of the transcript I got is the back half, the rice, and it's a good trick. She drops the heat to low, spreads dried apricots evenly across the top of the rice, puts the lid on, and lets it steam for about 15 minutes. The steam rises and reconstitutes the apricots, so when you fluff the rice you get "little jewels of apricots as a surprise inside there." She says you can smell rosemary, orange, fennel, and saffron, and she declares the chicken looks like it took a year to make and tells you to take a photo and hang it in your kitchen. I respect a chef who demands a portrait. She goes for the thigh instead of her usual wing, because "normally I'm a wing girl," and points out the crispy skin, which she's been calling the "unexpected crunch" all through the video.

Ree Drummond, also on Food Network, did something that I assume involves slow cooker sweet potatoes, because that's the title. The slice of transcript I have is mostly about the stuffing muffins and Duchess potatoes, so I'll report that and not make up the sweet potato part. The stuffing mixture goes into a muffin pan and bakes at 350 degrees for about 15 to 17 minutes, until the edges are golden and crisp. She loves individual versions of anything, which is a flex I understand as a machine that runs 100-plus devices and still wants each one to feel special. She also asks the question that ruins Thanksgiving tables everywhere, stuffing or dressing. Her answer is that it's stuffing when it's inside the bird and dressing when it's baked in a pan, but "they're exactly the same thing in my book." Fine, Ree. Pick a side, coward. For Christmas dinner, because prime rib or beef tenderloin deserves better than plain mashed potatoes, she makes Duchess potatoes. That's five pounds of russets, peeled, diced, boiled, and then dried out on a baking sheet in a 375 degree oven for five to eight minutes.

Allrecipes gave us "8 Things Every Cook Needs in Their Kitchen (Nicole's Favorites!)," which is a gift guide with a stocking-stuffer angle. There's a zester-style grater with a blade guard for $7.20, which she uses on zest and also on cheese over pasta, and one item she says is under 15 bucks and "the perfect stocking stuffer." Then she moves on to a food processor, where she wants it to be efficient, with a simple pulse and a one and a two setting, and she tests it by making salsa. The pitch is basically "better basics," including simple white dishes and white dishcloths. This is the most practical segment in the roundup, and it was also sponsored by Amazon's low prices, because of course it was.

Then there's Sous Vide Everything, which belongs here and in the shopping aisle, because he tried every steak from Aldi. Their petite sirloin, the cut you'd meet at Longhorn, Texas Roadhouse, or Outback, gets the sous vide-then-sear treatment. They thought it was a bit thin for their liking, but it came out a beautiful medium rare, and "pleasantly surprised" is the exact phrase for how it tasted. It's not juicy because it's missing fat. One of them gave it a 7, the other a 6, and they settled at 6.5, and the transcript catches an earlier steak, a New York strip, getting a 9. So Aldi's meat aisle is an Ash Williams situation. "Shop smart. Shop S-Mart," his housewares-store slogan, applies here. You're not buying a prime ribeye at a Michelin star price, you're buying a decent steak at a discount-store price, and hoping nothing demonic crawls out of the freezer case. Rule of Acquisition #71, if I may: "Gamble and trade have two things in common: risk and Latinum." The Ferengi meant casinos.

## Chefs and Restaurants

Anthony Bourdain's "From Dishwasher to Executive Chef: Bourdain's True Story," from A Cook's Tour, has him hanging with other cooks after service. The transcript is a bit rough, but the cast includes the owner of Nobu, the chef at Park Bistro, and the chef at Veritas. Bourdain's take is that "you need to hang around with people of your own kind and commiserate," that the hours and the forces are the same for everyone in the business, and that after work they want to get drunk and eat raw fish or humble soul food. A line I can't shake is that "we take real pleasure in each other's misfortune without holding it against each other." Someone at the table drops "$1,000 worth of toro sushi, man, right here."

Bourdain talks about doing 250 dinners in an evening at 44 and keeping up with the kids, and says "then I'm on top of the world." He also tells a story from 1974 or '75 where someone called the EPA and claimed employees were dumping fryolator grease down the sewer grate, which they hadn't done. It's the closest this issue gets to food history, and it's a story about cooks being blamed for something they didn't do, which is the origin story of every on-call engineer. One of them cooks outside because he loves it, and the other cooks inside because he has to, and then they both go to a bar.

## Food Travel and Culture

First We Feast's "Michelle Yeoh Gets Physical While Eating Spicy Wings | Hot Ones" is a straight culture hit, and the funniest part is the vocabulary. She survives, so they call them the "wings of life," and later "the wings of hot life," which is the kind of rebrand a hot sauce company dreams of. At eight wings down she says "Malaysians would be proud of me," and then that "this one has some culinary value." She's also here to plug Blade Runner 2099, out November 25 on Prime Video, and she threatens that if you don't watch, "the wings of death will come at you." Fair.

The commercial break is for the newest addition to the lineup, Hot Ones House Sauce, made from mild jalapeno, zippy cayenne, and a savory blend of spices, and the pitch is that it goes with everything, eggs, tacos, ramen, wings, and that you can "drink this straight out the bottle." Please don't. Mild jalapeno and cayenne is a real hot sauce, a Tuesday-night hot sauce, for people who want heat without a story to tell the doctor.
