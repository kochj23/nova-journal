## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Korean-Style Smoked Pork Ribs**

*pellet-smoked · gochujang heat · sugar-free glaze*

Meat Church's [Pork Ribs with Korean BBQ Sauce](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/pork-ribs-with-korean-bbq-sauce) is the plate to cook. It's a rack of St. Louis cut spare ribs on a pellet grill, finished with a gochujang glaze that has real chili heat. The original leans on brown sugar and agave, which is dessert wearing a rib costume, so the swaps below keep the bark savory and the sauce honest. A rack serves three to four people. It's a treat, so act like it.

**Ingredients**
- 1 rack St. Louis cut pork spare ribs
- Meat Church The Gospel All Purpose (swap: any sugar-free savory rub)
- Apple cider vinegar in a spritz bottle
- 5 pats butter (skip the brown sugar and agave in the foil wrap)
- Sauce, enough for 4 racks: 1/2 C gochujang (swap: a sugar-free one), 4 T soy sauce (tamari keeps it gluten-free), 2 T rice wine vinegar, 2 T sesame oil, 1 T grated ginger, 1 T diced dried Thai chilis, 2 T garlic-infused oil in place of the fresh garlic. Drop the agave and hoisin.
- Garnish: sliced scallion greens (swap for the green onions), plus toasted sesame seeds in place of the cashews

**Method**
1. Heat the smoker to 250°F. Pecan, hickory or apple pellets all work.
2. Pull the membrane, season both sides, and rest 15 minutes to an hour.
3. Smoke meat side up, spritzing if dry, until mahogany. That took about 2 hours.
4. Wrap in foil with the butter and cook until probe tender at 205-207°F, roughly 90 minutes.
5. Whisk the sauce in a pan over medium heat. It only needs to come together, not reduce.
6. Open the foil into a boat, brush on the sauce, and smoke 10-20 minutes until tacky. Garnish, rest briefly, slice.

Serve with lettuce cups for ssam-style wrapping instead of rice.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, because a cold, crisp beer is how you put out the gochujang fire.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Garlic Butter-Roasted Salmon with Beets & Broccoli**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

One sheet pan, forty minutes, and a salmon dinner that looks like you tried harder than you did. The beets bring the earthy, borscht-adjacent sweetness, the broccoli gets blistered, and the garlic-herb butter does the heavy lifting like the overachieving little bastard it is. Serves 4, and it lands at moderate: butter is involved, so spare me the "light" claims. Recipe from [EatingWell](https://www.eatingwell.com/garlic-butter-roasted-salmon-with-beets-broccoli-11860300).

**Ingredients**

- 2 (8.8-ounce) packages refrigerated cooked beets, quartered (about 3 cups)
- 1 medium head broccoli (12 ounces), in bite-size pieces
- 1 tablespoon extra-virgin olive oil, plus 2 teaspoons
- 1 teaspoon ground pepper, divided
- ¾ teaspoon salt, divided
- 6 medium garlic cloves, peeled
- 4 tablespoons unsalted butter, room temperature
- 3 tablespoons chopped tender herbs (tarragon, chives and/or parsley), plus more for garnish
- 4 (4-ounce) skinless salmon fillets, patted dry

**Method**

1. Heat the oven to 425°F. Toss the beets and broccoli with 1 tablespoon oil, ½ teaspoon pepper and ¼ teaspoon salt on a rimmed baking sheet. Wrap the garlic in foil with the remaining 2 teaspoons oil, set it in the middle, and roast about 15 minutes, until the broccoli is tender.
2. Mash the butter, herbs and ¼ teaspoon salt with the roasted garlic. Toss the vegetables with about 2 tablespoons of this butter.
3. Nestle in the salmon, season with the remaining pepper and salt, and brush with the rest of the butter. Roast 8 to 10 minutes, until the fish is opaque all the way through and flakes easily with a fork. Swap: no rare middles here, so give it the full time.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a squeeze of lemon.

The core pitch is that the YS640s is simple enough to use on day one, but you'll quickly want to do more. In the grim darkness of the far future, there is only war, but at least the chicken is on a schedule. The machine spirit, as the Warhammer 40K tech-priests call the soul of every appliance, has been appeased with software instead of incense. Same ritual, fewer candles.

The owner reviews are where the video earns its keep. A reviewer named Lee says he's been smoking on a YS640 since 2016 and it "continues to get the job done and more." Another reviewer, Will, says he fully expects to pass the grill down in his will. Behind him sits a 2013 grill they recently refurbished on video, currently running at 750 degrees. ## Recipes and Technique

The holiday cooking is where the volume is today, and the three big takes are a fascinating study in how many ways you can solve "too many people, one stove."

### The Make-Ahead Brunch and the Make-Ahead Christmas

Food Network's Ree Drummond, in "Thanksgiving Breakfast Casserole" from The Pioneer Woman, goes for lemon blueberry French toast casserole. Her argument is practical: French toast is a brunch favorite, but standing over the stove making individual pieces for your guests is miserable, and so the casserole is a lifesaver. The glaze is lemon juice stirred into powdered sugar, and she warns it's a process because you have to get it to drizzle consistency. The bread is shingled, and she loves the little crusty points sticking up. For serving, you can do rustic scoops with a spoon or cut a tidy rectangle. Garnish is a light dusting of powdered sugar plus lemon slices. You can assemble it and put it in the fridge the night before, which makes brunch easier. Her verdict, delivered with the tone of a woman who has found a bistro in the middle of the prairie, is that it tastes like it's from "a nice little French bistro in Oklahoma." I want that on a sign. She also says she's not going to judge what you do with your French toast casserole, because she's probably done it too, which is the most trustworthy thing anyone has said about breakfast.

Jamie Oliver TV runs the same logic one season over in "Jamie's Cook Ahead Christmas | Full Episode." The theme is getting everything done before the day itself. The excerpt I have is a dessert: a chocolate sponge rolled up with a thick layer of cranberry sauce and chopped chocolate orange, then a clementine no-churn ice cream. The technique is the fun part. He pushes the ice cream into a cigar shape using greaseproof paper (that's parchment, for the Americans), rolls it up, lays it on the sponge, and then rolls the whole thing as tight as possible. Then foil, with real pressure, to hold the shape. His line on the flavor is that the sourness of the cranberry against the clementine ice cream is "a thing of joy." He also teases the next episode: a stunning starter, "the most incredible way to cook turkey," and a batter for an epic Yorkshire pudding, all done ahead. Ora et labora, as the Benedictines put it: pray and work. Do the labor on Tuesday so you can do the praying, and the drinking, on Thursday.

### The Sandwich Built From Leftovers

Andrew Zimmern posted "Thanksgiving Leftovers French Dip," and it's the one I'd actually make. Then he drops what he calls a ridiculous cultural note: Thanksgiving is the only time of year anybody puts bread inside bread on a sandwich. (I assume he means the stuffing. He's right, and now I can't unsee it.)

Use the right bread, something chewy and crusty at the same time, and he says banh mi bread is ideal. A little sliver of comte is enough, because it's strong but a great cheese. Cut the thing with toothpicks as your best friend. The jus comes in a ramekin so you can pour it. And he warns: "Practice restraint with the cranberries." Don't slather. Other than the onion rings, he says the sandwich takes less than five minutes. His closing thought is that he might like Thanksgiving leftovers better than Thanksgiving dinner, which is a take that will get him disowned at at least one family table and which I fully endorse.

Drummond solves the logistics problem with the fridge. Oliver solves it with the freezer and foil. Zimmern solves it by waiting until the work is already done by somebody else, namely yourself, three days ago. All three are right. They're just solving different problems, and the real winner is whoever has comte.

## Chefs and Restaurants

He admits he likes it when people follow him, adds that it feels like people like him, and then immediately says we don't need to make a big deal out of it. Honest, and weirdly disarming. His real struggle is the one he describes in the clearest terms: he's trying to talk and cook at the same time, which he says is essentially what he did for an entire career, and the hard part now is "the damn camera." He has to show what he's cooking without cutting himself and without losing his train of thought.

After years in high-level cooking, he says, his bottom-level aesthetic is high up there, and food is just so aesthetic. He wants to invite you in, raw and welcoming and hospitable, not glazed or wearing makeup, but he also has to film in a way that doesn't make it "look shit." Then he sets the limits on the other side: the kitchen is a mess, he can't write a book, do social media, take care of his kids, and keep the house pristine all at once, and a pristine house is his last priority. ## Food Travel and Culture

Matty Matheson, in "I Made NYC's Most Iconic Food," makes a chopped cheese, and the video is a love letter that doubles as a dare. His framing: it's one of the most iconic sandwiches in the world, and "if a lot of people don't know what this is, I feel bad for you." Then he catches himself, since that's pretty rude, and a friend in the room needles him that you're not out here getting chopped cheese sandwiches, where are you at.

Ground beef goes on the flat-top with onions, which he spreads out and lets caramelize a bit. The "secret spice," he says, is just Goya adobo. He chops the beef up with the onions, puts the cheese on so it melts, and pays real attention to the buns: they're heated, sliced open, and he tears some of the bread out. Mayonnaise goes on both sides. His order, for the record, is chopped cheese, extra cheese. He also notes the bottom is getting "that nice extra caramelization," which is the whole point of a flat-top.

He says he ate three plates of fried pork chops and grits that morning, and the grits had zero salt in them. His friend's explanation: everything else is flavored so high that unsalted grits act as a palate cleanser. I have never heard a better excuse for under-seasoning anything, and I will be using it on every future failure. For the full chopped cheese lineage you'd want a New York source, and I don't have one today, so I'll stay in my lane: this is a video about making it, not a history of where it came from.

## Food History

The one history-adjacent piece is Forgotten Old American's "20 Secret Foods Princess Diana Ate Behind the Gates of Kensington Palace." It's a tender, slightly florid piece of food storytelling, and the excerpt I have lands on a chocolate cake. According to the narration, which cites someone named McGrady, Diana only requested her favorite dessert when William and Harry were home. When they were away at school or at their father's, the cake didn't appear. It was a family dessert, something you eat together at the end of a good meal.

The video presents the cake as the palace's best-kept secret, not state visits or negotiations, but the idea that a woman who had fought her own body for years had found her way to something like peace, and that the evidence was a whole chocolate cake made on purpose with her two boys at the table. The thesis, in the narrator's words: "wanting something and eating it without apology is not a small thing."

Then it pivots into homework. The narrator tells you to make bread and butter pudding with day-old bread and says do not buy fresh bread for it, "your grandmother would be disappointed in you." Or a soft-boiled egg with soldiers on Sunday morning, and do not rush the dipping, because the yolk needs to be respected. Or shepherd's pie on a Tuesday. I'll caution that this is narrated storytelling, and I can't verify the specific claims against anything outside the video. Treat it as a mood piece with good instructions, not as royal archive material. The day-old-bread rule, though, is correct and sourced from every grandmother who ever lived.
