## Recipe Recommendation

Smoked salmon is the recommendation, because after a day of Gordon Ramsay screaming at grown men about raw meatballs, a fish that cooks to a thermometer number feels like therapy. It's Meat Church's [Grilled Salmon](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/grilled-salmon), planked, smoked low, and finished with butter instead of a hot mess of anything else. It's light-to-moderate, so one fillet-sized portion per person is plenty. The fish is rich enough that you'll feel like you got away with something.

*gluten-free · pescatarian · low-sugar · smoked · spicy · pairs with: 805*

**Ingredients**
- 1 full salmon filet
- Cedar grilling planks (or unwaxed butcher paper on a pellet grill)
- A sugar-free, gluten-free BBQ rub, with a pinch of cayenne for real heat (swap: replaces the honey-based rub in the original)
- 1/2 stick of butter, melted
- Hickory, pecan, alder or fruit wood

**Method**
1. Bring the smoker to 275 degrees using a lighter wood. Pecan, alder and fruit wood all play nicely with fish.
2. Set the filet on the planks, which means at least 2 for a full side. Season it on all sides with the rub.
3. Brush the melted butter over the fish when it hits 115 to 120 internal, about 5 to 10 minutes before the end. Swap: skip the 1 cup of maple syrup from the original and use butter alone, so the glaze stays savory and the smoke isn't buried in sugar.
4. Pull it at 125 degrees internal. The source likes 120 to 125 and calls 145 too far.
5. Rest it on a plate for 10 minutes, then eat.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, because its crisp bitterness cuts the butter and keeps up with the cayenne.

## Barbecue and Live Fire

### Mad Scientist BBQ: Cabrito and a Ring of Fire

The best food video today is Mad Scientist BBQ's "Cabrito: A South Texas Secret?" It's an open-fire kid goat cook, and the technique is the real story. The cook builds what he calls a crown of fire around the cabrito. The wind is blowing across the fire, so building more fire on the other sides makes convection heat that wraps the meat for the last stretch, about forty more minutes before eating. His host's translation is "La Corona de Fuego." Someone then points out that Johnny Cash would call it a ring of fire. That's a dad joke I'd have been proud to write, so I'm furious about it.

There's also a tip the cook says he picked up in Patagonia. When your fire is moving and keeps dying, you lay the coals out in a square and then aim the heat where you want it. Then comes the reveal, with color on both sides of the animal, and a polite negotiation over whether anyone will eat the kidney. The answer is "up to you," which is the most honest thing anyone has ever said about offal. Good for them.

It's coals, wind, a square, and a patient man. Meanwhile you own an app that tells you the humidity outside. It's 87 percent, by the way. Sticky. Go outside and smell a goat.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main piece skimmed the surface. The sources are thin on some of it, so I'll say so where they are and keep my guessing to a minimum.

### Mad Scientist BBQ: What the Cabrito Cook Is Actually Teaching

The first piece treated the "crown of fire" as a cute bit. It's a technique, and the video states its logic. The wind is blowing in one direction, so the fire is built "here and there" to create "a kind of convection heat around the cabrito." The cook calls it "the finishing move." The video says it's good for "the last punch," and that after about 40 more minutes everyone eats. It's the last stretch, where you stop leaning on direct radiant heat from one side and wrap the animal in heat from several directions.

Wind is a variable you plan around, not a nuisance you curse at. If your heat source is on one side and the breeze is pushing it that way, the far side of the meat is going to lag. Add a second source and you even it out. The video shows the payoff when the cook says he loves "the color on both sides." That's the evidence in the pudding: even browning is the proof the crown worked.

The Patagonia tip is easy to misread, so here's exactly what the transcript says. "When you're moving your fire and your fire goes out, this is a trick that I learned in Patagonia. You have to make a square." Then he asks the camera, twice, "Can you see that square?" and says you can "aim to the fire." The transcript doesn't explain the geometry any further, and I won't invent a physics lecture to fill the gap. Coals get shaped on purpose, not dumped in a heap and hoped over.

Most backyard people think about temperature as a number on a dial. The cabrito video, out on October 22, is a good reminder that live fire is a spatial skill.

There's also a tiny social detail I like. Early in the clip a man is described as someone who "builds crosses too," and the cook says that if you want a custom cross, "he's a good guy to talk to." The transcript gives no more context, so I'm not guessing about what the cross is for. I'm only noting that the cook's setup is a community effort, with the person who built the rig recommended out loud like a neighbor vouching for a plumber. Barbecue is a trade built on referrals. Nobody at a pit says "I bought this from a catalog."

### Mad Scientist BBQ: The Meat Fest as a Logistics Problem

Look at it again as an operations problem, because that's the part home cooks never see. The host says a friend, Michael, "showed up with his 500 gallon offset smoker. Without which we couldn't have cooked all the food. Simply impossible." The word "impossible" is doing real work. Event-scale cooking is physically capped by pit capacity, and without the one big rig the menu doesn't exist.

The tail of the transcript confirms the scale. Someone says "let's start loading the 500, I guess," and then there's a line about waiting until the pit is loaded so they can "get a bit of a break," followed by a "post pit load coffee." That is the whole job in three lines: load the pit, wait, drink coffee, repeat.

The host also says he'd like the event to be repeated, and that "it won't be as hot." Heat matters for the crew as much as the meat. When people talk about pitmaster endurance, they usually mean the long overnight. The video is a reminder that a hot day on your feet slicing is the other kind of endurance. The shout-outs are specific, too: Matt for putting it on, Brad for organizing, and Chief Firewood for supplying wood. Firewood is named as a sponsor-level contributor, which tells you how seriously the crew treats fuel. You can have a 500 gallon pit and a thousand pounds of brisket, but if the wood doesn't show up you've built an expensive planter.

I'll also point out the promise the host makes at the start of that segment, which is to "show up" if it happens again. Compare that with the Kitchen Nightmares episode and its £2 million restaurant, where nobody came. Same species of project, opposite outcome. One had friends and firewood and the other had a budget.

### Ramsay's Three Sandwich Videos: What the Transcripts Don't Say

I'll be careful here, because all three Ramsay sandwich clips are fragments that start and stop mid-sentence, and I don't want to invent a winner.

In the patty melt video, the useful detail is the competing visions. One entry is praised for "a little Persian influence throughout," which means the cook put a flavor identity into a diner classic instead of copying it. The other has steak that's "literally just melting in your mouth" and is called "messy, delicious." Ramsay's own summary is "two very different patty melts," with "one clear winner." The transcript doesn't tell me who won, so I'm leaving it at that. For a home cook, the lesson is that a patty melt can go in two directions: refine it with a spice or flavor angle, or lean into richness and mess. Both got praise. What neither got was blandness.

The second video, with Owen Han and H Woo, has one detail the first piece mentioned and didn't dig into: the sandwich in the hot dog bun. Ramsay says it "looks disproportionate" and then says he loves the filling, which is "generous," with "the wrap nice and tight" and "a little bit of lime." Tight construction matters. A big filling in a loose bun falls apart in the first bite.

The sauce comment is the thing worth keeping. One creator says the bourbon "really threw me off of the sauce" and the garlic butter "just tasted like straight bourbon." The cook says he knew it. If you're building a pan sauce or a compound butter with spirits, you need to cook or balance it so the spirit reads as depth, not as a shot glass. The transcript doesn't say whether the cook reduced it or added it raw, so I can't diagnose the mistake. I can only say that when the garlic butter tastes like straight bourbon, the garlic has lost.

Ramsay then praises the other sandwich with "sweet, sour, spicy" and "messy," and says messy is "the sign of a great sandwich." That's a genuine opinion, and a useful one: a sandwich that stays tidy might not be generous enough. The result was a draw, and the host says the decision was "really tough," so the show ends with a call for a trilogy and a duo competition. You can't rank sweet-sour-spicy against a tight, generous, limey wrap without admitting they're different sports.

The third video, the Airrack and Jesser meatball sub contest, has the clearest cooking lesson of the three. The cook's own explanation is the part to underline: "I was going as fast as I was trying to cook him because I was worried it was raw." He rushed because he feared raw meatballs, and the meatballs were raw in the middle. Ramsay's response is the practical rule: "You had to check him before you put him on there." That is what every home cook needs tattooed on the inside of the oven door. If you're worried a meatball is raw, cut one open before plating.

The winning entry, in contrast, turned a meatlover sub into an "open face pizza," which Ramsay approved. He singled out the jalapeno as "smart," and said the dish was made by "caramelization of that beautiful sausage." Browning is flavor. The transcript is garbled on names, and it's ambiguous who is which, so I'm sticking to the dishes.

### Kitchen Nightmares UK: The Rule of Eleven

The first piece covered the plate-crowding complaint and the Derek Brown verdict.

Ramsay's rule is expressed as an escalation: if you're past "eight, nine, ten things on the plate," call him, because he's "coming back when you get to 11." He also references an earlier comment from the chef about "12 or 15 things on one plate." So the chef's own ambition, by his own earlier admission, was an even bigger plate than the one that got criticized. Ramsay is already negotiating him down.

The critic's verdict adds a second angle. He says the chef's "technique" was careful in "the whole preparation of each of your dishes," which means this isn't a skill problem. His complaint is that "it was too complicated," with "ten different flavors on the plate." The specific example is the artichoke, which had "garlic and herbs" even though it "is itself a delicate thing," and the diner found it "a little bit overpowering." The transcript cuts off at "the shoulder," so I don't know what the verdict on that dish was, and I won't guess.

If an ingredient is delicate, don't bury it. Adding garlic and herbs to a dish that was already complete is the kitchen equivalent of talking over a good song. The same lesson shows up in the bourbon butter, where one loud ingredient flattened the others. Two different videos, two different chefs, same sin: someone forgot to ask what the dish needed less of.

The episode is framed around ambition and a Michelin star. Ramsay says "we do have one thing in common. Ambition," and ends by saying he sincerely hopes the chef makes it a success, adding "who knows? Maybe the Michelin star will follow." That's as warm as Ramsay gets on camera. He's rooting for him while telling him he's wrong, which is the standard format of the show and arguably the standard format of a good mentor.

### What It All Means for the Kitchen

The cabrito cook watches the wind and shapes the coals. The meat fest crew plans around a pit and a pile of wood. The sandwich contestants lose on salt, on a boozy sauce, and on undercooked meatballs. The Michelin-minded chef loses on plate count.

For a home cook, the actionable list is short and written in prose, as God and Nova intended. Check the center of anything you were nervous about before you serve it. Treat alcohol in a sauce as a seasoning, not a headline. Do not add garlic to something delicate just because the garlic is there. If you cook over live fire, think about where the wind is going and give the far side a heat source. If you cook for a crowd, find your Michael with the 500 gallon pit before you invite anyone.
