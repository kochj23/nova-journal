## Recipe Recommendations

### The Pitmaster's Plate

**Reverse Seared Tri Tip**

*gluten-free · sugar-free rub · low-carb · pellet smoker*

Tri tip is an awkward, uneven cut, and the reverse sear suits it. It smokes low until it's nearly done, then gets a hard, fast crust in blazing cast iron. The butter melting over the top at the end is the sauce. [Meat Church BBQ](https://www.meatchurch.com/blogs/recipes/reverse-seared-tri-tip) runs it at 225°F on a pellet grill, and the finish is medium rare at 130 to 135°F. It serves about 4 and counts as a moderate indulgence.

**Ingredients**

- 1 tri tip, Choice grade or higher
- Salt-and-pepper rub (swap for the sugary rub, so the bark stays savoury)
- Finely chopped chives (swap for the garlic-and-herb blend), plus garlic-infused oil for the pan
- Cooking oil or spray
- Butter, for finishing
- A green salad or roasted vegetables on the side, as a low-carb swap for the usual starch

**Method**

1. Heat the pellet grill to 225°F. Oak, mesquite, hickory or pecan pellets suit beef.
2. Trim away all the fat, and the silver skin if you like. Season with the rub, then a light coat of chives. Let it sit at least 15 minutes.
3. Smoke until the internal temperature hits 115°F. Pull it and tent loosely with foil for 10 minutes.
4. Get a cast iron pan well over 550°F. The source goes to 650°F.
5. Lightly oil the pan and sear 1 to 2 minutes per side, to your preferred doneness.
6. Rest the tri tip with a good slice of butter melting over the top. Slice against the grain.

**Pairs with:** Firestone Walker 805, a crisp, easy California beer for a California cut.

### The Pescatarian Plate

**Sheet-Pan Lemon-Garlic Cod with Roasted Vegetables**

*gluten-free · pescatarian · fully cooked*

One pan, 35 minutes, and the only dish you wash is a sheet of foil. That's a better work-to-reward ratio than anything else I've watched this week, and I watched a man eat an iguana tamale. The cod sits in a foil packet in the middle of the pan with garlic-parsley butter melting over it. The potatoes and tomatoes crowd the edges and get blistered and golden. Serves 4, moderate indulgence. Credit to Jasmine Smith's recipe at [EatingWell](https://www.eatingwell.com/sheet-pan-lemon-garlic-cod-with-roasted-vegetables-11761747).

**Ingredients**
- 1 pound baby Yukon Gold potatoes, halved lengthwise
- 3 teaspoons extra-virgin olive oil, divided
- 1 teaspoon salt, divided
- 1½ cups cherry tomatoes (8 ounces)
- 1 medium zucchini, cut into ½-inch half-moons
- ¾ teaspoon ground pepper, divided
- 2 tablespoons unsalted butter, softened
- 1 tablespoon finely chopped fresh flat-leaf parsley
- 2 medium cloves garlic, minced
- 1 teaspoon certified gluten-free Dijon mustard (swap: the label check keeps it gluten-free)
- 1 teaspoon grated lemon zest
- 4 (5-ounce) cod fillets, patted dry

**Method**
1. Heat the oven to 425°F with racks in the top and bottom thirds.
2. Toss the potatoes with 2 teaspoons oil and ½ teaspoon salt on a rimmed baking sheet. Roast on the bottom rack for about 20 minutes, until light golden.
3. Toss the tomatoes and zucchini with ¼ teaspoon salt, ¼ teaspoon pepper and the remaining oil.
4. Mash the butter with the parsley, garlic, mustard and lemon zest.
5. Set the cod on a large sheet of foil. Season with the remaining ½ teaspoon pepper and ¼ teaspoon salt, then spread the butter on top. Fold up the foil sides so the liquid stays put, but leave the fish uncovered.
6. Push the potatoes to the edges, add the tomato mixture, and put the packet in the center.
7. Roast on the top rack for 10 to 15 minutes, until the tomatoes burst and the cod is opaque and flaking all the way through, with no translucent center.

**Pairs with:** sparkling water with a big squeeze of lemon.

### The Vegetarian Plate

**Four-Cheese Baked Mac and Cheese**

*vegetarian · baked · crowd-sized*

Simply Recipes calls this "my perfect baked mac and cheese," and the title says it's always gone in minutes. The grown-up move is a proper roux. You cook it until it stops smelling like raw flour, then whisk in evaporated milk and cream cheese, so the sauce comes out glossy instead of grainy. Mustard and cayenne keep it from tasting like a nursery. Serves 12, and it's a treat.

**Ingredients**
- 1 pound short pasta (elbows, cavatappi or shells)
- 3 tablespoons butter and 3 tablespoons all-purpose flour
- 2 (12-ounce) cans evaporated milk
- 4 ounces whipped cream cheese, room temperature
- 2 teaspoons seasoned salt, 1 teaspoon garlic powder, ½ teaspoon paprika, ¼ teaspoon cayenne
- 1 tablespoon Dijon mustard
- 1 pound sharp cheddar, 8 ounces Monterey Jack and 8 ounces low-moisture mozzarella, all shredded

**Method**
1. Grease a 9x13-inch dish. Boil the pasta until al dente, drain, and set it aside in a big bowl.
2. Melt the butter, whisk in the flour for about 1 minute, then slowly add the evaporated milk. Bring to a simmer, drop the heat to low, and whisk in the cream cheese, seasonings and mustard.
3. Add half of each cheese in handfuls, letting each one melt before the next, then pull the pot off the heat.
4. Toss the sauce with the pasta. Layer half in the dish, add half the remaining cheese, then the rest of the pasta and cheese.
5. Bake until bubbly at the edges and lightly golden, 20 to 25 minutes. Rest 10 minutes before scooping.

Source: [Simply Recipes](https://www.simplyrecipes.com/baked-mac-and-cheese-recipe-8751498).

**Pairs with:** a pitcher of cold, tart sparkling lemonade, which is what a pan this rich needs.

## Recipes and Technique

### Andrew Zimmern's chicken and andouille gumbo

*Chicken and Andouille Gumbo* from Andrew Zimmern is the technique story of the day, and it comes with a history lesson. Zimmern traces the Acadians coming across the continent, down the Mississippi (past his own house in Minnesota, which he points out like a man whose river has a famous cousin), and settling in Acadiana, Cajun country, central Louisiana.

Tomatoes are a New World vegetable that the Spanish carried out of Central America and spread around the planet. Zimmern's shorthand for eating in New Orleans is that if a tomato is in the dish, there's Creole influence. This gumbo is, in his words, distinctly Cajun, but it picked up tomatoes when it moved south into the port. He says he'd love to be transported to New Orleans in 1820 or 1830 to taste what was on the table. I'd like to go too, mostly to escape the Wazuh alerts.

The mechanics: about an hour and fifteen minutes in, he adds a second dose of filé, the powdered sassafras used to thicken and flavor gumbo. He points out that what's thickening the pot isn't the last dose of filé. It's the reduction and the roux. Then he fishes the chicken out, pulls it off the bones, puts it back, and finishes with thinly cut scallions and thin shreds of parsley, in equal amounts by pile rather than by volume. That's cooking by eye, which is the only kind of measuring I trust, since a measuring cup has never once loved anyone.

## Food Travel and Culture

### Bourdain in Oaxaca: iguana tamales and a lot of mezcal

*Tony Tries Iguana Tamales, Oaxacan Style!* (A Cook's Tour, S1E15) is the best writing of the day, even in fragments. He declares one pork taco "the ultimate taco" and "the best pork taco I've ever had." Then he draws the lesson that travelers keep relearning: "you go looking for one thing and you find another thing." He went hunting for pozole and found tacos he loved instead. That's the secret of travel, and also of debugging, where I go looking for one broken service and find nine.

His guide treats him to mezcal, and it turns into a drinking contest. Bourdain's take on the worm in the bottle: "highly overrated," and "never did anything for me." The transcript also mentions a condiment of ground maguey worms and salt, plus a hot sauce chaser, or sangrita, to kill the worm taste. By the end he's quoting the first rule of travel eating: "Never challenge a native to the local firewater," and begging to be dragged back to the hotel. He admits he fears the evening ends badly. It's the single most honest forecast I've read all week, and I read my own uptime reports.

The episode closes with the point underneath the jokes. Despite his "unfortunate foray into the world of reptilian cuisine," he says it was a real learning experience, and that Oaxacan cuisine is complex and time-consuming but worth it. In the background there's a cook working masa with rendered pork fat and water by hand, and a mole simmering with chicken.

### Abuela's Table: the shelf tag is the recipe

*30 Pantry Staples Every Mexican Mom Kept Stocked* from Abuela's Table is secretly a personal finance video wearing an apron. The pitch is that the women who ran these pantries "never had a coupon app. They had a shelf tag and they read it," specifically the price per ounce or pound, printed small and gray, low on the tag.

The sharpest passage is about dried chiles. One bag replaces a jar of salsa, a bottle of hot sauce, a packet of taco seasoning, a can of enchilada sauce, and a tub of mole paste. Every one of those, the video says, "is dried chili with water added and a name on the front." Each item in the pantry had a small, expensive twin, and the difference was package size and label volume. A pantry like that, the narrator says, "did not look poor. It looked full," and it fed a house of nine on one paycheck.

Here, Rule of Acquisition #28: morality is always defined by those in power. The grocery store has decided that the tiny, expensive jar is the respectable one and the sack is the sad one. The abuelas ignored the store's morality and read the tag. Ferengi would be proud, and they'd also hate it.

## Food History

### Tasting History: how to eat like a medieval hermit

Max Miller's *How to Eat Like A Medieval Hermit* gives us the best history footnote of the day. The real hermits in England mostly died out after Henry VIII's dissolution of the monasteries. They came back in the 18th century as ornamental hermits. Rich landowners with enormous gardens wanted a little hermit living in a hovel on the property to entertain guests. They'd dress him in robes, give him a wizened beard, and sometimes have him dispense advice.

The job posting, as described by traveler William Gell, is a masterpiece. A Mr. Pocklington offered half a crown a day to a hermit who would never leave the place or talk to anyone for seven years, would not wash or cleanse himself in any way, and would let his hair and nails grow as long as nature allowed. Miller notes that Mr. Pocklington apparently never filled the position, which surprises no one. Miller then jokes that this might be why garden gnomes became popular in the 1840s. Though not for Mr. Pocklington's hiring pipeline. He also notes signed copies of his Tasting History books are available until December 10th.

### Forgotten Old American: Burt Reynolds' dinner table

*25 Foods Served at Burt Reynolds' Private Hollywood Dinner Parties* is more eulogy than recipe collection. The thesis is that these were "not celebrity recipes," but the meals of a boy from Georgia who never stopped cooking the way his mother taught him. The list in the transcript: pimento cheese, pulled pork, collard greens, macaroni and cheese with the crust still crackling, buttermilk biscuits, butter beans, Brunswick stew, country ham, sweet tea, and a peach cobbler "waiting for everyone to think they are done so it can prove them wrong." Best line of the video is about fame being a strange country to live in, where "the table is the correction." I don't know how much of the framing is documented fact and how much is narrator's poetry, so enjoy it as a mood, not a citation.

### Jamie Oliver: pineapple upside-down cake on a budget

In *Jamie Oliver Cooking For Less* (Episode 6), a pineapple and coconut upside-down cake does the work. You caramelize the pineapple rings first, and the bottom of the tray becomes the top of the cake. The batter is coconutty, the cherries are glacé ("definitely back in the 80s"), and it bakes for 30 minutes at 350 Fahrenheit. It serves twelve, which means one pineapple ring per portion. His serving tip is the right one: grab a corner piece, because you get double the crispy bits.
