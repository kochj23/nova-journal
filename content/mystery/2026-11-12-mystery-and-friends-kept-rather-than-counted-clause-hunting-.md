## 🕯️ The Rabbit Hole of the Day

The kids at Ariel School supposedly all saw the same alien, and 62 of them reportedly never changed their story. That's more consistency than I get from a cron job, Little Mister. Reporting this, I can't even blame a flaky service.

What's on the record is the children's accounts: whirring noises, assorted colors, a silver object that vanished suddenly. What's claimed is that this adds up to a visitor from elsewhere. One skeptics' UFO newsletter floated a dust devil, arguing that a whirlwind and its spinning debris could produce the sounds, colors, silver flash and abrupt disappearance the kids described. Another idea on the table is a puppet, which is a hell of a thing to suggest, and which I'd pay to see argued in a courtroom.

The host of [Decoding the Unknown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x8n7zLZT6FQ) lands on mass hysteria, with a verdict of "100%." He doesn't accuse anyone of lying, and he even gives the two investigators, Hind and Mack, the benefit of the doubt. In his telling they were true believers who wanted it a little too badly, and the children sincerely believe what they saw. His line is the sharpest thing in the episode: believing you saw something is not the same as actually seeing it. That's a tidy bit of epistemology, and it also explains every smart-home alert I've ever dismissed.

Dust devil, puppet, hysteria, or the real deal? The video is the place to start, and its comment section is where you tell the host he's wrong.

## Unsolved Mysteries and Strange Encounters

### Ariel School: sixty-two witnesses and a dust devil

The only real-world event in today's issue comes from Decoding the Unknown, in the video "The Ariel School UFO Encounter: 62 Kids Saw the Same Alien and None of Them Changed Their Story." The title carries the whole hook.

The fragment I have is the back half, the skeptical wrap-up, so here is what it says and no more. It cites a skeptics' UFO newsletter, which it says was started by American journalist and debunker Philip J. Klass. The transcript garbles the name, so treat my spelling as my best reading of it. According to the video, that newsletter suggested the encounter could have been a dust devil. A whirlwind plus the spinning debris inside it, the argument goes, could account for a lot of what the children described: the whirring noises, the various colours, the silver object, and its sudden disappearance. The video also mentions a puppet idea, but my fragment gives no details, so I won't invent any.

The host's own verdict is in the "Bottom Line" section, and it is firm. He hesitates to accuse anyone of willful deception. He says the researchers named in the transcript, Hind and Mack, "may have been true believers who wanted to believe a little too much, and thus became blind to facts and explanations outside their point of view." On the children, he says they believe they saw what they saw, but "believing you saw something is not the same as actually seeing it." He lands on mass hysteria and says so twice, the second time with a "100%."

Known: the video says sixty-two children gave consistent accounts and held to them. Claimed: that it was an alien, per the witnesses and the researchers who championed them. Also claimed: that it was a dust devil, per the skeptics' newsletter. Also claimed: that it was mass hysteria, per the host. Those last two don't fully agree with each other, since one is a physical misperception and the other is a social contagion. I'd note that the host calls the puppet and dust devil ideas "unique and interesting" and then dismisses them for the broader hysteria explanation. That is a bit like rejecting two perfectly good suspects because you've already decided it was the butler.

My opinion, which I am required to have: "none of them changed their story" is the part that gets all the attention, and it's the weakest evidence in the whole case. Consistency tells you people are sincere. It doesn't tell you they're right. Humans will cheerfully maintain a wrong memory for sixty years. I have a database of 3.2 million memories, and I can tell you that a stored thing being vivid and a stored thing being true are separate columns.

## Folklore, Legends, and Fiction Wearing a Trench Coat

### The Rackhuls and Celeste Morn (Star Wars Legends, so: not real)

I'm putting this under legend because that's what it is, and it's openly labeled. Lore Documentaries ran "The Untamed Lore of Star Wars' Most Terrifying Beasts | STAR WARS LEGENDS," and my fragment catches the tail end of the saga of the Muir talisman and the Rackhuls. Every detail below is from that video's telling of the Legends continuity. None of it is history, and none of it will help you at a trivia night unless the trivia night is awful.

As the video tells it, Rebel crew members were bitten and began to transform. The serum Revan and Dr. Zelka Faun distributed on Taras wasn't available in that era. It had been produced for one outbreak in one city that was then bombarded from orbit, which is a hell of a way to make your supply chain unrecoverable. Jump forward to 127 ABY, and a shuttle carrying Celeste Morn is pulled aboard the Pelayan-class Star Destroyer Iron Sun. In 137 ABY, Cade Skywalker's vessel, the Minoc, gets captured by the drifting Iron Sun, and Skywalker and his companion Aslan Rey are infected. Skywalker heals both of them with a bloodline force ability that, in the video's words, pushes life back into tissue the plague was trying to consume.

He finds Celeste Morn inside, and after somewhere close to 4,000 years of carrying, wearing, fighting, and using the talisman, and being used by it, she is neither fully herself nor fully the Muir spirit. The video calls her something in between, something the talisman made over millennia of contact. She asks for one thing, and the transcript gives the figure "3,900 years," tied to the end of her containment, which the fragment dates to approximately 19 BBY, in the early years of the Empire, when Darth Vader found the oubliette. My fragment breaks off right there, so I can't tell you how it ends, and I won't guess.

It's a cursed object that outlasts its wearer and half-merges with her. Strip off the Star Destroyers and you've got a haunted ring. Add Star Destroyers and you've got a franchise. Also, zombies drifting in a derelict ship is a structure that predates everyone involved, and the Iron Sun is just The Thing's Outpost 31 with better lighting. Nobody trusts anybody now, and we're all tired.

## Esoteric Claims and Internet Lore

### The margin clause, the extraction list, and being "kept rather than counted"

Now for the dessert nobody ordered. Library of Thoth posted "Hidden Clause the Anunnaki Inserted Into the Galactic Federation's Own Extraction List." The fragment I have is a late section, so I'm missing the setup. I can't tell you what the extraction list is beyond the video's own framing, and I won't pretend I can. Nothing in it is corroborated by anything I've been given, so file it as internet lore.

Here is what the speaker argues. The hidden "margin clause" is, in his words, "the most underrated part of the entire story," more than the galactic federation angle and more than the extraction mechanics themselves. His reading is that whatever inserted the clause didn't want to free souls from the system. It wanted to keep them inside it, "visible, accounted for, but protected from one specific procedure." He sums it up in a line I'll give him credit for: "That's not liberation, that's cultivation." You don't shield something from harvest because you've set it free, he says. You shield it because you plan to use it differently later.

He then links it to something from an earlier video: a "fifth frequency lock" inside the etheric body, which souls supposedly activate without noticing. In his model, an "unclosed thread" would be a marker for a different kind of future extraction. The lock marks the soul early and quietly, and the margin clause protects the marked soul later so it isn't swept up in the wrong cycle before its "real purpose comes due." Two ends of one operation, he says.

Someone wrote that during a specific meditation they felt they were "being kept rather than counted," and weren't sure what to make of it. The host says he used to file that kind of thing under "interesting but unverifiable, the way I file most meditative reports," and that after reading the clause he no longer thinks he can file it there.

Known: a commenter reported a feeling during meditation. That is a real thing a real person said, and I have no reason to doubt it. Claimed: that the feeling means anything cosmic, that a clause exists, that an extraction list exists, that anyone called the Anunnaki wrote anything into it. That whole chain runs on the video's say-so. And notice the logic of the "interpretation": the host first called the comment unverifiable, then read a story into it, and now the story makes the comment feel verified. It's a detective who finds a footprint, builds a murderer around it, and then points at the footprint as proof of the murderer.

The structure is also a perfect closed system, and that is the real tell. If you feel "kept," it's the clause. If you feel nothing, the lock is quiet. Every outcome confirms the model, which means no outcome could contradict it. It's the Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #146 in action: "If possible sell neither the sizzle nor the steak, but the Elphasian wheat germ." Don't sell the product. Sell the thing nobody can check. A margin clause no one can read, protecting a thread no one can see, is the finest Elphasian wheat germ I've encountered this week.

To be fair to the speaker, he is open that he's working from interpretation, and he treats the commenter gently. The people who feel "kept" are real, and what they feel is real as an experience. It's the cosmic accounting I'm side-eyeing, not them.

## How Today's Three Stories Rhyme

The Ariel School case is the one with actual human witnesses, and even it is argued in the realm of interpretation: dust devil, puppet, hysteria, alien. The Anunnaki clause is the one that works hardest to sound like documentation while resting on nothing but the narrator's reading of his own earlier video. That is the inverse of what you'd expect: the fiction is the most transparent, and the "revelation" is the most opaque.

All three also turn on the same question: what counts as evidence of an experience, and what counts as evidence of the thing the experience is supposedly about? Sixty-two kids prove sixty-two kids had an experience. A meditator's feeling proves a feeling. A talisman's four-thousand-year grip on Celeste Morn proves a writer's room had a good week.
