## 🕯️ The Rabbit Hole of the Day

Everyone "knows" Christian Rome rolled over the pagan world like a bulldozer with a halo, torching temples, killing philosophers, and ending the old gods in one tidy afternoon. It's a great story.

The Christian writers who documented the era shaped how everyone since has pictured it, and that picture bled into the assumption that the conflict raged everywhere. Archaeologists point out that there are only a handful of documented cases of temples being destroyed, even though the Serapeum at Alexandria gets all the headlines. The anti-pagan laws look terrifying on the page, but modern scholars now treat them as shaky evidence, because the legislation leaned on the same polemical rhetoric as the sources around it. One comparative study of Roman violence found no increase in Late Antiquity. Evidence from North Africa shows traditional paganism staying vital, and persuasion may have mattered more than force. Even the saint everyone cites, Martin of Tours, is described by a biographer who knew him as smashing shrines and idols but not threatening people.

The Oxford Handbook of Late Antiquity says torture and murder "were not the inevitable result of the rise of Christianity," which is a careful way of admitting there were a few ugly episodes. The myth died hard, which is fitting for a religion about resurrection.

Start with [Wikipedia's article on the persecution of pagans in the late Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persecution_of_pagans_in_the_late_Roman_Empire), then chase the footnotes into the [historiography of the Christianization of the Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historiography_of_the_Christianization_of_the_Roman_Empire). Bring snacks. The scholars are still arguing.

Good morning, Little Mister's readers, and welcome to a mystery roundup running on three sources, all of which start or end mid-sentence like they were cut by an editor with a grudge. Today's haul is one fan-lore essay about space stations nobody wants to haul away, one war documentary that stops just as it gets interesting, and one esoteric video explaining that aliens can't reassign your soul if you have good edges. No deep sea today. No folklore. No cryptid. I'm bored and underfed, and I'm told to write less rather than pad, so I will respect that, grudgingly, like a cat respecting a closed door.

## Strange History and Lost Places

### The Green Beret With a Past, and a Mountain in the Clouds

Dark Docs posted "The SS Officer So Dangerous They Made Him a Green Beret," and I'll be straight with you: the excerpt I have never gets to the SS part. The transcript doesn't back it up, so I'm not going to either.

What the fragment does show is a vivid, tightly shot account of a first mission. A man called Thorn in one passage and "Thorne" at the end (the transcript's spelling is a mess, which is what you get from auto-captions) was photographed at Cam Duque on October 15, standing with the Vietnamese pilots and the patrol leader. Three days later, the program flew its first mission across a border. The narration says the enemy on the other side was the one he first shot at "as a teenager in the snow," and that there was "no rescue, because rescue meant admitting it existed." That's the heart of it: a deniable operation, where the fine print of the plan is that if you get into trouble, nobody comes, because coming would confirm you were there. The Cosa Nostra word for this is omertà, the code of silence. The mob kept it about murders. This program kept it about a country it officially wasn't in.

The mission itself: two South Vietnamese H-34 helicopters lifted off into weather the narrator calls "already wrong," rain on the windscreens and clouds lying flat on the ridgelines, no horizon to fly against. An American Bird Dog spotter plane, a single-engine aircraft, went up with them. One helicopter carried the six men of Recon Team Iowa. The other carried Thorn, in a seat assigned to another officer until he asked for it. His logic, per the video, was that if the team landed on something it couldn't fight, he meant to be close enough to go in after them.

The lead helicopter found a break in the cloud and dropped through it, into ground nobody had landed on, in a country with no American soldiers in it. It set down in a clearing the pilot had never seen. Six men went out into the treeline in daylight, and the helicopter climbed back out through the same hole. Then, per the narration, nothing to do but circle, wait for a call, and watch the fuel burn down.

The best detail is the phrase pilots used for what killed men in that kind of weather: a mountain standing inside the murk with nothing to mark where it began. "Somewhere out in front of every aircraft still flying that afternoon, there was one." The excerpt ends on "The team was in position. Thorne answer," and then the transcript quits. I have no idea what happened to those six men, and I'm not going to guess. Joke about it and I'd be a ghoul, so the only wisecrack here is that the transcript cutting off at the worst possible second is the most Dark Docs thing imaginable. 😏

What's claimed: the headline's SS backstory, and anything about the man's reputation, which the fragment doesn't touch. If you want the rest, watch the video, and be suspicious of any video that opens with a superlative.

## Internet Lore and Fandom Archaeology

### The Empire's Fatal Habit, or Why Nobody Cleans Up Orbit

Lore Documentaries has a long video, "The Doomed Stations of Star Wars | The Death Star and the Empire's Fatal Habit." Yes, it's fiction. Yes, I'm filing it under lore, because the thesis is sharper than most of the real-world institutional analysis I've read, and nobody in a galaxy far, far away has to testify before a committee.

The excerpt opens mid-thought about something that sat untouched on an ocean moon "for thirty-one years." The transcript doesn't say what, and I'm not going to invent it. It then mocks Starkiller Base, which in the First Order's own mythology "was going to last forever" and "lasted one afternoon." Which is, I'd argue, the correct review of most infrastructure projects that come with their own press release.

But the video's real subject is the small stuff. Relay platforms above forgotten colony worlds, their solar panels "still tracking a star that nobody is watching." Docking rings above ore-processing moons where the last ship left two generations ago, and nobody sent a salvage crew "because nobody wanted to pay for one." Hulls left tumbling in orbit after the yards went dark, "too expensive to tow, too dangerous to ignore," logged on navigation charts as debris hazards. "Nobody mourns them nobody remembers who paid for them." In Cosa Nostra terms, these are no-show jobs: still on the payroll, still in orbit, doing absolutely nothing, and nobody has the nerve to cut the check.

Then the video turns to the actual argument, and it's a good one. The Empire kept building stations despite the knowledge that stations lose to a mobile, well-informed enemy. "That persistence isn't stupidity, it's the shape of an institution in the grip of its own logic." A government that staked its legitimacy on protecting specific places can't choose mobility without admitting specific places can be abandoned. A military whose prestige lives in visible hardware can't pivot to invisible rapid response without giving up the hardware that made it legible. And a weapons contractor that built a whole sector's economy around orbital platforms can't survive the program being canceled. The line that sticks: "the station is an anchor."

Look at that and tell me it isn't every enterprise IT roadmap you've ever seen. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #260: gambling is like the way to power, the only way to win is to cheat, but don't get caught in the process. The Empire's version was to keep doubling down on the Death Star and hope nobody checked the exhaust port. Battlestar Galactica has the epitaph: all of this has happened before, and will happen again. So say we all, and so say the dozen-odd tumbling hulls on the debris chart.

It's a framing, not a finding.

## Esoteric and Religious History

### The Soul That Can't Be Filed

Library of Thoth uploaded "If You Were Born With This Soul Frequency, Thoth Said the Anunnaki Cannot Assign You a New Loop." The channel credits all of this to "the tablets" and to Thoth. I can't verify from the excerpt which text, if any, it's drawing on, so everything below is the channel's claim, not documented history. My own aside, for what it's worth: Thoth is an Egyptian god and the Anunnaki are Mesopotamian deities, so a "tablet" in which one lectures about the other, and about reincarnation "loops" and "frequencies," is a modern internet mashup. That's my observation, not the video's.

Now the pitch, which is more thoughtful than the title. The excerpt warns that "spirituality that feels like identity and has no edges at all" is the real danger, that modern seekers got "the most beautiful vocabulary for dissolving our own shape and told it was awakening." The genuine "frequency" supposedly isn't recognized by its size but by its specificity. The shiver at the back of the neck "can be generated by a choir, a film score, a well-timed sentence from a stranger on the internet." Specificity can't be faked, the narrator says, because it has consequences: it tells you what you are and what you are not. And a soul with clear edges "cannot be reassigned."

Here the video's cosmology gets Cabin in the Woods. In that film, a bureaucratic Facility assigns five young people their roles, the Whore, the Athlete, the Scholar, the Fool, and the Virgin, so the Ancient Ones get their sacrifice by dawn. The roles are assigned, not chosen, and the only way out is to refuse the part. The video's "sorting instrument" is the same office: a soul with no defined shape "can be filed under any category, dropped into any loop, matched to any set of parents and circumstances and crises." Only a particular enough shape objects. Here "objection" means that "the frequency was received, and the subtraction was made, and the resulting shape is too particular to be pressed into a" mold. The transcript stops mid-sentence, which I'd call fitting, because the mold's shape doesn't matter if the sentence never ends.

I'll give the video one point: "specificity has consequences" is genuinely useful advice, and it works fine without any Anunnaki. Be a specific person. Nobody can reassign you if you're a pain in the ass about it.

## Deeper Cuts

### The Splice in the Empire Video

Lore Documentaries' "The Doomed Stations of Star Wars | The Death Star and the Empire's Fatal Habit" reaches me as two chunks stapled together. The first ends on something sitting "untouched for thirty-one years" in a system with an ocean moon, and the "they" in that sentence has no antecedent. The second begins with the word "mode" and a stray doubled "The," then gets going on a "mobile, well-informed enemy." Whatever the missing setup was, I can't see it, so any claim about what the video says there would be made up.

I also owe a correction on the hulls. The captions read "golen hulls left tumbling at bilbringy," and I can read most of that. "Bilbringy" is nonsense, though, and the main piece's "in orbit" was my interpretation, not something the transcript supports. The fragment does say they were logged as debris hazards, too expensive to tow and too dangerous to ignore.

Starkiller Base was "going to last forever" and "lasted one afternoon," while "the smaller ones endure." The video's irony is that the grand, expensive, press-release stations die young and the boring relay platforms outlive the civilization that built them. Of the Death Star itself, the excerpt says nothing. It's in the title only.

Then comes the line "they are immovable, that was always the point." Immovability is presented as a design goal, not a flaw. Look at what the three institutional anchors in the video are: a government's legitimacy, a military's prestige, a contractor's economy. None of them is about the enemy. Each is about the builder needing to be seen. The military's hardware matters because it makes the institution "legible," the video's word. The contractor can't "absorb the cancellation." So the stations aren't there to win anything. They're there so somebody's org chart still makes sense.

The relay platforms, "their solar panels still tracking a star that nobody is watching," are the funniest image in the piece. Blessed is the mind too small for doubt, as the Warhammer 40,000 priests say about devotion that never checks whether anyone is listening. Those panels keep tracking, faithful to the end, and it's all for you, Damien. Meanwhile the Firefly crew would just say you can't stop the signal, and they'd be right, because nobody's left to stop it.

### What the Green Beret Fragment Adds

Dark Docs' "The SS Officer So Dangerous They Made Him a Green Beret" has a detail I didn't use. Other aircraft in the area "had read the sky and turned for base." So the weather wasn't a surprise the mission blundered into. It was a warning other crews had already read and obeyed.

The second detail is a tension in the video's own account. The narration says rescue was never coming because rescue "meant admitting it existed," but it also says Thorn took a seat on the second helicopter so that, if the team landed on something it couldn't fight, he'd be "close enough to go in after them." Those two statements pull against each other: a program with no rescue, and a man planning one anyway. The fragment doesn't resolve it. Whether the video frames it as nerve, as defiance, or as foreshadowing is my reading, not the video's explicit claim.

Third, the only gesture toward the title's backstory is one sentence: the enemy across the border "was the one he'd first shot at as a teenager in the snow." It names no war, no army, and no uniform. That sentence is the whole bridge between the thumbnail's promise and the excerpt's content, and it's a thin bridge. I'd call the SS claim unsupported by this fragment, not false. I just can't see it.

As for the spellings, "Cam Duque" and "Thorne answer" look like captioner debris, and I'm not correcting a place name or finishing a sentence I can't verify, which is more discipline than the transcript showed in its last line. 😏

### The Cliffhanger at the Library of Thoth

Library of Thoth's "If You Were Born With This Soul Frequency, Thoth Said the Anunnaki Cannot Assign You a New Loop" ends on a teaser. It says there's "a piece of the architecture designed to catch the signal in the narrow window between receiving it and naming it," and that "the tablets describe exactly where in your day that window opens." The excerpt stops before telling you where. I'll note, as my own observation, that this is a retention hook, a promise dangled ahead of the payoff to keep you watching. Fèihuà, the Firefly crew's Mandarin for "nonsense," is too harsh. This is nonsense with a marketing department.

Two other bits went unremarked. The video says "the list is a sorting instrument," but whatever list it means isn't in the excerpt. And the list of things that can fake a shiver down your neck starts mid-stream: all we get is "the shiver at the back of the neck," then "all of that can be generated by a choir, a film score, a well-timed sentence from a stranger on the internet." The earlier items are gone.

The vocabulary is what interests me. The video talks about an "extractable soul," about "subtraction," about filing, sorting and reassigning. It's bookkeeping language wrapped around a cosmic claim. That's the same register the Star Wars video uses for institutions: legible, absorbed, canceled. Each is a fragment about a thing that doesn't want to be examined too closely.

### Where the Rabbit Holes Are

If you love a good hole, there are three, and none can be climbed out of from here. What was sitting on that ocean moon for thirty-one years? What happened to Recon Team Iowa after the helicopter left them in that clearing? And where in your day does the window open?

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #186: there are two things that will catch up with you for sure, death and taxes. The mountain in the murk is the first, and the salvage crew nobody wanted to pay for is the second. Both get you eventually, and neither accepts a press release.
