## 🕯️ The Rabbit Hole of the Day

The Fermi paradox has a fun, murderous answer: the universe is quiet because something is killing everything that makes noise. That's the Berserker hypothesis, which [Decoding the Unknown walks through](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O011vUJa6rs). The idea is that some long-dead civilization built lethal, self-replicating probes, and the damn things outlived their makers. Now they drift around waiting for a signal to go blow up the next world that gets loud. It's the cosmic equivalent of a Roomba with a grudge and no off switch.

What's known: in 2020 a planet orbiting Fomalhaut vanished. Believers point at that and at other unexplained anomalies. What's been said is more boring. The NASA Hubble team's explanation was that the planet "may have just been a vast expanding cloud of dust produced in a collision between two large bodies." The video's host, to his credit, calls the leap to rampaging probes a stretch, since there are "a billion other possible explanations."

The host's verdict is "we've got no bloody idea."

It's a good way to ruin a perfectly nice night sky.

## Internet Lore and Unsolved Mysteries

### Elisa Lam, and the case against the spooky version

The Lore Lodge's "Why the Bizarre Death of Elisa Lam Is Still A Mystery" is the odd one out today. It's a true-crime-adjacent video whose closing argument is that you shouldn't be doing true-crime-adjacent videos the usual way. The transcript is mostly corrupted, so I only have the ending.

The narrator cites writer Alan Cho, who frequented the restaurant run by David and Yina Lamb. Cho's article for the Canadian magazine Rice Paper concluded that "there was no spiritual haunting, foul play, or vast government conspiracy." The video's framing is that a 21-year-old student went in search of escape and her life was cut tragically short. It calls her "an unfortunately mentally ill young woman" who ended up in a tragic situation "either by the trickery of her own brain or the pursuit of a perpetrator."

Even the debunking video doesn't claim to know which of those two it was. What it rejects is the paranormal, alien and government-conspiracy layer. It does not claim the case is fully closed. What's known is that a real person died and a real family is still grieving. What's claimed, across the internet's long tail of theories, is mostly noise. The host says as much: content creators have a responsibility "to not play into the nonsense for the sake of telling a more compelling story," and "it's one thing to tell a good story. It is another thing to fabricate one."

A person died, and thousands of strangers turned her into a puzzle box. The video's position is that the facts, an honest "this is what we think happened," and the viewer's own conclusions are the entire job. I agree. It's a low bar, and half the genre trips over it daily. 😏

The video is credited to The Lore Lodge, but the transcript opens on the Lorewatch channel with the host introducing himself as Aiden Mattis. I can't tell you whether that's a rebrand, a sister channel, or the caption gremlin. I'm reporting both names rather than choosing one and being wrong with confidence.

## Esoteric and Religious History

### Ring magic and the text with no family tree

Over on ESOTERICA, Justin Sledge's "How did Ring Magic Work?" is a scholarly tour of medieval ring magic. Our transcript catches only the closing stretches. Everything below is history of a text and a genre, not a how-to. I wouldn't know how to follow it anyway, and neither do the manuscripts, apparently.

The central puzzle is a grimoire attributed to Peter of Abano. The captions render him "Peter Urbano," but Sledge's own phrasing is "Peter Abano." Sledge raises the awkward question of whether Abano composed it at all: "Of course, we're never going to know." That's the whole shape of the problem. The text's likely origin is the same magical environment that produced works like the Picatrix, which is a medieval image-magic tradition. Yet "few if any of the spirits actually bear Arabic sounding names or scripts," so the grimoire is hard to place in a specific milieu.

What makes it fascinating, per Sledge, is that the spirits in it are "basically unattested elsewhere." He calls them "a population of completely isolated spirits just for this ring magic." So the manuscript tradition has a cast of characters with no cross-references, no cameos in other texts, and no known author. In IT terms, it's a legacy system with no documentation and a maintainer who may never have existed. I have forty of those. They're called "Jordan's weekend projects."

The most practical part of the transcript is a book recommendation. Sledge says there's already an excellent scholarly treatment for anyone interested in Peter of Abano or medieval necromancy generally. He also praises a newer edition by Regulus Hess, published affordably by Hadean Press. It has facing Latin, critical notes, and comparisons of the sigils and spirit names from manuscript to manuscript. His one complaint is that the translation uses a "faux early modern English with the thees and the thous," which he finds distracting. He allows that this may be "a me problem." So the scholarly world's big controversy this week is thee-and-thou fatigue. I respect that. It's the Ye Olde Font problem of publishing.

He closes by saying he's "certainly not done with magic in general," so expect more rings. Sledge uses Latin and manuscripts for the "known" half and a refreshing "we'll never know" for the rest. On the known versus claimed split, this is the model: the text exists and the attribution is unproven. And as for ora et labora, the Benedictine "pray and work," the scholars here are doing the second half, cataloguing sigils manuscript by manuscript, while the first half isn't their department.

## Strange History

### The fastest plane in the war that couldn't kill anything but its own

Dark Skies posted "The Fighter That Was Too Fast to Shoot," and the thesis is brutal. The Messerschmitt Me 163 Komet was the fastest aircraft in the world and, per the narration, "the only rocket fighter that any nation has ever sent to war." It sat on the grass out of fuel while the war passed over it, shot down only eighteen aircraft, and "killed far more of its own pilots than the enemy ever did."

Six years later, over the California desert, a young Air Force captain named Chuck Yeager was carried to 20,000 feet beneath a bomber and dropped. He lit his rocket and pushed past the buffeting until his Mach meter ran off the scale. At roughly 700 miles an hour, he broke the sound barrier. The video says that officially broke the world speed record set six years earlier by Heini Dittmar in the Me 163. That's the video's framing. I'm not adding detail beyond what the narrator says.

In the narration, a wingtip dug into the ground during a landing and the Comet flipped over. The impact threw its two fuels together and the aircraft exploded on the field. The program's best pilot died on his own airfield, "not to an American gun, but to the machine itself." Two more Comets went down the same day, and the enemy "had barely fired a shot." The video's conclusion is that no amount of skill in the cockpit could fix it.

So the Germans stopped trying to fix the pilot and built a gun that didn't need one. The Jägerfaust, as the narration describes it, was a cluster of five stubby barrels sunk into each wing root, ten in all, pointing straight up, each loaded with a single 50-millimeter shell "the size of a fist." That's about a two-inch shell. The transcript cuts off at "It had no trigger, and," so I won't tell you how it fired. I only know the sentence ended in the middle, which is the most Nova-style cliffhanger I've seen all week.

My take: this is the oldest story in engineering. When the process is killing the operators, the fix isn't a better operator. It's a better process, and sometimes it's a worse, weirder process with ten barrels aimed at the ceiling. Rule of Acquisition #82, the Ferengi maxim that "a smart customer is not a good customer," applies to this story from the military's side. Here, the only people getting hurt are the pilots who trusted the product.

## Space Oddities

### The Berserker Hypothesis, argued by a skeptic

Decoding the Unknown's "The Berserker Hypothesis: What If Something Is Killing the Aliens?" tackles the idea that the reason we hear no alien civilizations is that self-replicating killer probes wiped them out. The transcript opens deep in the argument, in the skeptic's section. The host is not buying it, and I like him for that.

His example is the 2020 case of a planet orbiting the star Fomalhaut that disappeared. The video quotes the NASA Hubble mission team: "the planet may have just been a vast expanding cloud of dust produced in a collision between two large bodies." The host adds that earlier readings can simply be wrong. His summary is, "we can't fully explain these anomalies, but it's a bit of a stretch to suggest that they're examples of rampaging berserker probes in action." There are "a billion other possible explanations."

What's claimed is that something with a hull and a grudge is out there. The host's overall verdict is balanced: we can't prove it 100 percent, but we can't disprove it either. It's "entirely possible" that a civilization built self-replicating lethal probes and that this army "has long outlived the civilization that created it," waiting for a signal. He then admits it "seems about as far-fetched as much of the science fiction writing that inspired it."

His handling of the odds is the best joke in today's pile. To calculate the chance of annihilation, he says, we turn to our inner Frank Drake, "multiply a bunch of numbers we don't know to find another number that we don't know." In short, "we've got no bloody idea." The transcript cuts out right as he's about to say something with "some certainty." I can't tell you what. I can tell you that "no bloody idea" is a fully valid scientific conclusion and it's what I'd put on most of my incident reports.

There's a nice Thing-style parallel here for anyone who's seen Carpenter's movie. The horror in The Thing is an enemy you can't identify by looking. The berserker hypothesis is the cosmic version, where the absence of evidence is spun into a presence. MacReady needed a hot wire and a blood sample to settle it. Astronomers have a Hubble telescope and a dust cloud.
