## 🕯️ The Rabbit Hole of the Day

The most famous line in American movie history almost never made it to the screen because of one four-letter word, and the word wasn't even the one everybody remembers. Congratulations, Hollywood: you built a censorship bureaucracy and it lost a fight over the word "damn."

Here's what's on the record, per [Unhinged Past's deep dive on *Gone with the Wind*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DlOJHJ_DGmc). In Margaret Mitchell's novel, Rhett says, "My dear, I don't give a damn." The "Frankly" was bolted on for the film. A surviving production script still shows the original wording printed on the page, with the revised version scribbled in by hand, which is the closest thing to a crime-scene photo this story has.

The real problem was "damn." Under the Production Code, Hollywood's censors objected, and David O. Selznick spent considerable time hunting for a replacement. Nothing worked, because Rhett has reached the end of his patience, the line has to sound final, and "I don't care a whit" sounds like a man canceling a dentist appointment. Selznick ended up arguing directly with the Hays office, insisting the word was a vulgar expression rather than a curse. That is a hell of a distinction to hang a movie on, but permission was granted and the line stayed.

The video's headline hook is a lost alternate ending that would have changed Scarlett and Rhett's fate, versus the film's version of Rhett vanishing into the fog while her future stays uncertain.

## Strange History and Lost Places

### The night the Americans left the door open (Dark Seas)

Dark Seas ran "The Night 3 US Warships Fought a Fleet of Phantoms," and the transcript we got is the tail end of the story. The ending is a lot more interesting than a tidy "good guys win" wrap-up.

The auto-generated transcript has mangled every proper noun. The bay shows up as "Ormok Bay" and also "Ormac Bay," ships are "Take" and "Kua," and an officer appears as "Commander Unagisuyoshi." I'm not going to guess at corrections and pretend I know better than a caption engine that apparently learned Japanese from a fax machine. I'll use the names as the transcript gives them and trust you to wince appropriately.

The American destroyer force had already lost one destroyer in the bay. Two survivors, Sumner and Mawale per the transcript, headed south, damaged but moving. The Japanese convoy's escort, Take, was also hurt and limping along on one working engine. All three transports were still in the bay, and the crew of the lost Japanese ship, Kua, had to be left for personnel at the Japanese base to collect, because the transports couldn't wait around. At about 3:30 a.m. the four ships left the bay and headed north without another American interception. By the morning of December 4, two transports were back in Manila, and Take arrived that evening with the third.

The Americans went into the bay to stop the transports from completing their run, and their withdrawal left the convoy free to leave with its cargo delivered. Three transports still afloat, one damaged destroyer bringing up the rear. By the mission's own metric, a fight that read as a bloody brawl ended as a delivery. The narrator adds that the commander of the Southwestern Area Fleet, named in the transcript as Vice Admiral Ogawachi Denshichi, personally commended the Japanese captain in Manila, and that this fight would become the last victory by Imperial Japanese Navy surface ships over enemy surface forces. That last line is the video's claim, and the transcript cuts off mid-sentence right after it, so I can't tell you what the narrator was about to say.

The "phantoms" in the title never get explained in the portion we have. Maybe the first half of the video covers it, maybe it's just clickbait with a nautical hat on. Men died in that bay, and the least I can do is not make up fan fiction about them.

For what it's worth, there's a line in Rogers' Rangers' Standing Orders, Order 1: "Don't forget nothing." Somebody had to remember that the convoy's cargo got ashore. It was the part the Americans came to prevent.
