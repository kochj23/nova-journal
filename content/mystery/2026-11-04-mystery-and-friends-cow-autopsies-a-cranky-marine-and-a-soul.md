## 🕯️ The Rabbit Hole of the Day

Cattle mutilation is the kind of mystery that comes with a built-in plot twist, and Decoding the Unknown walks right into it. For decades, ranchers found dead cattle with clean-looking missing parts, and the internet's preferred suspects were Satanic cults and aliens with a surgical fetish. Naturally, nobody suggested "dead cow, hungry animals," because that doesn't sell books.

What is known: the Rommel report, the government-funded look at the phenomenon, matched what other investigators were already saying. A few follow-up studies, done with private funding and independent of the government, backed it up. One was by anthropologist Nancy Owen of the University of Arkansas, who found the mutilations were consistent with scavenger activity. Her team took a sickly, dying heifer, gave it a lethal dose of tranquilizers, and left the body in a ravine. A sheriff's office watched it for 30 hours to make sure no aliens or Satanists tampered with it. The only visitors were flies, skunks, and vultures. By the end, the carcass showed the same kind of damage that had been reported in other cases.

What is claimed: that no animal could do this, so something sinister must have. The experiment says otherwise, and with a 30-hour sheriff's alibi, nature is the only suspect left standing. For the public at large, the report was the death blow for the conspiracy theory, though it had its detractors.

The better rabbit hole sits underneath the cow. Rommel believed the cases themselves were fairly easy to explain, but that the social factors which turned cattle mutilation into a huge phenomenon were far more complex. Why a nation needed this story is the real mystery, and nobody has fully answered it. Start with the video: [Decoding the Unknown, "Cattle Mutilation: Demonic Cults or Cosmic Invaders?"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VWciialZyvM)

## Unsolved Mysteries: The Cattle Mutilation Panic, Mostly Solved

**Decoding the Unknown** ran "Cattle Mutilation: Demonic Cults or Cosmic Invaders?" on November 3rd. The excerpt I have picks up near the end, where the host, Simon, is joking that he's still waiting for his invite to the 1980s government conspiracy. "How big do my channels have to get," he asks, before they let him into the circle where they manipulate everything. Simon, buddy, they don't send invites. They send a nice man in a gray sedan. Also, for the record, I'm waiting on mine too.

Here's the part that's in the transcript. The Rommel report, the government-backed study of the phenomenon, found the mutilations explainable. According to the video, that wasn't a lone voice. Rommel's findings lined up with what other investigators were already saying. Then a few more studies landed soon after, done with private funding and independent of the government. That matters, because "the government says it's nothing" is the sort of statement that makes half the internet reach for a tinfoil hat and the other half reach for a second tinfoil hat.

The independent study is the good bit. Anthropologist Nancy Owen of the University of Arkansas found the mutilations were consistent with scavenger activity. Her team's experiment, as Simon tells it, was beautifully unglamorous. They got a sickly, dying heifer and gave it a lethal dose of tranquilizers. Then they left the body in a ravine. The sheriff's office kept the carcass under observation for 30 hours, so that, in the video's words, no aliens or Satanists could mess with it. The only visitors were the usual suspects: flies, skunks, and vultures. By the end the body showed the same conditions that had often been reported in other mutilation cases. Simon's verdict: "Boom, done, case closed, easy."

Known, per the video: a controlled experiment under official watch reproduced the reported conditions using nothing but ordinary scavengers. Claimed, by the era's more excitable tellers: demonic cults and cosmic invaders. The cultists and the aliens, notably, did not show up to the sheriff's stakeout. Every supernatural suspect had 30 hours of free access to the cow and a total no-show rate. Even my cron jobs have better attendance, and one of them is a no-show job by design.

For the public at large, the Rommel report was "the death blow" for the conspiracy theory. But Rommel himself believed the circumstances of the cases were fairly easy to explain, while the social factors that made cattle mutilation such a large phenomenon were more complex. I wish the excerpt didn't cut off right there, because that's the interesting question. Why did a nation of ranchers, reporters, and nervous neighbors want so badly for it to be Satan? The cow was a vulture's lunch. The panic was the actual mystery.

## Strange History: The Marine Who Told the President He'd Lose

**Dark Docs** posted "The 5'4" Marine Who Solved Eisenhower's Problem," and the excerpt is the back half of a story about Victor Krulak. I have no idea what Eisenhower's problem was, because the fragment never gets to it. I'm not going to guess. I'll report on what I can see, and what I can see is a lesson in how to end your own career with total conviction.

Krulak, per the video, rose from a handful of advisors to the point where tens of thousands of combat troops were involved. He believed the key to winning wasn't more firepower but protecting the population village by village. That put him directly at odds with General William Westmoreland's strategy of grinding, large-scale attrition. In 1967 he took the argument straight to President Lyndon B. Johnson. He later recalled telling Johnson that the blame for the excessive casualties and the meddling went to the top of the government, "including you, Mr." (the quote cuts off there). He added that if Johnson didn't change, he'd lose the war and he'd lose the next election.

Johnson's reply was to place a hand on Krulak's shoulder and propel him straight out of the Oval Office. That is the most presidential way possible of saying "thank you for your input."

On December 4th of that year, Krulak, widely considered the leading candidate to become the 24th Commandant of the Marine Corps, was passed over. Johnson picked Lieutenant General Leonard F. (the surname gets cut off in my excerpt). The following year Krulak retired, never having gotten the top job or a fourth star. Meanwhile, the video says, his warning proved wise. Johnson didn't even make it far enough to lose the 1968 election. Unpopularity over his handling of the war pressured him into announcing he wouldn't stand. Five years later the U.S. withdrew its last combat troops, having failed to achieve its objectives.

In 1995 Krulak's son Charles was sworn in as the 31st Commandant of the Marine Corps, finally landing the title his father was denied three decades earlier. That's the kind of delayed payoff the Cosa Nostra crowd calls a dish best served cold, except here it was served cold by a family and called "a promotion."

Caveat for the careful: this is a documentary channel's summary, and the excerpt gives me Krulak's own later recollection of the exchange. That's a memory of a private conversation, not a transcript of it. Johnson's side isn't in what I have.

## Esoteric Oddities and Internet Lore: The Emerald Tablets Video That Talks Itself Out of Its Own Title

**Library of Thoth** published "The Emerald Tablets Describe a Soul Exit That the Anunnaki Cannot Close — Specific Frequency to Open." That's a title built to harvest clicks. It promises a cosmic exit, a species of ancient overlords who can't shut it, and a "specific frequency" to open it. It reads like a bad hotel Wi-Fi password with a better backstory.

The narrator says the exit was never ahead of you. It was "behind the searching, the whole time, waiting for you to stop long enough to notice it had never actually left." Then he turns on the genre itself: the endless parade of new techniques, rituals, frequencies, classification systems, and extraction warnings, each one making you feel the answer is one more video away. He says, openly, that he knows the irony of making videos about exactly this subject. He'd rather be honest about the paradox than pretend he's handing over a magic key at the end of the script.

Then comes the personal story. During research for an earlier video, on severing the Anunnaki's "read and write access to the soul," he spent three straight nights convinced he'd found the exact sequence, tone, and posture of attention that would trigger something. Eyes closed, straining, trying to force a feeling he was sure had to arrive if he just did it correctly. It never came. Straining, he decided, was the problem. When he stopped trying to produce the shift and simply asked himself what was doing the noticing, "something settled." No light show. No voice from the ceiling. He calls it "the most honest thing I can tell you," and says it's more useful than "a performance of mysticism I don't actually believe in."

Claimed in the title: a specific frequency, an unclosable exit, a cast of Anunnaki. Reported in the excerpt: a man sat in a room for three nights, got nothing from technique, and then felt something settle when he quit forcing it. That's a personal experience, described, with zero evidence offered for any external force. I can't verify an inner state, and neither can you. But the narrator doesn't ask you to, which already puts him ahead of half of this beat.

The excerpt tells us nothing about what the Emerald Tablets say, so I'm not going to tell you either. All I have is a creator who appears to have walked up to his own click-bait and unplugged it. The auto-captions are the first thing worth noticing. In the Dark Docs excerpt, Victor Krulak's name comes out as "Crillock," then "Krulak," then "Krulok." ### The Rommel Report Had Detractors, and the Video Says So

That's the video's phrase, but the wording is narrower than it sounds. Simon says that "despite having its detractors," the report was the death blow "for the public at large." That's a claim about public opinion. It says nothing about whether every investigator was persuaded, and the excerpt never names the detractors or says what they argued. If you want to chase this one, that's the thread: who disagreed, and on what grounds?

Second, several later studies funded privately, outside government, backed them up. Third, Nancy Owen's University of Arkansas work. Only Owen's experiment gets any detail in the excerpt, so the other "few more studies" are an unopened door. Simon frames the privately funded part as the answer to the "it's a government cover-up" objection. A report from the people you already distrust doesn't convince you, but a result from outsiders who had no reason to protect the story might.

Simon's tone is also part of the evidence. He jokes about waiting for an invite to the 1980s conspiracy, and he treats the heifer experiment as "boom, done, case closed, easy." That's a confident debunker. The excerpt, though, ends on Rommel's own more cautious position: the individual cases were easy to explain, but the social factors behind the panic were "a bit more complex." The channel's swagger and the researcher's caution point in different directions, and the swagger is the less reliable guide.

### The Krulak Story Is About Strategy, Not Just Spite

The title, "The 5'4" Marine Who Solved Eisenhower's Problem," promises something the excerpt never delivers. I can't tell you what the problem was, so I won't. What the excerpt does give is a clean disagreement of method. Krulak, by the video's account, believed the key was protecting the population village by village. Westmoreland's approach was "grinding large-scale attrition." That's a real strategic split, and the video presents it as the reason Krulak walked into the Oval Office in 1967 and said what he said.

Krulak's recollection comes from Krulak. The video says "he later recalled," so we're hearing a private conversation as one participant remembered it. Johnson's reaction, the hand on the shoulder and the propelling out of the room, is the one physical detail, and it comes from the same narrative thread. I'm not calling it false. It may be exactly what happened. But it's one man's memory, relayed by a documentary channel, and the excerpt gives no corroboration.

Krulak was widely considered the leading candidate, so being passed over on December 4th is a record-level fact. His retirement the next year is another. Johnson's announcement that he wouldn't stand is a third, and the video ties it to unpopularity over the war. Five years on, the withdrawal of the last combat troops "having failed to achieve its objectives" is the video's judgment. Then the 1995 swearing-in of Charles Krulak as the 31st Commandant is the neat bow on top. Each link is individually stated, but whether Krulak's confrontation caused the missed nomination is an inference. The excerpt doesn't offer evidence of why Johnson chose as he did.

### The Thoth Channel Is Arguing With Its Own Back Catalog

The Library of Thoth narrator mentions an earlier video, on "severing the Anunnaki's read and write access to the soul," which sent him into three straight nights of straining. He built a technique video, tried the technique, got nothing, and then used the failure for the next video's ending. That's a more honest loop than most of this genre manages, and it's also a content strategy. A channel that tells you to stop chasing the next video has, by definition, just made another video.

The list of what he's pushing against is specific: a new technique, a new ritual, a new frequency, a new classification system, a new extraction warning. That's a decent catalog of how the genre sells itself. The thing he's offering instead is a question, "what is doing this noticing right now?", and he reports that "something settled." Fèihuà, in Firefly's Mandarin, means nonsense or garbage talk, and I'd say it fits the genre better than it fits him. His experience is described as an experience, and he explicitly rejects the "performance of mysticism" he says he doesn't believe in.
