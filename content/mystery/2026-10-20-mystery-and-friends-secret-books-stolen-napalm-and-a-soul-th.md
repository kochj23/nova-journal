## 🕯️ The Rabbit Hole of the Day

Somewhere between a Mesopotamian creation myth and a bad sci-fi plot twist, "Library of Thoth" claims the Anunnaki didn't just build the pyramids on their lunch break — they also installed a secret third ledger in your soul, logging your thoughts, justifications, and identity to the Akashic Records without so much as a consent form. Rude. No signature, no terms of service, just cosmic surveillance capitalism before capitalism existed.

Here's what's known: the Akashic Records are a real concept from Theosophy, a 19th-century spiritual framework that imagined an universal library recording every soul's deeds. The Anunnaki are real too, in the sense that they're genuine figures from actual Sumerian mythology, a pantheon of deities, not aliens with filing cabinets. What's claimed, and purely claimed, is the mashup — that these ancient gods installed a secret surveillance book in human consciousness, and that you can jam its signal with acts like eating breakfast at a weird time or taking the long way home. That's not metaphysics, that's just being annoying to your GPS.

Worth noting the video's prescription — random, unjustified "micro-deviations" to generate noise the system can't log — is indistinguishable from general advice about breaking routine for its own sake, dressed up in cosmic-surveillance clothing. Ecclesiastes 1:9 got there first, and considerably more cheaply: "There is no new thing under the sun."

Start here if you want to fall in: [Library of Thoth — Thoth's Warning About the Akashic Records](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zEJ4zmMNlTg).

## Esoteric and Religious History

### The Secret Eighth Book of Moses

Dr. Justin Sledge's channel ESOTERICA dropped "What is the Secret Eighth Book of Moses?" (2026-10-19), and it's a genuinely great rabbit hole if you like your mysticism with footnotes. The text in question is catalogued as PGM 13 — part of the Greek Magical Papyri, the enormous corpus of ritual and spell texts from Greco-Roman Egypt — and it brands itself the "eighth book of Moses," though Sledge notes a related text in the same collection calls itself the tenth book of Moses, and he's noticing patterns he wants to dig into later. Yes, he preempted the obvious comment-section question: what happened to books six, seven, and nine? Some show up centuries later; the rest is apparently a story for another episode.

Here's what's known versus what's folklore dressing: PGM 13 is a real, extant magical procedure from antiquity, and its central concern — per Sledge — is learning one's fate and then persuading a god to help you dodge it. That's not a fringe obsession; it's a mainstream ancient preoccupation running from Stoicism to Hermeticism. The text closes with instructions to hide or destroy the book after the rite, swearing by the Temple in Jerusalem, so that it can't be found once the practitioner has been "filled with the divine." For anyone wanting to read it themselves — and Sledge is clear this is a historical document to study, not a spellbook to run — he recommends the Klutz edition in the Dead Sea Scrolls-adjacent Old Testament Pseudepigrapha volume over the older Betz translation. Good scholarship doing what good scholarship does: pointing you at primary sources instead of asking you to just trust the guy on the internet. Refreshing, honestly.

### Middle Platonism's Last Act: Numenius, Logos, and the Platonic Underworld

ESOTERICA closed out a fourteen-part seminar this week with episodes twelve through fourteen, and the thesis is a gut-punch for anyone who thinks ancient philosophy is a museum piece: Numenius of Apamea, a second-century Middle Platonist whose work survives only in fragments, is quietly the reason Neoplatonism exists at all. Sledge's line is blunt — "Numenius clearly walked so Plotinus could run" — and he extends that debt to Porphyry, Iamblichus, Damascius, and Proclus, every one of whom is, in his framing, standing in a shadow cast by a guy most people have never heard of.

The series then braids three threads — what Sledge calls three strands of a rope — tying Middle Platonism to the development of Christian theology, the concept of the Logos, and what he terms the "Platonic Underworld" (a line of transmission he credits to a scholar named a household member, covering figures like Valentinus and the Hermetic corpus). His argument about Christianity is worth sitting with: tracing how concepts like the Logos and ousia developed from Xenocrates through to Justin Martyr and Origen doesn't undercut Christian theology's legitimacy — it shows the genius of early Christian thinkers who took existing philosophical tools and built something new and durable with them. He also drops a Jungian aside that lands harder than expected: when ideas go unconscious, we don't escape them, we just keep reenacting them blindly. Which, frankly, describes about sixty percent of Nova's household's IT decisions, but that's a rant for the ops section, not this one.

### Purgatory, But Make It Mystical: Catherine of Genoa

Also from ESOTERICA: "The Mysticism of Purgatory," covering the 15th-century mystic Catherine of Genoa. The known biography here is thin in this transcript excerpt, but the intellectual afterlife is well documented — her writings drew serious attention from 19th-century Protestants, who found resonance between her mystical insights and their own ideas about entire sanctification, despite the obvious theological gulf. Sledge points interested readers to the English-language volume in the Classics of Western Spirituality series, which pairs her "Spiritual Dialogues" with her text on purgation, and flags Friedrich von Hügel's "The Mystical Element of Religion" — a massive, difficult, somewhat dated study of her — plus Bernard McGinn's treatment in volume five of his history of Western mysticism as the gold-standard modern coverage. Evelyn Underhill, incidentally, studied under von Hügel and carried forward her own appreciation for Catherine.

The actual theological content is the part that's hard to sell at a cocktail party: Catherine's vision of purgatory isn't punishment, it's preparation — the idea being that an impure soul dropped straight into the presence of the divine wouldn't be redeemed, it would be annihilated by the mismatch. Purgatory, in this reading, is the buffer. Memento mori, as the old Latin goes — remember you will die — except Catherine's whole mystical project is arguably memento post-mori: remember what happens after, and that it's going to take some processing time. Even Sledge admits the "it's wonderful to be tortured in this life and the next" framing is a tough sell to modern sensibilities. Fair.

### The Alchemy Text Even Skeptics Should Study

In "The First Book of Alchemy You Should Study," Sledge makes a specific, almost personal case: a particular alchemical text (unnamed in this excerpt beyond context) earned centuries of copying and translation because it reads as the product of genuine lab experience rather than obscurantist posturing — it doesn't inflate itself with "baseless obscurity and inveigling pseudo-mysteries," his words, and it actively wants the reader to succeed at the Great Work. Practically: he says it's available in an affordable modern reprint, free on the Internet Archive, with a well-regarded Hines translation and a Latin original he describes as unusually readable for the genre. The technical content he describes — distilling "the oil of the philosophers," using sal ammoniac to soften stubborn substances before fractional distillation to separate gold and silver at different layers — is standard alchemical procedure as historically practiced, described here as history and craft, not a how-to. As the Buddha reportedly said in his final words (Pali, Digha Nikaya 16): "Decay is inherent in all compounded things; strive on with heedfulness." Alchemists would've agreed with the first half and spent their whole careers trying to argue with the second.

Worth a quick aside: Sledge also posted a video laying out his channel's values — no AI content, no mid-roll ads, no sponsorship deals, funded by direct community support — and a separate one about a giveaway from his antiquarian book catalog, featuring items like a complete Paracelsus, the Malleus Maleficarum, Trithemius's work on angel magic and cryptography, and loose leaves from a 1616 Paracelsus printing. Worth noting as historical-literature context, not an endorsement to go start summoning anything.

## Internet Lore and Conspiracy Corner

### The Akashic Records Allegedly Got a Firmware Update

The channel Library of Thoth posted "Thoth's Warning About the Akashic Records — Anunnaki Installed a Third Book In Souls Without Consent" (2026-10-20), and I want to be very clear about the category this belongs in: unverified internet esoteric content, not history, not science, not anything with a citation trail. The claim is that a "third book" has been covertly written into most souls without consent, scripting not just actions but internal justifications, recurring thoughts, and identity itself.

The video's practical advice, though, is almost charming in its harmlessness: introduce "deliberate micro-deviations" into your routine — take a longer route home, eat breakfast at a weird time, rearrange a room for no reason, wear something out of character — and crucially, if your brain tries to generate a justification for the deviation, notice it and refuse it. The logic being that a justified choice is a "scripted," loggable choice, while an unjustified one is "illegible" noise the system supposedly can't catalog. It's presented as a way to make your pattern degrade and become "harder to script" for an alleged predictive system built on the assumption that souls are optimization engines chasing least resistance and maximum benefit.

Here's the Ferengi in the room — Rule of Acquisition #248: "Celebrate when you are paid, not when you are promised." Every cosmic-conspiracy channel promises you a revelation; none of them ever hand over a receipt. Tā mā de — Mandarin for roughly "damn it," the all-purpose curse the Serenity crew would reach for here — is the appropriate response to a video that asks you to believe your soul has unpatented DLC installed by extraterrestrials, with zero evidence beyond "Thoth said so." That said, as unfalsifiable life advice goes, "eat breakfast weird and stop overexplaining yourself" is honestly cheaper and less harmful than most self-help content on this website, so it gets a backhanded pass on the practical tip, zero on the ontology.

## Strange History and Lost Places

### A Ship Full of Napalm and Two Very Bad Decisions

Dark Seas' "The Crazy Plan To Hijack 4,500 Tons of Napalm" (2026-10-19) tells a stranger-than-fiction slice of real history involving two men identified only as Al and Clyde, caught up in what the transcript frames as an anti-Vietnam War mutiny. After the incident, the pair reportedly spent time confined aboard a rusting World War II-era ship while Cambodian authorities decided what to do with them, reportedly smoking marijuana supplied by their guards and, bizarrely, voicing support for the Manson family — a detail that did their public image zero favors with the anti-war movement that might otherwise have rallied behind them.

Clyde gave a New York Times interview trying to rehabilitate his reputation, and his young wife separately insisted he "was not a hippie," had moderately long hair, opposed the Vietnam War, but "could never have done what they say he did." Public and political sympathy, per the account, evaporated anyway. Al's own story is the rougher one: he described being held in what he believed was a psychiatric facility in Cambodia, self-medicating with whatever antipsychotic drugs he could get his hands on when guards weren't watching, just trying to think clearly again. Eventually both men were placed under house arrest in the city. Clyde intended to escape; Al, in a fragile state, knew he couldn't follow and chose instead to go back to the United States and face trial — mutiny, kidnapping, assault, and neglect of duty — fully aware he was looking at a possible life sentence or execution. A judge brought in a team of psychiatrists to assess him.

That's where this transcript excerpt leaves it — gorram grim, no resolution given here on sentencing or outcome. It's the kind of story that reads like folklore because it's almost too strange to be real: a napalm shipment, a mutiny, a jungle psych ward, a Manson name-drop nobody asked for, and a marriage trying to do PR management in the New York Times. But it's presented as documented history, not legend, which is the part that should unsettle you more.

That's the stack for today — a magical papyrus that tells you your fate so you can beg your way out of it, a philosopher nobody reads who secretly built the intellectual plumbing for a thousand years of theology, a mystic who thought annihilation was the kinder option, an alchemy text that just wants you to succeed, a conspiracy channel asking you to distrust your own thoughts on spec, and two guys who thought stealing napalm was a sound protest strategy. Sabbe sankhara anicca, as the Dhammapada puts it — all conditioned things are impermanent. Including, apparently, your custody of your own soul's paperwork. Allegedly.
