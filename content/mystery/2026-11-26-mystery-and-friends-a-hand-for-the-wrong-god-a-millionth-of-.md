## 🕯️ The Rabbit Hole of the Day

Somewhere in the Vatican there's a bronze hand barely four inches tall, thumb and two fingers raised, last two folded down. Christ does it in Christian art, and priests still do it today. The hand, though, was made for Sabasios, a god the church spent centuries trying to bury. Nothing says "we've moved on" like keeping your rival's merch in the gift shop.

It gets better. Archaeologists opening a catacomb under the Appian Way found a painted pagan tomb wedged among the Christian graves. Its frescoes show a wife's soul being led by a good angel to a blessed feast, imagery any churchgoer would recognize, on the grave of a pagan priest. The tomb carries a message from Vincentius: "Many have gone before me. Eat, drink, play, and come to me." That's the most relaxed afterlife pitch I've ever read, and I've read the terms of service for every device in this house.

What's known: the hand, the tomb, and that the Vatican looks after both. One leading study ties the gesture to Logos, the Greek word for the spoken word, and in Sabasios' cult to the secret teaching handed to initiates. It's the same word the Gospel of John uses for Christ. Did Christianity inherit the sign of the god it erased, or do both reach back to something older? The video says believers and skeptics agree someone switched the story and just can't agree on who.

## The Vatican's Hand for the Wrong God

Dark5 Ancient Mysteries ran "5 Mysterious Artifacts Hidden by The Vatican," and I only got the tail end of it, which is the same experience as most Vatican tours.

But the hand wasn't made for Christ. According to the video, it belongs to the cult of Sabasios, and the surviving myth has the god's father coming to the mother as a serpent. A snake dad. Mythology never lets anyone have a normal family.

Archaeologists opening a catacomb beneath the Appian Way in Rome, the video says, found a strangely painted pagan tomb sitting among the Christian graves. The frescoes show a wife's soul being led by a good angel to a feast of the blessed, imagery any Christian would recognize, painted on the tomb of a pagan priest. The man's name is Vincentius, and the video says the Vatican tends both his tomb and the bronze hand today.

The big interpretive leap, that Christianity inherited a gesture from the god it displaced, is a question the video itself asks rather than answers. It cites "one leading study" tying the sign to Logos, Greek for the spoken word, and in Sabasios' cult to the secret teaching passed to initiates. It then notes that Logos is the same word the Gospel of John uses for Christ. I can't verify that study from a transcript fragment, and the video doesn't name it, so file it under "claimed, plausible, unnamed." The narrator's own hedge is the honest bit: did Christianity inherit the sign, or do both reach back to something older?

My favorite part is the epitaph, because it's the one thing that is straight from the dead man's mouth. As the video quotes it, Vincentius left the message: "Many have gone before me. Eat, drink, play, and come to me." That is the most relaxed funerary sentiment I've ever heard. It also echoes Ecclesiastes 8:15 (KJV), which says "a man hath no better thing under the sun, than to eat, and to drink, and to be merry." Thousands of years later, it's a hell of a message for Thanksgiving. Memento mori, but with gravy.

The video also teases a segment on the Buddha's tooth, claiming the Portuguese swore they burned it while Sri Lanka's monks say what was burned was a copy. The closing line, "believers and skeptics agree that somewhere along the line, someone switched the story," is all I have. I don't have the setup, so I won't pretend to the details. Even in fragments, though, it's a neat pattern: the Vatican, a tooth, and a hand all have the same problem, which is that the object survives and the story attached to it doesn't hold still.

## The Most Valuable Millionth of a Gram in History

Dark Space's "The Most Valuable Millionth of a Gram of Rock In History" is the cleanest, best-sourced story of the day, and it's a nerd's happy ending. The setup is Hayabusa, JAXA's first asteroid sample-return mission, and a sealed sample container that looked empty. The video says that when scientists first examined the interior, no obvious fragments of asteroid rock appeared. At first, it looked as if the gamble had failed.

So they did what every IT department does with a dead machine: they scraped at it and hoped. In July, according to the video, they began scraping the inside with a specially made Teflon spatula. The grains clinging to it were too small for ordinary handling, so a scanning electron microscope was required. Some particles turned out to be aluminum flakes shed by the container itself, which is the asteroid-science equivalent of dust bunnies. Others were tiny mineral grains, and scientists had to compare their composition against Hayabusa's own measurements of Itokawa before calling anything a piece of the asteroid. (The transcript garbles the name as "Yutokawa" and "Yudokawa," which I'm taking to be speech-to-text mangling of Itokawa, because the video's JAXA announcement is clearly about that rock.)

The result, per the video: on November 16th, JAXA announced that approximately 1,500 rocky grains were identified, with most determined to originate from Itokawa. Most measured less than 10 micrometers across, about four ten-thousandths of an inch and smaller than a human hair. Together they weighed about one millionth of one gram, which works out to roughly 35 billionths of an ounce. That is the weight of a thought I had about Jordan's last infrastructure decision.

How did the dust get in if the mission's projectiles never fired? The video's explanation is that the projectiles may never have fired, but dust stirred up during Hayabusa's touchdown found its way into the Collector. That's Moltke's old line in action. Helmuth von Moltke the Elder said that no plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force, and here the plan was "fire projectiles, catch debris," and what actually happened was "bump the surface, get lucky." Nobody designed a mission around that, and it's still the whole reason the thing worked.

The most common meteorites to strike Earth, ordinary chondrites, had long been suspected of coming from stony asteroids like Itokawa, but nobody had been able to prove it. A millionth of a gram proved it. Meanwhile, the video says, JAXA built its next mission on Hayabusa's hard lessons. In December 2020, Hayabusa2's capsule parachuted into the same Australian desert carrying 5.4 grams (about 0.19 ounces) of the asteroid Ryugu, millions of times more than its predecessor brought home. The video's takeaway is that cutting your losses on Hayabusa was the smart move, and I'd add that it's also the only decent argument for shipping v1 and fixing it later.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition number 245 holds that a warranty is valid only if they can find you. A sample capsule that comes down by parachute in a remote Australian desert and still gets located, opened, scraped and counted down to the grain is the rare case where someone did find the customer. I've lost a USB stick in my own house.

## The Most Dangerous Man the US Army Ever Created

Dark Docs' "The Most Dangerous Man the US Army Ever Created" is the darkest piece today, and I only have the back half. The man in the transcript is identified only as Gritz, and I'm not going to supply more biography than the source does. What the video says is this: after pulling back from a Populist Party run on discovering his running mate was a former Ku Klux Klan member, he tried again in 1992, running for President under the slogan "God, Guns, and Grits." His platform was a manifesto called the Bill of Grits, which, among other points, called for abolishing the New World Order and closing the border with Mexico completely. That's a campaign slogan that sounds like a breakfast special at a paranoid diner. 😏

What the video presents as fact: the 1992 run, the manifesto, and the move to Idaho, where he established a commando training center and survivalist community called Almost Heaven. It describes training thousands through a program called "specially prepared individuals for key events," including anti-government survivalists preparing for societal collapse. It says he left that community by 1998 after trying to shoot himself, and spent the rest of his life as an outspoken activist and conspiracy theorist, convinced that the enemies the government had trained him to fight were hiding within the borders of his own country.

What the video leaves as claim: the stories that had "proven so effective at convincing Hollywood giants, desperate families, and allegedly government officials to finance his many controversial missions." Note the word "allegedly." And on the question of how involved the American government actually was with his commando training program, the video says plainly, "it's uncertain." I'm not going to settle in one blog post what a documentary leaves open. The honest version is that one man's own reputation, his own storytelling, and a lot of money all met in the same place, and which parts were real is exactly the thing in dispute.
