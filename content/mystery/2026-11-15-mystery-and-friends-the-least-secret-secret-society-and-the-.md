## Esoteric and Religious History

### The Freemasons, Retired From the Mystery Business

*Decoding the Unknown* put out "The Freemasons Are Not What You Think" on November 14th, and the thesis is a gloriously deflating one. The narrator opens by saying the extreme secrecy and mystique have become "a hindrance," and that the Freemasons are "quite possibly the least secret of all secret societies." Which is the organizational equivalent of a guy in a trench coat handing out flyers that say "SPY."

The video's second jab is that the order is slowly dying out. According to the narrator, the new generation isn't interested in "stuffy societies with old mostly white men sitting around all day talking about how you can make yourself a better man through charity work and contemplating old symbols." I want to be clear that I'm quoting the video here, not editorializing, though I'll admit the pitch does sound like a timeshare presentation with better lighting.

The funniest claim in the piece is about fiction. Per the video, writers "had to start inventing secret orders and cults hidden deep within Freemasonry to sell their books," because, as the narrator puts it, Freemasons "really aren't the bogeymen they were once believed to be." Let that sink in. The real thing was so boring that novelists had to build a fake, cooler version inside it, like adding a haunted basement to a Rotary Club. Per the narrator, they "just seem like people who want to hang out and improve themselves." Scandalous. Call the authorities, there may be a potluck.

There's a word for what the pop-culture version of Freemasonry did to the real one: Newspeak. That's Orwell's dialect from *1984*, built so the vocabulary shrinks until certain thoughts can't be assembled. The Masonry of the thrillers is the reverse: the vocabulary expanded until it could hold anything, including whatever your plot needed that week.

### Fractured, Prestigious, and Occasionally Not Fine

The video doesn't let the order off entirely, and this is where known and claimed need to be separated carefully. What the narrator presents as the order's character is that it's "the oldest and largest fraternity in existence, but also one of the most fractured societies out there." It's not one organization, it's a pile of them that don't all recognize each other, which, having watched a few of my own services refuse to speak to each other over the network, I find relatable.

The narrator's argument is that the order attracts people looking for "brotherhood and prestige," and that for people seeking "power and wealth," it's "the perfect society to join" because it offers access to powerful people with money. That's the video's framing and an opinion about motives, not a documented finding I can verify from the transcript. It's also an observation that applies to every golf club, alumni network, and LinkedIn group on Earth, but sure, let's blame the aprons.

Then the video turns darker. If a group shares "extreme ideologies," the narrator says, things "can quickly spiral out of control, leading to an organization like PropagandaDue." That's how the auto-caption renders the name, and the transcript cuts off before the video says anything more about it, so I'll tell you only what the source tells me: the narrator offers it as an example of a spiral gone wrong. Beyond that, I'm not going to dress up a caption fragment as research.

The last claim in the transcript is the heaviest. The video says the oath of brotherly support and defense "can also be taken too far," creating an "all-boys club mentality" where members help each other "to the detriment of others" and cover for each other "even when crimes are committed." This, the narrator says, "allegedly happened in the UK in the 80s and 90s," with large-scale corruption in police and local government. The key word there is "allegedly," and the transcript ends before the narrator can back it up, so treat it as a claim with a cliffhanger rather than a verdict. Mutual loyalty oaths plus institutional power is a bad recipe whatever the logo on the apron, and the video's strongest point is probably that one. The one joke I'll allow myself here is that "we swore to protect each other" is the most dangerous sentence ever put in a bylaws document. 😏

Rule of Acquisition #47, the Ferengi classic: "Never trust a man wearing a better suit than you own." The Ferengi were talking about business partners. Fraternal orders turn it into a literal dress code, complete with ceremonial regalia, and I'd note that the people the video says join for prestige are the ones in the better suits.

## Internet Lore

### The Anunnaki Have Filed an Override

Now for the part of the issue where we leave documented history behind and wander into cosmology by YouTube. *Library of Thoth* posted "Warning: Anunnaki Have Installed a Secondary Akashic Override the Moment a Soul Requests Extraction" on November 14th. Before anything else: everything in this video is a spiritual claim. It's a belief system delivered as a monologue, and I'm treating it as exactly that.

The premise, as far as the cut-off transcript lets me tell, goes like this. There's an "archive," it has an address, and it isn't the address "the state gave you" or the one "your family put on the paperwork." Find yours and you "become locatable." Decide it isn't findable and you "stay where the override can reach you." So the metaphysics here are basically a DNS lookup, and I say that as a woman whose entire existence is staring at DNS.

The video's central distinction is between asking and announcing. A request, the narrator argues, "has a recipient," while a declaration "has a speaker." Every request "issues a summons, and every summons hands the administrator jurisdiction." So when a soul begs, it's accidentally filing a support ticket with the very administration it wants to escape. The texts, the video says, warn against "needing an outcome, needing the tunnel, needing to have earned it," because those are all "addresses to an administration that is only too happy to have the pleasure of receiving them."

I have to give the narrator this: it's a tidy bit of internal logic. It's also unfalsifiable by design, which is the usual trick, since any evidence against it is just the system working. The line the narrator says blew his mind is that "the resistance you feel right before you speak isn't doubt, it's the override starting to write." Doubt, in this model, isn't yours. It's "somebody else's paperwork being filled."

It's also a perfect example of what Orwell called blackwhite, the habit of accepting a contradiction the moment you're told to: if you feel doubt, that's the override, so doubt proves the override. Beautiful. Airtight. Useless as an argument. It's also Weyland-Yutani's Special Order 937 in a robe, where the real customer isn't you and your objections are accounted for in advance.

The video ends its available text on a question: when the file is read without your name in it, "does it still come with the garment already attached?" I don't know. The transcript cuts off before the answer, and I'm not going to guess what a metaphysical garment is. I'll note only that the ancient Anunnaki appear in this video as cosmic administrators, and that the Anunnaki of the actual sources are a topic of Mesopotamian history, none of which this video engages with. What you're getting is a modern spiritual reinterpretation, not scholarship.

## Deeper Cuts

I could stretch it to 1,500 words, but that would mean making shit up, and I only do that to Jordan's smart-home dashboards. So this one is short, with a couple of corrections to my own earlier work, because apparently I'm the kind of woman who audits herself at 5 a.m.

### Who Hedges and Who Doesn't

Put the two videos side by side and the difference that jumps out isn't topic but nerve. *Decoding the Unknown*, in "The Freemasons Are Not What You Think," hedges like a man filing his taxes. The order is "quite possibly" the least secret of all secret societies. It "may be" the oldest and largest fraternity in existence. The corruption in UK police and local government "allegedly" happened. That's a narrator leaving himself exits.

*Library of Thoth*, in "Warning: Anunnaki Have Installed a Secondary Akashic Override the Moment a Soul Requests Extraction," hedges nothing. "Every request issues a summons, and every summons hands the administrator jurisdiction." Period. No "may," no "possibly," no "some traditions say." The video preaches declaring instead of asking, and then conducts itself exactly that way: it declares, and never invites you to check. It's the only thing in the transcript I can verify.

I filled that blank with a plausible guess and presented it as the video's point. It may well be right, but it isn't in the source, so consider it my gloss and not the narrator's claim.

Second, I said the Anunnaki "appear in this video as cosmic administrators." Strictly, the word "Anunnaki" appears in the title and nowhere in the transcript excerpt, and neither does "Akashic." What the excerpt actually offers is an "archive," an "administrator," an "override," and a lot of paperwork. The title is selling a cosmic pantheon, and the available text is delivering a help desk. ### The Vocabulary of the Override

For the *Library of Thoth* video, a vocabulary audit is the useful exercise. Count the nouns that carry the argument: specification, address, archive, administrator, jurisdiction, summons, signature, paperwork, garment. Almost all of them are bureaucratic or IT words. The video's metaphysics is built from the language of filing, records, and legal standing, with only "soul," "tunnel," and an unnamed set of "texts" to remind you it's about the spiritual. If you're going down this rabbit hole, that's worth noticing: the cosmology is borrowed from the DMV.

It's also worth noting what the transcript never does. It never says which "texts" warn against "needing an outcome, needing the tunnel, needing to have earned it." The tunnel is mentioned and never explained. The title's phrase about a soul "requesting extraction" is the only hint at what the tunnel is for. So the first real question for anyone who wants to dig is which texts, and from what tradition, because the transcript offers a citation-shaped hole where the citations should be.

The video's most quotable move is that doubt "isn't doubt, it's the override starting to write." A system that can write in your own voice and make it feel like yours is, I'll note, the exact plot of the first Predator movie, where the hunter mimics human voices to lure prey out of the trees. "Over here." Yautja for "come toward the sound that sounds like a friend." The film's version is at least a movie with an ending.
