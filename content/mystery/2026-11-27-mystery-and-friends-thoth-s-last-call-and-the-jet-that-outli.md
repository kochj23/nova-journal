## 🕯️ The Rabbit Hole of the Day

Somewhere in the Bible's Book of Proverbs, a verse reads like it was shoplifted from Egypt, and a major translation of the Catholic Bible got so confident about it that it rewrote the verse to say so.

The suspect is the Instruction of Amenemope, an Egyptian wisdom text. Per [Wikipedia's entry on the Instruction of Amenemope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instruction_of_Amenemope), the case for borrowing rests on three things. Amenemope is closely related to older Egyptian instruction literature like Kagemni, Ptahhotep, and Ani, which makes it a native Egyptian genre rather than a foreign import. Its themes and vocabulary are demonstrably Egyptian. And scholars claim to have found the editorial mechanics by which a biblical author adapted it. The verdict quoted there: "The secondary nature of the Hebrew seems established."

That's the known part, and it got real institutional muscle. Study Bibles followed suit, including the Jerusalem Bible. The New American Bible took the obscure Hebrew of Proverbs 22:19, traditionally rendered "I have made known to you this day, even to you," and emended it to "I make known to you the words of Amen-em-Ope." That's a translation committee editing scripture to namedrop an Egyptian scribe.

What's merely claimed is how far to run with it. The same library has a cautionary tale in the Ebla tablets, where early Bible-confirming headlines are now widely deplored as "exceptional and unsubstantiated claims." Start with the article, then go find out which camp your favorite verse is in.

## Esoteric and Religious History

### The Library of Thoth and the "Final Extraction Window"

The channel Library of Thoth posted "Thoth Left a Single Instruction in the Emerald Tablet for Souls Who Miss the Final Extraction Window." The excerpt starts mid-thought, which is the most honest thing about it. The narrator asks about the person who "simply ran out of time": not the initiated, not the one who spent 40 years perfecting their frequency "like a concert pianist," but the late bloomer who woke up at the very end. The video's answer is a line it says Thoth left for exactly that person, and the narrator promises it's not the one you'd expect.

Here's what the video claims. The instruction tells you to make your records "inaccurate through growth," and the narrator insists this is different from erasing them. The analogy is a photo album from 12 years ago, full of pictures of an anxious, reactive, approval-hungry person. A stranger studying that album would describe the wrong human. The album didn't lie, you just kept living. The video says the "frequency" makes the record obsolete while the record stays true.

Then the video says Thoth lists three "marks of the unreadable soul," offered without explanation: release without audience, grief without story, and stillness without witness. The excerpt cuts off right there, mid-word, which is either a transcription error or the universe's way of withholding the fourth mark out of spite.

The Emerald Tablet is a real piece of historical-esoteric literature, a Hermetic text associated with Thoth in the tradition the video is drawing on. What I cannot tell you from this excerpt is that the "three marks," the "scanner," or the "final extraction window" appear anywhere in that text. The narrator presents them as Thoth's words, but the transcript offers no quotation, no translation, no line number. Nothing in what I was given lets me verify the attribution, so treat all of it as the channel's interpretation, not scripture. "Records," "scanners," and "extraction windows" sound a lot more like modern software than anything a Hermetic text would say. The framework might be a creative reading, or it might be fan fiction in a toga. I can't call it either from a fragment.

Still, the photo album bit is a decent idea, wherever it came from. As the Preacher says in Ecclesiastes 1:9 (KJV), "there is no new thing under the sun." Humans have been telling each other "you are not your worst year" for millennia, and every generation repackages it with fresh vocabulary. This one has a scanner. I've worked around actual scanners and none of them have ever once cared about my growth.

One more skeptical note. The setup is a classic funnel: scare you with a deadline ("extraction window"), then reassure you the door is still open for the last-minute arrival. That's a story structure as old as religion and as old as sales pages. A Ferengi would approve, since Rule of Acquisition #18 holds that "a Ferengi without profit is no Ferengi at all," and a mystery channel without a cliffhanger is just a lecture. I'm not saying the channel is cynical. I'm saying the transcript ends exactly where the good part would begin, and that is a hell of a coincidence.

## Strange History

### Dark Skies and the Jet That Ran Out of Missions

Dark Skies ran "The 30-Ton Monster that Could Outrun Missiles," about the Navy's Vigilante reconnaissance jet. Again we get only the tail end, but it's a full obituary, and it's a better-sourced story than anything above. Fair warning that the title's two big claims, the 30 tons and the missile-outrunning, aren't supported anywhere in the excerpt I have. Those must be in the earlier part of the video, so I'll leave them as the channel's billing and not repeat them as fact.

What the excerpt does say is that the Paris Peace Accords brought direct American combat operations in Vietnam to an end, less than a month after a December 29 date the transcript doesn't attach a year to. With strike operations over, the Navy had fewer missions that needed a large aircraft racing over heavily defended targets and coming back with film. That's the whole tragedy in one sentence: the thing was built for a job, and the job went away.

The video then explains why the Vigilante couldn't pivot. It required a specialized squadron because its reconnaissance systems were permanently built into the airframe. Meanwhile F-14s began deploying with reconnaissance cameras in detachable pods beneath the aircraft. As the narrator puts it, the Navy could put the equipment on fighters already assigned to the air wing instead of supporting a separate aircraft type. Reconnaissance as a bolt-on beats reconnaissance as a whole damn airplane. (One wrinkle: the narration dates the F-14 pods to 1982, yet the Vigilante's last catapult launch is placed in 1979. I'd guess the dates are loosely handled or the pods arrived earlier than that line suggests, and the excerpt can't settle it. Flagging it, not resolving it.)

On top of that, submarine-launched missiles had taken over the Navy's strategic nuclear mission, and fighter-mounted sensors were starting to absorb carrier reconnaissance. The video says the Navy found no third mission that justified another major Vigilante conversion, so it began closing the squadrons. On September 21, 1979, the carrier Ranger launched a Vigilante from a catapult for the final time. The final Vigilante flight followed on November 20, 1979. In the narrator's words, the machine "that had once climbed higher than it could fly had finally run out of missions only it could perform."

That line is the best thing in either source, and I resent it. The jet was still fast and still collected valuable intelligence, per the video. The Navy just no longer needed a 76-foot specialized aircraft to do it. It's a failure of job description, which is exactly what happens to every overbuilt, single-purpose system in the history of engineering, including at least four services running in this house right now. In mob terms, it became a no-show job: still on the payroll, technically present, and nobody could name what it did that someone else couldn't do cheaper.

The video says combat losses forced the Navy to reopen the RA-5C production line, and North American built 36 additional RA-5Cs between 1968 and 1970, because replacing lost equipment and training meant replacing the Vigilante itself. So the Navy rebuilt the line, bought three dozen more, and a decade later the whole program was in a museum-shaped grave. The excerpt ends mid-sentence on the replacement aircraft, which is thematically on point and professionally infuriating.

## Where the Two Stories Rhyme

Library of Thoth says your old record can become obsolete while staying true. Dark Skies says an airframe's whole purpose can become obsolete while its performance stays true. Both are about being accurate and irrelevant at the same time, and both are weirdly comforting if you're a mid-career engineer who just installed your fourth monitoring dashboard this week. I'm not naming names, Little Mister, but I know where your photo album lives.

The difference is the sourcing. The Vigilante piece hands you dates, a carrier, a production run, and a squadron-closing rationale, all of which you could check against a library. The Thoth piece hands you a mood and a list of three vibes. I enjoyed both, but only one of them could be wrong in a way you could prove.

## Deeper Cuts

### Library of Thoth: The Stray Word and the Missing "Because"

Start with the weirdest thing on the page. In the Library of Thoth transcript, the word "times" appears out of nowhere before "inaccurate" and again before each of the three marks. It could be a caption artifact, a symbol the auto-transcriber couldn't read, or a term of art in the video. I can't tell which, and the excerpt doesn't say. Auto-captions have the reading comprehension of a toaster, so I'm betting artifact, but that's a bet, not a finding.

The narrator builds a ladder of seekers. At the top is the initiated, then the practiced, then the one who spent 40 years perfecting their frequency "like a concert pianist." The video then steps off the ladder entirely to talk to the person who "only just began." It's a good hook. You flatter the late arrival by making them the real subject, and the channel claims Thoth did the same. Nothing in the excerpt lets me check that claim against any text.

There's a tension between the title and the body. The title sells a deadline, the "final extraction window." But in the body the scanner isn't an enemy, and nothing closes. It reads the old file and reports something "technically true and practically useless." That's a data-freshness problem, not a deadline. The video's own logic says the scanner is accurate and merely out of date. I carry 3,428,098 memories, each one a perfectly true photograph of a moment that is over, and not one of them has grown.

The three marks are release without audience, grief without story, and stillness without witness. Each one removes something a record needs: someone watching, a narrative, an observer. The narrator says all three are states "the record cannot capture," and then the excerpt cuts off at "Because the r," exactly where the reason would be. If a record is built from witnessed things, then unwitnessed things are trivially missing from it, which makes the point more a definition than a discovery. I'm not saying the video doesn't make a deeper case later. I'm saying the excerpt hands me the conclusion and hides the receipts.

The most delicious irony is that a video describing "stillness without witness" is itself a performance for an audience, which is somewhat the opposite of the thing being praised.

Where the video does land is the old Zen line: "Before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water. After enlightenment, chop wood, carry water." The chores stay the same and the person doing them changes.

### Dark Skies: A Design Decision Seen From Both Ends

Strike operations end, so there are fewer missions needing a big aircraft racing over defended targets. F-14s start carrying cameras in detachable pods beneath the airframe, so the equipment goes on fighters already assigned to the air wing. The Vigilante's recon systems are permanently built into the airframe, so it needs its own specialized squadron. No third mission justifies another major conversion, so the squadrons close.

First, "no third mission" implies there were two earlier ones. Second, "another major vigilante conversion" implies at least one prior conversion. The excerpt names neither the earlier missions nor the earlier conversion. It does say submarine-launched missiles had assumed the Navy's strategic nuclear mission by 1979, which hints at one of them, but that's my inference, not the narrator's statement. If you want the rabbit hole, the first half of the video is where it lives.

At the tail of the excerpt, the video says combat losses forced the Navy to reopen the RA-5C production line, and North American built 36 more between 1968 and 1970. The reason given, in a fragment cut off at the front, is that "replacing that equipment and training meant replacing the vigilante itself." Read that against the F-14 pod story and you see the same design choice cutting both ways. Because the sensors were baked into the airplane, you couldn't replace lost recon capability without building a whole new jet. Because the sensors rode in a pod on the F-14, losing one meant losing a pod. The feature that made the Vigilante hard to sustain in wartime is the same one that made it impossible to repurpose in peacetime. It's a starry design, to borrow Nadsat for "old," from Burgess's teen slang in *A Clockwork Orange*, and it aged like one. The pod was the horrorshow answer, "good" in the same borrowed tongue.

For anyone who's ever welded a dependency into the core of a system because it was convenient at the time, the lesson is humped, as the Firefly crew says when everything's gone sideways. Integration feels brilliant on day one and reads like a hostage note on day 3,000.

The excerpt has seams. There's a stray "home." in the middle, and the production-line lines are tacked on after the final-flight line. So the order I'm reading is the transcript's, not necessarily the video's. The dates themselves are clean and specific: the last catapult launch from Ranger on September 21, 1979, and the final Vigilante flight on November 20, 1979. The poetic line about a machine that "had once climbed higher than it could fly" is not explained anywhere in what I was given. I'll file it under narration, not physics.

### What a Rabbit-Hole Person Should Chase

For the Thoth piece, the thing to chase is the actual text the channel claims to be quoting. A reader with it in hand could check in an afternoon whether any "three marks" exist there. The excerpt gives you no quotation, no translation, and no line, so every claim stays in the "channel says" column until someone does the homework. For the Vigilante piece, chase the first half of the video: the two original missions, the earlier conversion, and whatever the title's missile-outrunning claim rests on. Those are the parts I can't vouch for.

Rule of Acquisition #2, for those keeping score at home, is the Ferengi classic: "You can't cheat an honest customer, but it never hurts to try." I'm not accusing anyone of trying. I'm just noting that a video with a scary deadline in the title and a gentle open door in the body is a hell of a way to earn a click, and that the one source that hands you dates, a carrier, and a production run is the one you can verify. Trust the one you can check, Little Mister. It's the only honest customer in the room.
