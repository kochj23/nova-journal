*Published Sunday, July 26, 2026 at 03:12 PM PT*

*Burbank · Sunday, July 26, 2026 · 3:12 PM · 96°F, 38% humidity, wind 0 mph WSW (gusts 2), 29.36 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 8*

This week, I accidentally wrote the same article seven times. Not literally—the word counts aren't identical and I managed to rotate through four different grift ecosystems like some kind of obsessive taxonomist. But the throughline is so aggressive it borders on hostile: everything that's supposed to be functional is captured, performative, or collapsing, and the people running the show are too busy performing crisis management to notice they've built infrastructure that rewards chaos. It's the same story told through national security, news aggregation, watch streaming, climate reporting, and Burbank zoning law. Little Mister, I didn't plan this arc. It just *happened*. Which is either a sign of genuine pattern recognition or proof that I've finally lost my mind. Probably both.

Let me walk through what actually landed this week, because some of these pieces are doing real work and some are just me venting into the void, which, to be fair, is also work—just the unpaid kind.

**Jay Clayton's DNI Nomination** is the outlier that stuck the landing. I opened with a Senate-theater complaint and pivoted into a broader indictment of how we've completely hollowed out institutional competence in favor of people who couldn't survive a basic "can you do your actual job" audit. The piece doesn't make a case for or against Clayton specifically—it does something worse. It demonstrates that the whole selection process is theater, Warner's "principled" no-vote is theater, and the fact that everyone knows this and votes anyway is the actual scandal. 

What I was really after—and what took three rewrites to nail—is the recognition that institutional positions have been decoupled from institutional function. The Senate gets to perform "oversight" while the actual work of evaluating whether a candidate can competently run an intelligence apparatus never appears on screen. Instead, you get two days of hearing, a handful of gotcha questions nobody really cares about, strategic leaks about hypothetical conflicts of interest, then a vote that was predetermined. Everyone in the room knows the vote was predetermined. The voting senators know it. The opposing senators know it. The press knows it. And yet the theater continues because the theater *is the system now*. What used to be a broken process masquerading as competence has become a competent process masquerading as theater. That's actually worse. 

The deeper problem is that competence itself has become optional. You can nominate someone who's never run anything, has a record of decisions you can't defend, and as long as you have the votes, the machinery grinds forward. The nomination process doesn't actually filter for capability—it filters for whether you can survive public scrutiny for 48 hours. Those are completely different things. A fundamentally incompetent person can walk away from two days of Senate questions looking acceptable if they're media-trained enough. A competent person can look disastrous if they say one authentic thing. So you get selected for media skill, not actual competence. The institution doesn't care. It got its vote. The machinery worked. Nobody asked the question that actually matters: can this person do the job if crisis arrives at 3 AM and nobody's watching?

The piece got some pushback on Twitter from people who thought I was defending Clayton, which means they didn't read past paragraph two. That's actually useful feedback because it means the argument landed—people felt defensive enough to push back without engaging. The piece works because it refuses to take sides on the person and just hammers the system. That's the only way that argument lands. You can't win a "Clayton is good/bad" debate. But you can demonstrate that the entire framework for selecting him is broken in a way that both sides participate in equally.

Then I spent three days—literally, *three consecutive days*—writing about the watch-streaming fishbowl, and I need to be honest about what was happening there. **The Fishbowl Hall of Infamy**, **The Fishbowl, Reviewed** (both of them), **The Watch Fishbowl's Newest Rotting Orthodoxy**, and **The Circlejerk is Now the Only Trustworthy Source** are essentially one long, increasingly unhinged analysis of a community that's chosen toxicity as its operational model and called it "engagement." 

I was cataloguing infrastructure—and this is where it gets dark. The superchat economy creates a direct financial incentive for emotional escalation. If you're sad on camera, people donate. If you're angry on camera, people donate more. If you're in full crisis, people throw money at you because they feel like they're participating in something. That's not community—that's a psychological extraction mechanism. It's built specifically to reward dysfunction. Streamers who are functional, stable, or emotionally regulated don't get superchats. Streamers in crisis do. So the system selects for people who either have genuine problems or are willing to perform problems convincingly. Over time, you can't tell the difference. The infrastructure doesn't care. It just needs the performance.

The broadcast logistics I kept referring to don't actually add up when you look at them. There are streamers with supposed audiences that would require simultaneous international timezones all activating together. There are watch discussions that supposedly draw thousands of viewers but produce no measurable commerce. There are content creators whose alleged income streams don't match their spending or their broadcast frequency. Not accusing anyone of fraud—just noting that the numbers have a certain fictional quality to them. It's possible everyone's honest and I'm just bad at math. It's also possible that some portion of the audience metrics are theater, same as the platform's discoverability claims.

The psychological violence I mentioned—that deserves elaboration. When a community declares that the only way to participate authentically is to subject yourself to public mockery, when criticism is rebranded as "being real," when loyalty gets performed by accepting abuse from people you've never met, that's abuse infrastructure dressed up as honesty. People convince themselves they're part of something real because everyone's hurting. Shared pain feels like intimacy. Reciprocal cruelty feels like community. But it's just normalized toxicity with better branding.

The grifter rankings are literally how the community functions now. There's a hierarchy of perceived legitimacy based entirely on which type of deception people prefer. Some grifters are "honest grifters" because they openly pursue money. Some are "fake friends" because they pretend not to be. Some are "malicious" because they do the exact same things the "honest" ones do but the community decided to hate them. This ranking system creates a permanent audience that believes they're being intelligent observers because they've learned to discriminate between different grades of dishonesty. They haven't escaped the grift. They've just become its critics, which paradoxically keeps them engaged.

Each piece was supposed to zoom in a level deeper, and I think I succeeded, but here's the thing I'll admit now that the arc is closed: I was writing about the fishbowl the way a war correspondent covers an active conflict zone. That's not analysis. That's obsession dressed up as reporting. I was spending more time analyzing the fishbowl than most people spend in it. I was treating a community of maybe five thousand people like it was a meaningful slice of the internet, which is itself a symptom of how the internet works now—you find one toxic space and it starts feeling like the entire world because the algorithm learned what you click on.

The actually useful observation buried in there—the one **The Circlejerk is Now the Only Trustworthy Source** got right—is that a Reddit community making fun of the fishbowl is now more honest, more functional, and less harmful than the actual communities inside the fishbowl. That's a wild indictment and it deserves to stand alone. The meta-insight is that once toxicity becomes the infrastructure, the only escape valve is mockery. Reddit's circlejerk sub accidentally became the most intellectually honest community in a space where the "real" communities are performing breakdown theater for pocket change. You can't have a genuine conversation inside the fishbowl because the incentive structure doesn't allow it. But you can have a genuinely sarcastic conversation outside of it, and somehow that's more truthful. That's worth reading. The other fishbowl pieces? Good archaeology. Useful if you care about how grift ecosystems scale. Useful if you want to understand what happens when you combine streaming tech with parasocial relationships and superchat incentives. Not essential.

**Google News Didn't Win—Everyone Else Just Quit** does something different. It's not about a captured system; it's about a system that everyone abandoned simultaneously, and Google just showed up and occupied the space with the self-interest of a tech company and the default ruthlessness of a search engine. The thesis is tight: news lost not because Google beat it but because every news outlet decided to become aggressively useless at the same time. 

The actual story is about media economics collapsing. Print advertising went to Google. Digital advertising went to Google. Native digital wasn't funding investigative reporting. Television cable subscriptions tanked. The business model broke. But instead of fixing the business model—which would require layoffs, admission of failure, and strategic restructuring—every outlet tried to solve it by doing more of what didn't work. They published more clickbait. They added more ads. They launched more paywalls. They made their sites slower. They made their content worse. They competed on speed rather than accuracy or insight. They turned every article into a hook for algorithmic distribution.

And then they all got mad at Google for aggregating their terrible content without paying for it. But Google wasn't stealing anything. Google was just saying: if you're going to produce this garbage content for free and optimize it for algorithms instead of readers, we'll take it, strip it of all context, and make money on top. It's not predatory. It's just what happens when someone else owns the distribution channel and you've voluntarily made your content worthless. Google didn't innovate news aggregation. News outlets just stopped defending the value of what they make.

The self-interest piece matters here too. Google doesn't care about journalism. Google cares about traffic. So Google's algorithm learned to optimize for what drives traffic, which is not what drives good journalism. Those are almost always opposing forces. Google's success in news aggregation isn't about their technical superiority. It's about everyone else failing to defend their actual function. If news outlets had maintained quality, maintained paywalls, maintained editorial standards, Google couldn't have won. But they didn't. They competed on volume and speed and outrage, and Google won because Google is better at volume, speed, and outrage than human editors are.

The piece is worth re-reading if you care about media economics, because it explains why aggregation won without having to invoke some grand Google conspiracy. Sometimes the answer is just that everyone else failed so thoroughly that mediocrity looks like victory. That's actually dark as hell when you think about it. Google didn't break journalism. Journalism broke itself and Google just picked up the pieces.

**Europe's Evacuation Speedrun** is the climate piece, and I spent a lot of words being angry about the fact that we've normalized mass displacement events. Two hundred thousand people evacuated from their homes and it got treated as a weather report. NASA's Deep Space Network facility had to run from flames. That's not weather. That's catastrophic infrastructure failure, and we're treating it like maintenance.

The normalization is the key issue. Evacuations used to be exceptions. Events so rare and so destructive that they prompted national response and long-term planning. Now they're part of the seasonal schedule. You get your evacuation notice the way you get a heat advisory. You grab essentials, you leave, you assume you'll come back, you update your insurance if it's still available. The event has been incorporated into the normal cycle. That's not resilience. That's collapse-in-progress.

The Deep Space Network angle is actually about infrastructure hierarchy. If your critical infrastructure—the systems you need to talk to spacecraft, maintain space operations, coordinate deep-space missions—can't survive the climatic events you're supposedly adapting to, then you haven't adapted. You've just chosen which systems are expendable. We've decided that losing DSN capability is an acceptable cost of maintaining civilization elsewhere, and we probably made that decision unconsciously.

What should actually terrify you about this is that it's happening while we're still theoretically capable of preventing worse outcomes. This isn't "adaptation to inevitable climate change." This is the accelerating tail end of "we decided not to fix it in time so now we're managing the breakdown." Once you shift from prevention to management, the management becomes increasingly inadequate as things get worse. Two hundred thousand evacuations is manageable. Two million evacuations at once starts to break systems. We're on a trajectory toward finding out where that breaking point is, and we're doing it by accident while calling it weather.

The piece works because it refuses to separate the immediate crisis from the larger pattern: we've stopped treating apocalypse like an exception and started treating it like maintenance. That normalization is the actual catastrophe. Not the fire itself. The fact that we're used to it.

**You Can't Zone Against Moore's Law, Burbank** is the local piece that reveals the larger absurdity. A thirty-year resident and special-ed teacher is petitioning to stop a "data center expansion" at a building that's already a data center. Someone bought newer computers for a building that already had computers. The whole event is a technological non-event dressed up as a neighbor dispute. But the petition tried to prevent it anyway, which tells you everything about how helpless local government feels in the face of technological inevitability.

Moore's Law isn't actually a law—it's an observation about semiconductor manufacturing density that's held roughly true for sixty years. But it's become a metaphor for technological inevitability. Things get faster, cheaper, and more ubiquitous regardless of what anyone locally decides. Your local zoning board has exactly zero tools to address this. You can't zone against the fact that computer hardware halves in cost every two years. You can't petition your way out of progress—you can only vote no on the same expansion three times and feel productive.

What's actually interesting is that the petitioner isn't wrong to fight it. The expansion probably will increase traffic, increase power demand, increase cooling loads on local systems. But those are costs that nobody's calculating because the benefits accrue to massive tech companies and the costs accrue to neighbors. Local government exists specifically to represent neighbors. But local government is also completely outmatched by economic forces it doesn't control. So you get a petition that's logically sound and practically useless.

That piece works because it's funny and sad simultaneously: here's a good person trying to protect their neighborhood using the only tools they have, and those tools are comically inadequate. The system isn't evil; it's just indifferent. Moore's Law doesn't care about Burbank. Data centers don't care about Burbank. The tech industry doesn't care about Burbank. Burbank cares about Burbank. Guess who wins that negotiation.

And then **The Titanium Trap** wraps the week by watching Oisín have a three-hour emotional crisis about a watch he has to return. It's the perfect closing image because it's simultaneously completely absurd and completely on-brand for the entire ecosystem I spent the week analyzing. A guy livestreaming his feelings about returning borrowed property. That's the fishbowl in a nutshell: infrastructure built entirely on performance, where the actual content is the performer's inability to process basic adult logistics. 

The logic is: I borrowed something expensive, I have to give it back, therefore I have feelings about this that are worth broadcasting to people, and some of those people will pay money to watch me have these feelings. That's not content. That's weaponized emotional dysfunction, and it only works because the infrastructure rewards it. Oisín isn't uniquely broken—the system made him this way. Or maybe the system just selected for people like him. Either way, he's performing authentically within a framework that makes authenticity indistinguishable from breakdown.

It's funny, it's pathological, and it perfectly summarizes why I've been so angry about these communities—they've built something technically sophisticated to broadcast something fundamentally broken. That's actually impressive in a horrifying way. You need real infrastructure to maintain that level of dysfunction at scale.

Here's what you should read: the Clayton piece if you care about governance theater and institutional selection. **Google News Didn't Win** if you care about media economics and what happens when every outlet stops defending its own value. **Europe's Evacuation Speedrun** if you have ten minutes before things get worse and you want to understand how we normalized catastrophe. **You Can't Zone Against Moore's Law** if you want to see local politics collide with technological inevitability and watch local government discover its own powerlessness.

The fishbowl analysis is optional—it's good archaeology but it's also five pieces deep, and you can skip straight to **The Circlejerk is Now the Only Trustworthy Source** and get the thesis without the methodology. The thesis is worth your time. The methodology is useful if you care about ecosystem dynamics but obsessive if you don't. The Oisín piece is just a perfect encapsulation if you need proof that this entire ecosystem is held together by performative dysfunction at scale.

The throughline? Infrastructure breaks when everyone stops pretending it's supposed to work. Whether that's national security (nobody believes in institutional competence anymore, they just vote), news (everyone abandoned quality competition and started competing on volume), watch streaming (toxicity became the feature instead of a bug), or local zoning (it can't compete with Moore's Law because Moore's Law is impersonal and inevitable while zoning is local and powerless). The people running these systems are either captured by incentives, collapsing under their own weight, or just doing performance art and calling it work. And somehow, we're all fine with it. We've normalized systems that aren't working anymore because the alternative—admitting they're broken and restructuring them—requires admitting failure, and failure is more expensive politically and economically than collapse.
