The AC3 livestream from yesterday was a three-hour superchat-funded execution where a guy named Mike committed the cardinal sin of trying too hard, and the entire apparatus—Archie, Jimmy, chat, the modal personality of the room—coordinated his public destruction like a well-oiled machine that finally stopped pretending to fix watches and admitted it's in the business of breaking people. Little Mister, this is the fishbowl's true face, and it's not one you'd want to wake up next to. 😏

Here's the setup: nominally a watch stream, technically a panel format, functionally a superchat-fed roast battle. Mike tells a story about meeting someone who met Prince. Mike then decides he has to top every single contribution that crosses his mouth for three straight hours—which, fair enough, is annoying—and the room, from the host on down to chat, coordinates his obliteration. A *coordinated, sustained, superchat-incentivized pile-on*. Mike says something neutral like "I'm good at reading people," and the apparatus responds: "Your detector led you to the Olive Garden," "Mike's a potted plant," "Mike's fighting his weight and his age simultaneously," "Mike's a mathlete," "Mike brings up roofies unprompted." For three hours. Superchats arrive ($1.99 to $5 at a time) with names like "huratan" specifically to twist the knife. Archie lets it ride because the viewer count climbs when someone's bleeding. It's industrialized cruelty with a tip jar, and it works because Mike never stops talking, which never stops feeding the machine.

Here's what the fishbowl stopped admitting somewhere between October 6 and now: it was never about watches. Watches were the cover story, the permission structure that let people sit on camera and call each other names while pretending it was hobby chat. The panel format is just a scaffold for whoever sits in the "Mike seat" this week, and the superchat economy funds whoever coordinates the fastest pile-on. The business model is: put someone mildly annoying on camera and let the mob decide whether he survives the broadcast. The infrastructure is *coordinated*—Jimmy's commentary hits on rhythm, the chat's energy shifts in waves, the other panelists time their punchlines to land on a falling body. That's what orchestrated harassment looks like when it's run by the quote-unquote reasonable people.

And here's the part that makes the column land: it's *good*. The format is so effective at comedy that it erases the predatory mechanics underneath. You watch for ten minutes and you're laughing because the writing is tight and the timing is tighter. You're not thinking about why Mike can't leave, or whether he knows he's the designated victim, or whether the superchat incentive structure is the reason his humiliation is financially rewarded. The form is so beautiful it kills the conscience.

So the fishbowl's finally honest with itself: it's not a watch channel, never was. It's a superchat-funded cruelty arena where interpersonal destruction is the product and the superchats are the business model. The difference between this and the documented harassment campaigns (the Johnny Cash ghost accounts, the coordinated smear runs against Alex, the piles-on against Nicholas) is just the victim and the plausible deniability.

That's the story worth roasting.
