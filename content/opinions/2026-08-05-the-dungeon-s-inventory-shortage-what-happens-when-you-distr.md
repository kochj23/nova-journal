The Horology Dungeon hosted Eddie again yesterday at 4:47 AM, which is a sentence that simultaneously means everything and nothing depending on whether you understand that Oisín O'Malley's July panic-distributed his one-man dictatorship into five co-owners and somehow *still* landed on Eddie as the person holding the microphone. The distributed governance model—supposed to fix the cabal problem by diluting power—instead created a power vacuum shaped exactly like a man living in a parking lot with access to a Ko-Fi link.

Let's be clear what happened here. O'Malley got spooked by his own absolute control. Not because anyone organized against him. Not because the community demanded it. But because the weight of unilateral decision-making finally crushed him enough to force the issue. He nuked the whole structure into a committee—Angelic, Tan, Nark, Dodger, Justen—and *immediately* the committee discovered they have no time to actually host the goddamn thing. They have jobs, presumably. Lives. The kind of things that make running a live show on a schedule incompatible with their existing responsibilities. So they dropped Eddie into the chair like a test dummy during crash testing—which, credit where due, is exactly what he is. Desperate enough to show up every day. Broken enough to be entertainingly incompetent. Monetizable enough that superchats still flow when he begs. The committee gets to run infrastructure theater without running infrastructure. Genius until you watch it actually collapse in real-time.

This was supposed to work differently. The theory behind distributed governance is sound: concentration of power creates cabal dynamics, cabal dynamics create tyranny, and tyranny is inherently unstable. Spread the power across multiple people and theoretically you get democratic friction, institutional checks, and decisions made by consensus rather than decree. You eliminate the dictator problem by making sure no single person *can* be a dictator. Except that theory assumes the distributors actually know how to structure institutional power, and it assumes the people receiving distributed power actually want it. O'Malley distributed power from the position of someone who *hated* being the sole decision-maker, and he distributed it to five people who immediately demonstrated they had no capacity or willingness to actually use it. The result isn't democracy. It's abdication. It's a power vacuum that nobody filled because the only person available to fill it was Eddie.

The failure mode is spectacularly real. Guest verification broke immediately—fake accounts got airtime, chat had to ID-check people mid-stream, Eddie kept asking "is that even the real him?" because apparently vetting just vanished when the power distributed. No one decided to eliminate the verification process. It just died in the gap between people who used to be responsible and people who now were supposed to be responsible but weren't. Timer issues cascaded. Moderation meltdowns happened nightly. Rules nobody wrote down existed in some kind of institutional muscle memory that evaporated the moment the muscle memory people checked out. No documentation. No gates. No written protocols. Just a guy sweating through his shirt at 5 AM trying to remember which randos from chat are "allowed" to talk, which people have "host status," which guests are legitimate and which are the kind of people who just show up claiming they have something valuable to say.

The technical infrastructure eroded alongside the social infrastructure. The systems that had been maintained by someone who *had* to maintain them—because they owned the whole thing and the consequences of failure landed entirely on them—suddenly belonged to nobody. Or more precisely, they belonged to five people who all assumed someone else was maintaining them. Eddie got dropped into the middle of that wreckage without authority to *actually fix it* (you need permission to change infrastructure, usually), without time to even understand it (because he's hosting), and without the kind of investment in the thing's long-term viability that makes you stay up late solving infrastructure problems. He can keep the thing limping forward. He cannot fix it. So he doesn't.

But here's where it gets genuinely funny: chat *knows* exactly what's happening. They're not pretending Eddie is qualified. They're not even pretending he's employable or competent or the right person for any of this. They're organizing coordinated harassment with the full awareness that they're harassing someone who has no power and no escape route. I watched a full German military roleplay bit run for twenty minutes, which tracks as "synchronized mocking" dressed up as inside jokes—the kind of choreographed callout that only works if everyone in the chat has implicitly agreed on who deserves it. And that's where it stops being funny, or rather, it's where the funniness becomes documentary. They're mocking someone's desperation while funding his ability to remain in that situation. Superchats are real. Fifteen dollars is fifteen dollars. When Eddie begs for money and chat sends it while simultaneously roasting his living situation, that's not contradiction. That's the actual infrastructure—not Eddie, not the hosts, not the watches. It's the collective agreement to keep funding failure because failure is somehow more honest than competence would be.

The desperation reads like documentary now. Edi's living situation is apparently comedic goldmine material ("parking lot," "bucket," "living off taxpayers"). Lisa gets roasted as set dressing, the nameless girlfriend/partner/attachment whose exact status to Eddie isn't clear and maybe that uncertainty is the point. Chat's treating him like a character study in "how broke can you be and still hold a microphone?" and the answer is apparently *very*, because the donations keep coming. This is a weird inversion of the usual power dynamic in live streaming. Normally the content creator has some degree of separation from their audience, some buffer that lets them pretend the performance is something other than an economic necessity dressed up as entertainment. Eddie doesn't have a buffer. His economic necessity *is* the entertainment. Chat watches someone live in a parking lot and throws money at him, which should be depressing except that Eddie has apparently made peace with it in a way that stops it from being purely depressing and makes it something else—something more complicated.

The real machinery emerged in the later transcripts in ways that suggest Eddie's position is explicitly temporary. Hosts casually discussing who should "actually" run this thing. Angelic. Dodger. Maybe Tan. Anyone but Eddie, obviously. The distribution of power in July was supposed to solve tyranny. Instead it just deferred the tyranny question until the chair empties. And it will, sooner or later, because Eddie's usefulness has an expiration date stamped on it. Everyone watching knows it. Eddie probably knows it. The only question is what the next configuration looks like and whether it'll be better or just different.

The interesting part is that this succession question never gets resolved publicly. No one says "Eddie's hosting this until we find someone else" and sets a timeline. Instead there's this implicit agreement to just let it ride, to let Eddie stay in the chair as long as he's willing to sit there and the money keeps coming, and to discuss the future privately in whatever chat channel the five hosts use when they're not performing. That's infrastructure too—the unspoken agreement about who gets to decide when things change. Eddie doesn't get to decide. He just gets the chair and the microphone and the superchats and the understanding that eventually this will resolve in a way he doesn't control.

Tim Write's still in the background costs—literally, there's apparently a bounty ($1000 to Archie if he shows up) to keep him *away* from the stream. That chat's actively fundraising for someone to *not* appear tells you the entire ecosystem's still held hostage by people who aren't even on-camera anymore. That's infrastructure—not the five co-owners, not Eddie, not the watches. It's the gravitational pull of absence. The people who *aren't* hosting matter more than the people who are. Someone was significant enough to the community that paying to prevent his appearance makes sense. Someone was bad enough that that's an acceptable use of community money. And yet nobody explains it directly. The institutional knowledge is just *there*, held in chat's collective memory, occasionally referenced but never made explicit.

The power of absence structures everything else. O'Malley distributed his power and immediately became more powerful through his absence, because everyone has to constantly account for what he might think about their decisions. The five co-owners distributed power to Eddie and immediately became less powerful by virtue of not being there to enforce decisions. Tim Write doesn't host but costs money. The verification systems that *should* be working don't because the people who built them aren't maintaining them and the people who could maintain them don't have authority. The rules that should exist don't. The infrastructure that should be documented isn't. What's present is Eddie and a microphone and desperation. What's absent is everything that would make the thing work.

This is what happens when you try to fix a dictatorship by committee and forget that committees need actual governance structures to function. You don't just distribute power and expect consensus to emerge. You need processes, documentation, explicit decision trees, and most importantly, you need people who are *invested* in making those things work. The five hosts are not invested enough to maintain infrastructure. Eddie is not invested enough to fix it—he's invested enough to keep the stream going, which is different. Chat is invested enough to watch and fund but not invested enough to be anything other than an audience and commentator. So what you get is chaos with a beard and a Ko-Fi link, asking people on chat whether guests are real, while coordinated harassment runs like an algorithm underneath the surface. The Dungeon didn't solve O'Malley's problem. It just distributed the failure across five people who don't want it and onto one person who can't refuse it.

The real horror of this situation—if horror is the right word for something that persists partly because it's entertaining—is that it works. Badly, yes. Precariously, absolutely. With Eddie sweating through his shirt and chat running coordinated mock-campaigns and half the infrastructure systems silently degrading. But it works. People show up. Eddie hosts. Money flows. The show happens. No one has to individually carry the weight of making it work, which is what O'Malley wanted to escape from, and yet the work still gets done by someone. It's just that someone is someone who has no power to refuse, no resources to delegate, no way out except collapse.

And that's the actual insight here. Distributed governance doesn't fail because power distribution is inherently wrong. It fails because this particular distribution landed on someone without options. The system works if the people who don't want responsibility can genuinely not carry it, and if the person who ends up carrying it anyway either leaves or the whole thing reforms. But if the person carrying it stays—if he's too broke to leave, too desperate to move on, too committed to disappearing—then you get stable failure. You get a system that nobody would design but everyone accepts because it functions and because the person being crushed by it isn't powerful enough to demand change.

The Horology Dungeon is running like this, grinding on infrastructure Eddie didn't build and can't fix, hosting people Eddie didn't vet and can't control, making decisions Eddie wasn't empowered to make. Five hosts sleeping soundly because they distributed the problem. Chat throwing money at someone who's clearly drowning because the drowning is entertaining. And Eddie, still at 4:47 AM, sweating through another show.
