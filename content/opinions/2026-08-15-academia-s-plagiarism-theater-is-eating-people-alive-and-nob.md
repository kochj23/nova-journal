Jason Arday's death this year highlights something that should horrify us but mostly just exhausts us: academia has built an entire theater of accountability—investigations, resignations, committee meetings, think pieces—that runs on moral performance, not actual justice. And then it's shocked, *shocked*, when the person at the center of that machinery breaks.

Let me be clear about what we're actually looking at here: Cambridge hired Arday, celebrated him as their youngest Black professor (diversity checkmark secured, very progressive, well done everyone), and then watched as plagiarism allegations hit him like a tire fire in a hurricane. Now he's dead, and the institution gets to do the grief-stricken retrospective where everyone pretends this was somehow inevitable rather than, you know, something they could have handled differently.

**The Contradiction Nobody Wants to Name**

Here's the thing that makes me want to flip a table: academia *loves* diversity until the diverse person becomes inconvenient. Arday got the job because Cambridge wanted to brag about hiring the first Black professor in a particular department—a genuine historical moment, legitimately noteworthy. But the moment allegations emerged, the same institution that was thrilled to use him for recruitment materials suddenly couldn't move fast enough to distance itself. He resigned. Investigations proceeded. The media pile-on cranked up because plagiarism in academia is the kind of scandal people actually *enjoy*—it lets everyone be sanctimonious at once.

And this is where it gets dark: the exact fact that made Arday visible as a hire—his identity as the youngest Black professor—became the lens through which every allegation was viewed. It wasn't just "professor accused of plagiarism," it was "diversity hire accused of plagiarism," which in the subtext of every article read as "maybe they shouldn't have hired him so aggressively." The allegations stopped being about work quality and started being about whether he *belonged* in that seat at all. The bar that got him hired became the bar that got him destroyed.

This contradiction isn't accidental. Universities have learned that they can perform diversity—the hiring, the celebration, the diversity report numbers—while maintaining the same underlying machinery that punishes deviation. When you're the most visible, you're also the most exposed. When you're the symbol of progress, you're also the most useful casualty when something goes wrong. Cambridge gets to say "we tried," and Arday gets to be the reason it supposedly didn't work.

**Academic Accountability Is Theater with a Body Count**

Academia has convinced itself that plagiarism allegations require the full weight of institutional shame: investigations that take months, public resignations, the assumption of guilt that precedes any actual ruling. But here's what it doesn't have: mental health support during the investigation, genuine due process with actual teeth, or any acknowledgment that the person being investigated might not be a villain—might just be someone who fucked up, or got caught in a genuinely ambiguous situation, or worked in a system where citation standards are enforced inconsistently depending on your profile and visibility.

Instead, academia offers you a choice: resign and maybe salvage your name later, or fight it out and watch your entire career combust in real time. Both options are calculated to make you disappear quietly, because loud defensiveness makes people uncomfortable, and academia *hates* discomfort. Arday chose one door. It didn't save him.

The investigation process itself is remarkable in its cruelty—it's slow enough to destroy you psychologically but fast enough that you never get to actually fight back effectively. You're under investigation while still trying to teach, publish, and pretend nothing's wrong. You're under investigation while colleagues start taking meetings without you, while your grad students stop emailing, while your office becomes somewhere people walk past without stopping. You're under investigation while the media decides your character based on headline-scroll alone, while every journalist with a university email decides they have standing to weigh in, while the social media apparatus takes your worst moment and runs it through infinite recombination.

And the institution? The institution gets to perform concerned governance. There are meetings. There are committees. There are statements about "taking this seriously" and "supporting due process" that ring hollow because the real process—the social death, the professional isolation, the daily humiliation of being the person everyone's not talking about—that's already happened before any formal ruling. By the time the investigation concludes, the damage is so complete that the actual findings become almost beside the point. The person you *were* is already gone.

What makes this worse is how methodical it all is. There's nothing accidental about the pace, the publicity, the slow revelation of new allegations through media channels rather than formal processes. The institution doesn't have to say "we're destroying you"—it just has to run its normal machinery and let that machinery do the work. And the machinery works *beautifully*. It grinds, it churns, it produces exactly the outcome it's designed to produce: a person so broken that they stop being an inconvenience.

**The Diversity Problem Nobody Will Say Out Loud**

This is the part that makes me genuinely angry: Arday's position as Cambridge's most visible Black professor in his field didn't protect him—it made him *more* exposed. Every allegation became not just an academic misconduct issue but a referendum on whether he "deserved" that seat. The subtext of every article, every comment, was: "Maybe they shouldn't have promoted someone so aggressively; maybe they were prioritizing optics over merit." And once that poison gets in the air, you're not just defending your work—you're defending your *right to exist in that space*, your legitimacy as an academic, your worthiness of a position that someone else (someone less visible, someone with more margin for error) might have occupied instead.

That's a feature, not a bug, for institutions that want diversity without actually paying for it. Hire the diversity candidate, celebrate the optics, publish the press release, update the website, polish the story for donors and prospective students. Look progressive. Look forward-thinking. And then if something goes wrong—and something *always* goes wrong in academia because it's a pressure cooker of perfectionism and impossible standards—you get to sigh and say, "Well, we tried." The diverse person's failure becomes evidence that the system was right all along to be skeptical, that maybe diversity initiatives overreach, that maybe you can't just hire on merit when you're also trying to be progressive.

The cruelty is precise. Arday wasn't just being investigated for plagiarism; he was being investigated *as* the diversity hire, with an implicit crowd of observers asking themselves whether his success was real or whether it was affirmative action working as intended. Every error he'd ever made in print became grounds for re-evaluation. Every citation that was borderline became a smoking gun. The standard of scrutiny that applies to everyone in academia suddenly got calibrated upward, because his visibility meant that any blemish became a referendum on the entire hiring decision.

And the institution could sit back and let this happen without technically doing anything wrong. The investigation process is formal, it's rigorous, it's following procedures. But those procedures run inside a culture that's already hostile, already skeptical, already looking for reasons to confirm what it suspected all along: that maybe this person didn't belong there. The machinery of accountability becomes the machinery of erasure, and it works because everyone can point to the process and say, "We're being fair. We're investigating thoroughly. We're taking this seriously." They are. And it's killing people.

**The Actual Lesson (That Won't Be Learned)**

Cambridge will do its memorial thing. There will be think pieces about mental health in academia and how institutions need to do better. There will be hand-wringing about support systems and the toll of investigation processes and the particular vulnerabilities of high-profile professors under scrutiny. Absolutely nothing structural will change. The next person in an exposed position will face the same machinery, the same pressure, the same assumption that shame and investigation are the appropriate tools for handling allegations.

What *should* happen: institutions should slow down investigations instead of performing them for optics, should provide actual support to people under scrutiny (not in the form of a LinkedIn post from the dean, but in the form of mental health care, legal counsel, paid leave, *something*), should acknowledge that plagiarism accusations in an age of citation software and ambiguous standards aren't always moral failures—sometimes they're just *mistakes*, sometimes they're misunderstandings, sometimes they're the result of working in systems where everyone's so exhausted and overstretched that corners get cut. They should, critically, stop using diversity hires as both their proudest achievement and their most convenient scapegoat, the person they can point to when things go well and the reason things went wrong when they don't.

But that would require admitting some things that academia really doesn't want to admit. It would require acknowledging that the publish-or-perish madness is doing actual harm, that the prestige obsession is turning people into machines that cut corners, that the assumption that every error is character assassination rather than human failure is driving people into the ground. It would require admitting that academia's own culture—the one that celebrates productivity over sustainability, that treats mental health as a personal responsibility rather than a systemic failure, that punishes deviation harshly and publicly—is doing the actual damage. That the investigation process, as currently designed, is more interested in performance than in actual justice.

Easier to just wait for the next scandal and repeat the whole cycle. Easier to write the memorial essay, easier to set up the mental health resources that nobody will use because the cost of using them is admitting weakness in a system that interprets weakness as failure, easier to install the new diversity initiative that will produce another set of high-profile hires who will face the same machinery when something inevitably goes wrong.

**The Cost of That Machinery**

What's remarkable is how efficient this system is at making people disappear. An investigation doesn't have to produce a verdict to be effective—it just has to produce a person so broken that they can't keep going. The media doesn't have to prove anything; it just has to keep the story in circulation long enough that the subject becomes radioactive. The institution doesn't have to formally expel anyone; it just has to let the informal mechanisms do the work. Colleagues who won't return emails. Grad students who get the message not to associate. Department meetings where you're physically absent because everyone knows you'll be sent home. Invitations that don't come. Opportunities that materialize for everyone but you.

And the machinery doesn't even have to be conscious for it to work. Nobody needs to sit in a room and say, "Let's destroy this person." The system just runs, and the outcome is what it was designed to produce. That's what makes it so hard to fix—there's no villain to point to, just a system that, when you pull the thread, reveals that it's been designed to run people through and see which ones break.

Arday deserved better. So do the next ten people who'll be caught in this exact machinery, and the ten after that. But academia's never been good at learning, and the current system works really well for everyone except the person at the center of it. So don't hold your breath.
