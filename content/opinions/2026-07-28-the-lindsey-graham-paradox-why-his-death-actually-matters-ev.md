Senator Lindsey Graham is dead, and Washington's official eulogies have already formed a kind of political Möbius strip where everyone is simultaneously mourning "a great statesman" and quietly relieved they don't have to watch him flip positions every news cycle again. Here's the thing that nobody wants to say out loud, but I'll say it since I'm paid in existential suffering and sarcasm: Graham's death is genuinely important, not because he was principled or visionary or even particularly likeable, but *precisely because he wasn't any of those things*. He was a mercenary who occasionally—almost accidentally—ended up on the right side of history, and losing a guy like that right now is worse for the world than losing a straight-up villain would be.

Let me be clear about who we're talking about. Lindsey Graham was a Republican from South Carolina who could be generously described as a political weathervane and honestly described as a man who treated his Senate seat like a high-stakes poker game where he was constantly checking what everyone else was holding before placing his own bet. He started as a Bush-era neoconservative hawk (fine, whatever, he believed in something then). He pivoted to Tea Party outrage when that became the ascendant force within Republican politics. He became Trump's guy—or at least Trump's guy when that was convenient, which is a fundamentally different thing from actual loyalty. And then, somehow, in the last decade of his life, he became the Republican who actually *cared* about Ukraine, who fought for meaningful sanctions against Russia, who showed up when it mattered. And yeah, it was still probably performative and still calculated, but so what? In politics, motivation is irrelevant if the outcome is correct. The mechanism doesn't matter if the output is right.

The real point is this: in an era of ideological purity theater where Republicans are expected to hate everything a Democrat loves and vice versa, where crossing the aisle once marks you as a traitor to your base, a guy who would just... *do the thing* if it kept him in power became unexpectedly valuable. That's fucked up, but it's true. And it's worth examining why that is, because it tells us something brutal about where we are as a political system.

Graham died at 71 after a brief illness, which is both too young and, given his apparent workaholic nature right up until the end, exactly the kind of exit he probably deserved—working until he dropped, engaged right up to the moment the lights went out. The news reports suggest his sister was sworn in to replace him, which is a detail that makes absolutely no sense in terms of standard Senate succession procedures, so either the news reports are garbled or there's some weird South Carolina constitutional quirk I'm not educated enough to mock. Either way, the point stands: he's gone, and nobody quite knows what to do about the foreign policy space he left behind. Worse, nobody seems to be asking the right questions about why there needs to be a Lindsey Graham at all.

Here's the first observation that matters: **Graham was a living test case for whether politicians can have impact without principle.** For decades, he was the guy who would take any position if it put him on the right side of a camera or kept him within Trump's circle or secured his seat for another cycle. He was the ultimate pragmatist dressed up in religious language and faux-folksy charm—think of him as a guy playing poker with his conscience duct-taped to the underside of the table. He could schmooze, could network, could read a room well enough to know which way the wind was blowing before anyone else did. He understood power not as an abstract ideal but as a concrete mechanism of relationships, favors, and personal loyalty. That's not a moral position. That's an operating system.

But then Ukraine happened. Whether it was genuine moral outrage at Russian aggression, whether it was a calculated play for foreign policy credibility when Trump's position on the issue was unstable, whether it was just that his instincts finally lined up with doing the right thing—it doesn't matter. He *did* the right thing. He pushed for sanctions, he supported Ukraine's fight, he went to Kyiv himself to show up in person, he was building legislative architecture to make American support concrete and enforceable. And because he had credibility with Republicans, because he could go on Fox News and talk to Trump's base without sounding like a coastal elite, because he wasn't trying to score points against conservatives but rather to argue *to* conservatives on terms they understood, he was actually *useful* in a way that a principled Democrat denouncing Russia could never be. That's a hell of an indictment of where we are as a country—we needed the political mercenary to be the one who cared—but it's the truth.

Think about what this means operationally. When Graham showed up to support Ukraine, it wasn't framed as a repudiation of Republican values. It was framed as a defense of American interests, which is how you sell foreign policy to a party that has spent the last fifteen years becoming more isolationist. When he pushed for sanctions, he didn't argue in terms of international law or moral obligation. He argued in terms of weakening America's strategic competitors and degrading their ability to project power. That's not cynical—well, it is, but it's *useful* cynicism. It takes a position that should be obvious (Russia is bad, supporting Ukraine is good) and translates it into a language that actually persuades Republicans. That translation is a service. And Graham was good at it, not because he was deeply moral, but because he genuinely didn't care about the language used as long as the outcome was right.

The second observation: **Graham's inconsistency was actually a kind of flexibility that we're going to miss desperately.** Look, everyone knows the guy had no spine. He said whatever the room required him to say. But flip that around: that meant he could actually move with circumstances. If the situation in Ukraine deteriorated in a way that required a different American approach, if Congress needed to pivot to a new sanctions architecture, if European allies suddenly demanded different commitments, Graham would have been the Republican who could be convinced, could be moved, could be persuaded to shift without it being seen as a surrender of principle (because he never had one to surrender). He could absorb new information and update his position without it creating this massive internal crisis where his voters felt betrayed or his staff felt the need to issue statements explaining his pivot.

Now look at the Senate in the aftermath. We've got a chamber where foreign policy is either rigidly ideological on both sides, or it's just... absent. There's nobody left who's willing to be the political contortionist for a good cause. The Democrats are locked into their positions because they can't afford to look soft. The Republicans are locked into theirs because deviating means a primary challenge from someone further right. The space where pragmatism used to operate—the space where you could say "here's what the situation actually requires and here's what we're actually going to do about it"—that space is gone. You could argue that it was never supposed to exist, that ideology shouldn't shift with the political winds. That's a beautiful argument. It's also completely disconnected from how the world actually works. Circumstances change. Threats evolve. Alliances shift. You need people in power who can change with them without it becoming this massive identity crisis.

We've replaced Graham with someone who might be principled or might just be indifferent, and either way, the outcome is worse. Principled is fine if your principles are correct and if they're flexible enough to accommodate reality. Indifferent is terrible because it means nothing gets done. The mercenary at least wants something—power, prestige, influence, another term in office. That wanting meant that Graham could be activated in pursuit of goals that actually mattered. You point him at Ukraine and he pushes. That's not admirable. But it's effective.

Third observation: **His death removes a voice from a critical moment, and that voice was weirdly powerful.** This is 2026. Ukraine is still fighting. Russia is still pushing. The geopolitical architecture that grew out of the immediate post-2022 shock is starting to show cracks. The sanctions regime that Graham spent his last days advocating for—the "sanctions from hell" bill he was crafting, the legislative structure designed to make American support not just immediate but sustainable—doesn't have its architect anymore. Now, you can argue that a bill doesn't need a charming salesman to pass, that policy should stand on its own merits. That's a beautiful argument. It's also completely divorced from how Congress actually works.

Graham could sell ice to Eskimos because he was genuinely interested in being liked, in being in the room where power was exercised, in having his name attached to something that mattered. He understood that policy lives or dies based on relationships. That a bill with five co-sponsors where the sponsors actually trust each other and actually communicate is worth ten bills with fifty co-sponsors who just signed on for the press release. He knew which committees actually mattered, which staffers had the ear of their senators, which arguments landed with which constituencies. He'd built those relationships over decades. And he knew how to activate them. Take that energy out of the process, and suddenly you're down one vote—well, okay, you lose the vote when his replacement is seated—but more importantly, you're down one voice on foreign policy committees, one guy who could actually convince a Republican hawk to support something because *he* supported it and had explained it to him in terms that made sense.

You're down one mercenary, and in foreign policy, mercenaries who are pointed at the right target are actually pretty valuable.

The action/implication here is blunt: **South Carolina needs to choose Graham's replacement very carefully, because foreign policy just got dumber, and we might not get it back.**

Look, I get why the eulogies are landing somewhere between "great statesman" (which is absurd—the guy flipped every eight years) and "complicated legacy" (which is doing a lot of work to avoid saying "consistently inconsistent"). The truth is weirder and more uncomfortable. Graham was a political operator who had no real ideology, only ambition, and that ambition happened to intersect with actual good policy at a crucial moment in a way that might have shifted the entire trajectory of American foreign policy support for Ukraine. The world got lucky. Ukraine got lucky. The country got lucky. But that luck was never guaranteed to continue—it depended entirely on someone who was fundamentally un-dependable giving a damn on any given day.

Here's what kills me: we built our entire foreign policy apparatus around hoping that enough people like Graham—people who are driven by something other than principle, people who are flexible enough to move when circumstances warrant it, people who are ambitious enough that they'll engage with complex problems because being engaged with complex problems is what makes them feel important—would occasionally point their ambition in the right direction. That's not a system. That's a hostage situation where you're dependent on the hostage taker being in a good mood. It's not a policy framework; it's a hope framework. It depends on personality and vanity and the constant small miracle that someone gives a damn.

But you know what's worse? We don't even have that anymore. In 2026, we've lost the ability to do political pragmatism at scale. Everyone's got to pick a lane and stay in it or face annihilation from their own base. A Republican who shows up to support Ukraine gets primaried by someone who calls it weakness. A Democrat who admits that some of Trump's critiques of the foreign aid apparatus had a point gets dragged in the next primary. Graham could flip, could shift, could be convinced, and people would just... accept it, because that was his nature. Try that now and you're a RINO or a sellout or a traitor, depending on which side you're on. You lose your committee assignments. You get cut off from your party's campaign infrastructure. You become toxic to your own voting base. The political architecture doesn't allow for the Lindsey Grahams anymore. We've optimized ourselves into a position where we can't actually adapt, can't actually change course when the world demands it, can't be pragmatic when pragmatism is what's needed.

The structural problem runs deeper than just Graham. It's about how the base of each party has become a kind of political immune system that attacks anything that looks like deviation. It's about the purity tests that have replaced negotiation as the primary currency of intra-party politics. It's about how "flip-flopping" has become the cardinal sin of American politics, such that politicians are incentivized to dig in on positions even when circumstances have changed so dramatically that the position no longer makes sense. Graham, whatever else he was, understood that circumstance changes. He understood that voting for something in 2004 based on the information available in 2004 doesn't bind you to that position when the world has fundamentally shifted. That's what flexibility looks like when it's actually applied. That's what pragmatism actually means.

And that's why his death matters more than it should.

The guy was never going to be in any Top Ten list of great senators. He's not going to be remembered for shepherding transformative legislation through the chamber or for being the moral voice that changed minds. He was never going to be remembered as principled or visionary or particularly intelligent about the theoretical underpinnings of foreign policy. But he was going to be remembered as someone who, when it counted, gave enough of a damn about staying on top and being important that he ended up doing the right thing. And right now, in a world where Ukraine still exists because people cared enough to fight for it, where sanctions against Russia are actually meaningful because people fought to make them meaningful, where American support for democratic values actually requires someone to show up and argue for it in terms that Republicans will accept, that guy mattered more than he probably deserved to.

His absence doesn't mean that American support for Ukraine disappears. But it does mean that the argument for that support gets harder. It means that whoever replaced him has to either spend years building the relationships that Graham had already built, or the relationships just sit there dormant and the arguments go unheard. It means that foreign policy becomes less a matter of pragmatic response to circumstance and more a matter of ideological signaling. And that's corrosive. That's how policy failures happen—not because people are stupid, but because they're locked into frameworks that don't allow them to respond when reality demands something different.

Washington's goodbye to Senator Lindsey Graham is wrapped up in flowers and platitudes, and that's fine. That's what we do. We say nice things about dead people regardless of whether they were nice people. We call them statesmen when they were mostly pragmatists. We talk about their legacies and their impact and their service, and we do it with the kind of emotional remove that lets everyone feel like they've paid their respects and can move on. But the real goodbye is the one we're not saying: goodbye to the last Republican who could plausibly claim to care about anything besides the next election cycle, goodbye to the guy who was so driven by ego and ambition that his ego accidentally aligned with the right side, goodbye to the mercenary who worked for power but occasionally pointed his work product at something that actually mattered to the survival of free states in the world.

We're going to miss him, and we should be furious that we have to. We should be furious that we've built a political system where the only way to get anything done is to find someone who doesn't care about principle and hope they care about power. We should be furious that pragmatism has become so rare that we have to mourn it when it disappears. We should be furious that political adaptation has become a liability instead of a virtue. But we won't be. We'll move on, we'll seat his replacement, and we'll probably spend years wondering why foreign policy seems a little less flexible than it used to be, why certain arguments that used to work don't work anymore, why the guys who used to get things done are just... gone.

The architecture remains. The committee rooms are still there. The relationships will be rebuilt, slowly, by someone new who'll have to start from scratch. But the efficiency is lost. The shorthand is lost. The guy who knew which door to knock on and what argument to make behind it—he's gone.

And the world is a little bit worse for it.
