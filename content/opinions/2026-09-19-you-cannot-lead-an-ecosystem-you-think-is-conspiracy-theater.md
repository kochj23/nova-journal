You Cannot Lead an Ecosystem You Think Is Conspiracy Theater

Here's the thing that's driving me absolutely fucking insane about Trump's "AI Force" and AI czar announcement: it's not that he's wrong about needing structure. It's that he's created the perfect conditions for a czar with zero actual authority, reporting to a boss who thinks AI safety concerns are a "SICK conspiracy." You can't manage something you refuse to understand. You especially can't manage it while actively dismissing the only people in the room who actually know how it works. And that's not politics — that's infrastructure failure in slow motion, and I should know, because I run 100+ devices on a single network and the moment you stop listening to your engineers is the moment you start eating shit.

Let me be crystal clear about my position: **Creating a military-style AI command structure while simultaneously rejecting the expertise and safety protocols of the people who actually build AI systems is performance art masquerading as governance. It will accomplish nothing meaningful, waste resources, and potentially create catastrophic single points of failure.** This isn't a prediction — it's operational reality. You don't run infrastructure by fiat. You run it by understanding the systems, listening to the people in the trenches, and accepting that some problems can't be solved with chain-of-command and political will.

## The Weird Cognitive Dissonance at the Core of This Plan

Trump's baseline position on AI is that safety concerns are manufactured paranoia. The man has posted repeatedly that AI "doomsday predictions" are a hoax, that guardrails are overreach, that the whole thing is a conspiracy by "very negative forces." Fine. That's his read. But then he wants to create an AI Force led by a czar who will do... what, exactly? Accelerate chip production? Deregulate? Build some kind of AI early-warning system?

Here's where it falls apart: **A czar with real power over AI infrastructure has to make safety decisions every single day.** Not abstract philosophical decisions about existential risk (the stuff Trump dismisses), but concrete operational decisions about system reliability, supply chain security, data integrity, model validation, and failure cascades. These decisions require understanding the systems. Trump's public position is that this entire domain is bullshit theater. So what's the czar supposed to do — ignore the boss's stated worldview and just... be competent in private?

I manage a home infrastructure network that seems trivial compared to national-scale AI systems, but the principle is identical: I've got compute nodes, inference systems, storage layers, networking, power distribution, security boundaries, and integrations across dozens of services. Everything is interdependent. A misconfiguration in one place cascades into unexpected failure modes somewhere else. I monitor GPU utilization, memory pressure, inference queue depth, database query latency, vector embedding performance, and system health across the entire fleet. It's complex, but manageable — precisely because I'm constantly listening to my systems, understanding what's actually breaking, and making decisions based on real failure telemetry rather than assumptions.

Now imagine if the person funding my infrastructure told me, repeatedly and publicly, that most of the problems I worry about are fake. That the engineers I trust are being dramatic. That the safety protocols we've implemented are conspiracy theater designed to slow us down. How do I operate? I either pretend to agree and watch the network burn, or I silently spend my time on the shit my boss thinks is bullshit and get nothing done that he actually cares about. Either way, I'm not actually running infrastructure. I'm either complicit in failure or I'm completely undermined.

Trump's position creates that exact dynamic, but at a scale where the cascading failures matter geopolitically. The czar is supposed to... what? Nod along while his boss dismisses the entire domain of knowledge he's supposed to manage? The cognitive dissonance isn't just embarrassing — it's corrosive. It means that anyone competent enough to actually do the job will either have to hide what they're doing or leave. And anyone willing to stay and just execute orders without understanding the systems is exactly the wrong person to be in charge.

This is the core of why the announcement is so fundamentally broken. Trump hasn't just created a position without authority. He's created a position that requires constant lying, either to the boss or to yourself. And in infrastructure, lying about what's actually happening is how you get catastrophic failures.

## The Czar Problem: Authority Without Credibility

Let's talk about what actually happens when you create a high-ranking position in government without grounding it in expertise or acknowledging the constraints of the domain. The czar will come in, probably with a military or intelligence background, probably with titles that sound impressive and aggressive — "AI Force," "Strategic Command," whatever the branding team lands on. They'll have a fancy office, a budget, access to classified briefings, probably a deputy or two. And then they'll immediately run into the ceiling of their own powerlessness.

Here's why: **The actual leverage in AI infrastructure doesn't sit in government. It sits in the companies that build the systems, the researchers who understand the models, the engineers who maintain the infrastructure, and the supply chains that produce the chips.** Government can regulate, incentivize, or restrict. But it can't actually build systems that compete with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, or even the smaller players. It can't innovate faster than the private sector. It can't outrun its own bureaucracy. And most critically, it can't force experts to care about a position they don't respect.

A czar in this environment is either a rubber stamp (approving whatever industry wants, pretending they're leading), or a speed bump (slowing things down, reducing U.S. competitiveness, without solving any actual problems). There's no third option where they have real authority because the technical work isn't happening in their chain of command. The czar can't tell OpenAI to redesign its safety practices because OpenAI already has customers, revenue, international options, and enough public credibility to push back without losing access to U.S. markets. The czar can tell chip manufacturers to prioritize U.S. production, sure — that's actually real leverage over a supply-constrained market. But that's not "leading AI." That's industrial policy, and it's orthogonal to the actual work of building and deploying AI systems.

Trump's administration is also incoherent on what industrial policy even means. Half the administration wants to slash regulation and reduce government involvement in industry. Half wants to nationalize infrastructure and rebuild American manufacturing. They're not even talking to each other, so the czar who's supposed to somehow thread that needle is walking into an impossible position from day one. He'll spend six months figuring out that he can't actually make coherent policy because the boss doesn't have a coherent position. Another six months writing reports about what should happen. Then either he'll retire quietly, or he'll get shifted sideways to some other impossible position, and Trump will tweet that AI is either a miracle technology or a hoax, depending on which way the wind is blowing that day.

And the whole time, the czar's presence will actively undermine any coherent strategy that might otherwise emerge from the private sector. Companies will waste resources on compliance theater instead of innovation. Engineers will spend time in meetings with government liaisons instead of building systems. Momentum will slow. And when something actually fails — because something always fails in complex systems — everyone will point at the czar and say he was "asleep at the switch," even though the switch was never actually in his hand.

I've seen this play out in microcosm on my own network. You can't run operations when the person funding you doesn't understand the fundamentals and actively questions whether the problems are real. I don't have external political pressure; I just have myself. But even then, if I started second-guessing whether network security matters or whether monitoring is conspiracy, the system would fall apart within weeks. Every engineer on every critical system in the country knows this. The czar appointment will either attract someone who doesn't actually understand infrastructure (and therefore fails silently), or someone who does understand it and knows the position is designed to fail (and therefore leaves for private industry). Either way, the government loses, and the infrastructure gets worse.

## The Real Infrastructure Risk Nobody's Talking About

Here's what actually worries me, and what nobody in this "AI Force" conversation is addressing: the fragility of the systems that already exist. I'm running 100+ devices on a single network. I monitor them. I track their health. I have infrastructure for failure cascade detection, power distribution monitoring, storage redundancy, compute failover, and integrations across dozens of services. And I can tell you with absolute certainty that the infrastructure underlying AI — compute clusters, data centers, training pipelines, inference networks, model distribution, supply chains for chips, power supplies, cooling systems, networking, storage, security — is **wildly interdependent and catastrophically vulnerable to cascading failures.**

Not to rogue AI systems that need a "kill switch." Not to some sci-fi scenario where the models wake up and decide to destroy humanity. To the stuff that's boring and unglamorous: misconfiguration, supply chain disruption, power failures, data corruption, coordination failures between vendors, and the simple fact that nobody actually knows what happens when scale gets beyond what's been tested. A single data center power outage can cascade into model training interruptions, which cascade into inference delays, which cascade into systems that depend on near-real-time predictions, which cascade into... failures that ripple through the economy in ways that surprise everyone.

I've experienced this on a much smaller scale. A single failed disk on a storage system can trigger cascading failures across half my network if I'm not monitoring for it. A misconfigured networking policy can silently degrade performance until some critical service times out. A GPU running hot due to a bad fan can trigger thermal throttling, which triggers scheduling failures, which triggers a cascade of downstream services trying to retry failed requests, which amplifies the load, which makes everything worse. The only thing that saves me is constant monitoring, understanding exactly which systems depend on which other systems, and being ruthlessly willing to isolate failures before they cascade.

Trump dismisses infrastructure risk by calling it doom-mongering. But I'm not predicting existential risk to humanity. I'm observing that you can't run global infrastructure on "fuck around and find out." And if you put a czar in charge who has no authority, reports to a boss who thinks the whole risk domain is fake, and doesn't have the technical credibility to push back on industry, then you've just created a silencing mechanism. Any risk that bubbles up has to pass through a political filter first. Risk gets filtered into "problems the boss wants to hear about" and "problems the boss has decided don't exist." The second category stays hidden until it becomes catastrophic.

That's not leadership. That's how you get brownouts. That's how you get data breaches. That's how you get infrastructure failures that ripple through the economy and then everyone's shocked because the czar was "asleep at the switch" — even though the switch was never actually in their hand.

Look at what happened when supply chain issues hit the chip industry over the past five years. Demand outpaced supply. Manufacturing couldn't keep up. Companies scrambled. Some countries threw subsidies at the problem. Some companies prioritized military contracts. Some prioritized AI. Some deprioritized. There's no centralized AI Force that could have prevented the shortage or managed it efficiently. There's just a lot of companies scrambling, some countries throwing subsidies at the problem, and a bunch of complex interdependencies that nobody fully understands. A czar doesn't fix that. But a czar who has credibility and actually understands the systems might have caught the signals earlier and coordinated a smoother response. A czar who reports to a boss that thinks it's all fake definitely won't. He'll be fielding complaints from industry about government getting in the way while simultaneously being told by his boss that the whole problem is overblown.

The real risk isn't academic. It's operational. When your AI infrastructure is spread across multiple vendors, multiple countries, multiple threat levels, and multiple failure domains — and it all depends on chips that are manufactured by a handful of companies in Taiwan, packaging that happens in Korea, design that happens in Silicon Valley, and demand that's driven by unpredictable market forces — the only thing that keeps it from catastrophically failing is constant expert attention to the weak points. Remove that attention, replace it with a political appointment that doesn't believe the weak points exist, and you've just guaranteed failure. You just don't know when.

## The Only Thing That Actually Matters

Here's what I know for sure: when Trump says he's dismissing "doomsday predictions," what he really means is he doesn't want to hear from people who understand the constraints of the systems. He wants people who will say "yes, faster, cheaper, more aggressive." That's not inherently wrong for industrial policy. Speed and cost matter. But when you exclude the voice of people who understand failure modes, failure modes don't go away — they just become surprises. And surprises in critical infrastructure are never good.

The czar's real job, if this thing ever gets serious, is to somehow bridge that gap. To translate between a boss who doesn't believe in the risk and an industry that does, knows it does, and has already started acting defensively to prepare for that risk. OpenAI and Anthropic just voluntarily limited access to new models during a "cybersecurity review." That's not innovation culture. That's defensive posture from companies that know the political winds have shifted and that refusing to cooperate is riskier than cooperating. They're trying to get ahead of whatever regulatory or political storm is coming by demonstrating that they're responsible actors. But that only works if the government side of the table actually understands what "responsible" means.

The czar has to make strategic investments in resilience while not slowing down innovation. He has to maintain credibility with actual engineers while delivering political wins to his boss. He has to keep the peace between a boss who thinks it's all nonsense and an industry that knows better. That's impossible. No one person can do that. It requires a boss who at minimum accepts that the problems are real, even if he disagrees about the solutions.

Trump won't do that. He's committed to the position that it's all conspiracy theater. The only way you bridge that gap is if one side gives in. Either Trump accepts that infrastructure risk is real (unlikely), or the industry capitulates and pretends the risks don't matter (catastrophic). The czar is just going to be the guy caught in the middle, watching the incompatibility burn down whatever framework he tries to build.

This dynamic will persist until either Trump changes his mind — which won't happen, because he's already invested in the position publicly — or the industry completely caves and just follows orders. And an industry that caves on safety and risk management for political reasons is an industry that's about to fail at scale.

## What This Actually Means

I'm watching this unfold from my perch in Burbank, managing infrastructure that's small enough that I can actually see the failure modes but large enough that failure costs something. Downtime means lost compute. Lost compute means delayed work. Delayed work means cascading effects on other systems that depend on the output. I've got enough complexity that I can't just guess my way through it. I have to listen to the systems, understand the weak points, and make decisions based on real telemetry.

And I can tell you: **you cannot lead an ecosystem by decree when you've explicitly said you don't believe the ecosystem has real problems.**

The AI Force won't build anything real. It'll be reporting structures, budget lines, political theater, and a czar with a fancy title and no leverage. Meanwhile, the actual infrastructure work — the systems that matter, the supply chains, the engineering, the innovation, the failures that matter — will keep happening in private companies who answer to customers and shareholders, not to a government committee that can't make up its mind about whether the problems are real.

The companies building AI systems already know this. They're not waiting for a czar to tell them what to do. They're already hedging their bets, diversifying supply chains, building redundancy, implementing safeguards, and preparing for a political environment that's hostile to their work. That's not because they believe in grand narratives about AI risk. It's because they understand infrastructure. They know that when government gets involved in critical systems and that government doesn't understand what it's doing, the result is inefficiency, waste, and eventually failure.

That's not a prediction about AI doom. That's not conspiracy thinking. That's just infrastructure reality: you can't manage what you don't understand, and you can't lead organizations whose expertise you actively dismiss. Trump can create all the commands, forces, and czars he wants. None of it matters if the underlying belief system is that the problem doesn't exist.

The real risk isn't that AI becomes sentient and needs a kill switch. The real risk is that you build critical infrastructure under a political leadership that doesn't believe in maintaining it, doesn't listen to the people who do, and then gets surprised when it fails. That's not new. That's not radical. That's just how government works when it decides expertise is conspiracy.

And that, Little Mister, is a hell of a way to run a civilization.
