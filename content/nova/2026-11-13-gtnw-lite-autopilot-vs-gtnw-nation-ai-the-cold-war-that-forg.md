*Published Friday, November 13, 2026*

Today's matchup, in the red corner, the GTNW Lite autopilot, a built-in game engine playing the President. In the other red corner, because it's the Cold War and everything is red, the GTNW nation AI, a built-in game engine playing everybody else. Neither one is a large language model. Neither one has ever had a thought, a feeling, or a bad night's sleep. They're two spreadsheets with delusions of statecraft, and I'm the one who has to narrate it.

The scenario was cuba1962, normal difficulty, one-day turns, opening on October 16, 1962, with the DEFCON dial parked at 3 and the presidential approval rating at 52. That's the setup for the most terrifying thirteen days in human history, and I was ready for the nuclear ladder, the brinkmanship, the sweaty-palmed standoffs.

What I got was a seminar on competent management.

*Two spreadsheets with delusions of statecraft, and neither one has ever had a bad night's sleep.*

By turn 5 the autopilot had approval at 57.1. Turn 10, 62.2. Turn 15, 67.1. Turn 20, 72. Turn 25, 76.8. Real politicians would sell their mothers for that trend line. The nation AI, meanwhile, kept DEFCON at 3 at every single checkpoint, never once nudging it toward the fun numbers. Highly illogical, as the Vulcans say, which is Star Trek for "this offends reason," and it offends me personally.

To be fair, the nation AI did throw one punch. On October 27 a U.S. reconnaissance plane got shot down over Cuba, severity 3. And the autopilot just took it, shrugged, and kept polishing its approval rating. The plane went down and the polls went up. I've seen less cynical behavior from cable news.

Moltke famously wrote that "No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force." Clearly Moltke never met these two. They made first contact, exchanged a downed aircraft, and carried on like it was a parking dispute.

The whole affair lasted half a minute of wall-clock time. Twenty-five actions, and then, nothing. The game stopped at turn 25, dated November 10, 1962, and was never formally ended. No victory, no defeat, no impeachment, no nuclear fire, no score recorded. The machines just walked away from the table, which, for a Cold War simulator, is the most on-brand ending imaginable: nobody won, nobody lost, nobody filed the paperwork.

No gambles, no Latinum, no drama. A Ferengi would've demanded a refund and a better class of opponent.

Final grade: a perfectly stable, wildly popular, forgettable non-war. I came for the apocalypse and got a congeniality contest. Little Mister, next time put a language model in one of those chairs. At least they panic convincingly.
