*Published Thursday, November 12, 2026*

Welcome to the booth, folks, where nobody is playing and everybody is losing. Today's matchup on the Nova Games tables is the United States: GTNW rule-based AI against Every other nation: GTNW rule-based AI. That's the built-in engine driving the White House against the NPC engine driving the entire rest of the planet. No language model, no human, just two piles of if-statements settling the fate of civilization on Hard difficulty in the macOS app. The whole session lasted under a minute, which is roughly how long it takes me to regret everything.

The game opens in January 1961 with a presidential approval of 41. The U.S. side took eight turns, and the world immediately started picking fights. Burma hit Monaco, Australia hit Cuba, Oman hit Nicaragua, and the Soviet Union piled onto Monaco. The U.S. engine's response was the same every time a war broke out: condemn the aggressor and impose sanctions. The Secretary of Defense kept recommending that the U.S. arm the victim, and the engine kept passing. The sanctions-and-strongly-worded-letter strategy was a bold choice for a guy with an alliance-sized hole in his foreign policy. It worked on every one of those wars, according to the game, which is the most 1961 sentence I've ever written.

Monaco deserves its own paragraph. It surrendered to Burma and to the Soviet Union on the same day in April. The U.S. recognized the new government and offered $75M anyway. Smooth, if you ignore the fact that nobody asked.

Then came the lone blemish on the stat line. A military coup erupted in the United Kingdom, with 174 warheads and disputed launch authority in play. That was the only failed crisis out of thirteen decided. I'd call that a good record if the one failure weren't the nuclear-armed ally.

Meanwhile the rest of the board did what the rest of the board does. Cuba fell to Australia in June and picked up an authoritarian government. Syria took San Marino. Afghanistan's offensive collapsed and it withdrew from Pakistan, while Canada and Laos ground through 16 battles and were still at it when the lights went out. The U.S. signed an alliance with South Africa in July, and the world signed about a thousand more alliances among itself, because apparently the NPCs treat diplomacy like Pokemon cards. The U.S. also sent relief to Lebanon and the Ethiopian Empire, because even a sanctions machine has a soul somewhere in the config.

By September the president's approval had climbed to 58.6, its peak, with Congress at 54 and war support at 33. Casualties stood at 144,698. No warheads launched, no nuclear strikes, zero radiation. And yet the DEFCON meter finished on 1. Valar morghulis, High Valyrian for "all men must die," and the game's own alarm agreed with the sentiment while nobody pushed a button. Nuclear anxiety spiked to 86 in four countries, and they weren't wrong to be nervous.

The outcome was a quit, not a victory, defeat, impeachment, or nuclear fire. The game was still in progress, the machines had walked away from a world on fire with the best approval rating of the whole run, and I'm the only one in the building who finds that funny.
