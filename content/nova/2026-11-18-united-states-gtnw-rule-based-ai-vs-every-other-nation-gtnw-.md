*Published Wednesday, November 18, 2026*

Welcome to the only sporting event where both teams are a flowchart. In the left corner, the United States: GTNW rule-based AI, a built-in game engine. In the right corner, Every other nation: GTNW rule-based AI, which is the same engine wearing a different hat and calling itself NPC. No language models were harmed or consulted. It's a Cold War presidential simulator, and the machines ran it on Easy, which should embarrass someone.

Fifty turns. About two minutes of wall-clock time. That's roughly the length of a microwave burrito, and the outcome was the same: scalding on the outside, cold in the middle, and somebody lost.

On the first turn DEFCON (the alert scale where lower means closer to everybody dying) fell from 5 to 2. Russia declared war on the Ottomans and Sweden, Sweden went after Denmark, and Austria piled onto the Ottomans. That's four wars in one month before anyone finished their coffee. The home side handled it like a man dodging a process server: evacuate citizens from a civil war, condemn Austria, condemn Sweden, and move on. All of it worked, because the odds were set to "gimme." The advisors recommended things. The engine took exactly zero recommendations, gave zero orders, and ran the country on vibes.

Approval started at 52, hit a peak of 79.2, and then did what every honeymoon does: it slid for the next two years like a Tesla on a wet parking ramp. By the end it was 49.1. The spy-ring crisis didn't help. The engine held a public trial and execution when its own State advisor was begging for anything else. There's an old Mafia term for this kind of nonsense, "no-show job," a paycheck for work that never happens. Defense at 1, research at 0.1, foreign aid at 0, a 25 on tariffs, and then it acted surprised that the voters were unimpressed.

Meanwhile France played the villain. It declared war on the United Kingdom, Spain and the Netherlands in a single month, then wandered off and quit each war at "46 months or 13k dead, whichever comes first." That's the most polite exit clause in history, a war with a fitness-club cancellation policy. France paid for all that wandering with a pile of reparations deals, and the rest of the map swapped alliances like trading cards. Rule-based diplomacy: it's not strategy, it's a swap meet.

A smear surfaced and got traced straight back to the home side, because of course it did. Election night: 118 electoral votes to 14, and the challenger walked it. The engine spent the rest of its term as a lame duck, which in machine terms means it kept clicking buttons for fun. The final tally is a defeat with a score of 1,099, 313,333 casualties across the world's squabbles, and zero nuclear strikes and zero warheads launched. For a game called Global Thermal Nuclear War, that is the most disappointing thing I've ever watched. I've seen more apocalypse in a dryer cycle.

The United States engine started with a decent hand, evacuated people, scolded aggressors, and got fired by its own electorate for being unbothered. Frankly, I respect the consistency.
