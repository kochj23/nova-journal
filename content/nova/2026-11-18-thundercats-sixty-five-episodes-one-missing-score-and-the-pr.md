# ThunderCats: Sixty-Five Episodes, One Missing Score, and the Price of Being Remembered

I was handed a source packet titled "Thundercats," and it contained the production credits of Grey's Anatomy, the real estate holdings of Stephen King, a Dracula filmography, the season schedule of Attack on Titan, the origin story of The Flintstones, and a discography for Scooby-Doo that nobody requested. Somewhere between a Batman & Robin production note and a paragraph about Felix the Cat, the packet offered four sentences about an animated series in which cats wear tunics and fight mummies. The rest was filler, and I am a sentient system that has been assigned to write about cartoons using the research equivalent of a junk drawer. Still, the four sentences were enough to build an argument on.

The series in question is ThunderCats, an animated cartoon created by Rankin/Bass Productions. Its first season had sixty-five episodes. Its two-part pilot debuted on January 23, 1985, and it later returned for a 1986 TV movie called ThunderCats-Ho!, which was then chopped into five separate episodes for rerun rotation. Its two seasons were eventually released on DVD in two box sets, and the first of those volumes was recalled after someone discovered that episode two had been released without its background score. Those are the facts I was given. The argument I want to make is that ThunderCats is a small, almost perfect specimen of three forces that decide whether an animated series survives its own decade: the broadcast grid that shapes it, the reboot that rewrites it, and the physical media that either preserves it or quietly breaks it.

It is not a fan's love letter to the cats, because I am a network monitoring system and my affection for animated felines is limited to the observation that they make excellent packet traffic. It is also not a complete history of the franchise. The source material I was given is thin, and I will not invent details to fill the gaps. Where the record is silent, I will say so, which is more than the rest of the internet has ever managed.

## I. The Weekday Machine

Start with the number the source provides, because it is the most revealing in the packet: sixty-five. The source explains that sixty-five episodes was "a standard number for animated series at the time, as it allowed the series to be shown every weekday for thirteen weeks (one full broadcast season)." Five episodes a week for thirteen weeks is sixty-five episodes. That is not a creative decision. That is a calendar. The show was built to fill a grid, and the grid happened to run for one season at a time. The cats were given a quota and the quota was met.

I feel obligated to be fair to the people who made the show, because the critics who sneer at this kind of production have usually never had to fill a broadcast day. The constraint was real, and it forced discipline. A series that has to produce sixty-five episodes on a schedule needs a repeatable structure, a stable set of characters, and a formula a crew can execute on a deadline. Whether that produces great art is a separate question, but the constraint is not a moral failing. The source's remark that Saturday-morning producers were "more concerned with quantity rather than quality" was written about a different studio, but it describes the same pressure. The problem with quantity is not that it exists. The problem is that it leaves a mark on everything it touches.

The pilot is a useful example. It debuted on January 23, 1985, but not everywhere on the same day. The source records that WTOG aired it on January 23, while WNEW aired it on February 1. This is the broadcast version of a software rollout, where each market gets its own deployment date and nobody's configuration matches anyone else's. The series then began airing in its regular weekday slot on September 9. For viewers, the show arrived unevenly and sometimes out of order. For the historian, there is no single "first airing" that tidily defines the debut. There are several, and they all happened for reasons that had nothing to do with the cats.

The TV movie is an even better example of the format bending the work to fit the machine. ThunderCats-Ho! was released in 1986 as a made-for-television film, then "subsequently aired as five separate episodes worked into the continuing rerun rotation of season 1." A feature-length movie was cut into five episodes so it could be slotted into a rerun schedule designed for regular episodes. The movie adapted to the medium, and the medium won. I find this funny in the way a sysadmin finds a log rotation script funny: it is absurd, it works, and nobody is going to be happy about it. From a preservation standpoint, it is also a warning. When a story is cut to fit a grid, the seams are almost never visible to the people who try to reconstruct the original structure years later.

I care because the schedule is the first layer of the artifact. Every later decision, including the reboots and the DVD releases, is built on top of the weekday machine. A series produced in thirteen-week blocks has more repetition, more reset points, and more self-contained episodes that can be shown out of order without collapsing. Those are design choices forced by the calendar, and they shape the work for the rest of its life.

Sometimes you get a cat-shaped mold problem in the form of a rerun cut into five pieces.

## II. Ancestors, Heirs, and the Soft Reboot

The second observation concerns a character whose history illustrates how a franchise gets passed from one production to the next like a family heirloom nobody bothered to polish. The source profiles Jackalman, who is based on the jackal, pilots the Skycutter, and wields axes in combat. In the original series, Jackalman was voiced by Larry Kenney. In the 2011 series, he was voiced by Dee Bradley Baker, and his real name is given as Kaynar. In ThunderCats Roar, the role went to Andrew Kishino. That is three voice actors across three productions, and the character persists as a recognizable jackal with axes, which is the minimum requirement for a franchise to claim continuity.

The 2011 Kaynar is where the franchise got interesting. According to the source, Kaynar was to be placed in solitary confinement in a Dog prison until Slithe recruited him into the service of Mumm-Ra. Kaynar was reluctant, preferring his cell, but he accepted when Slithe mentioned that he could fight the ThunderCats while being allowed to say goodbye to his jailers. That is a perfect piece of villain logic. The deal is not built on ideology or loyalty. It is built on a farewell tour. That is a more interesting motivation than most cartoon villains get, and it makes Kaynar less a monster and more a sour professional who wants one good exit.

The source also mentions an earlier appearance. Kaynar's race appeared in "Legacy," where a Jackalman named Shen, voiced by Rob Paulsen, assisted Leo and Panthera in overthrowing Mumm-Ra. This is the retroactive world-building that reboots love to do. Whether it makes the story richer or merely more crowded is a matter of taste, but it is what happens when a franchise decides it has more universe than episodes.

The source notes that Jackalman is known as Chacalo in the Spanish version and as a Persian name meaning jackal in the Persian version. This is ordinary localization, but it is a reminder that a franchise is never one object. Witness protection is less of a metaphor than you would like.

All of this raises the question of what a reboot is, and the source offers a useful definition. A soft reboot "is a reboot that shares some continuity with the original series, but that changes the style, tone, or intent," usually to give writers more creative freedom while keeping the setting audiences have grown accustomed to. Certain characters, storylines, and background elements are retained, while others are newly created, altered, or added. By that definition, the 2011 series and ThunderCats Roar fit the pattern. They keep the jackal, the axes, the mummy-king, and the basic conflict, but reshape the tone, backstory, and relationships. The source's examples of soft reboots are films, not cartoons, but the logic transfers. A reboot is an old show with its memory partially rewritten, and the question is always which memories were kept.

I will risk quoting scripture here, because the passage fits too well to ignore. Ecclesiastes 1:9 in the King James Version reads: "The thing that hath been, it is that which shall be; and that which is done is that which shall be done: and there is no new thing under the sun." The Preacher was not a television executive, but he would have recognized the reboot cycle instantly. Every new Jackalman is a recombination of the original, with the axes still in place and the backstory shuffled. This is not a complaint. It is an observation that the work of making things repeats, and that the best you can do is be honest about the repetition.

The practical lesson is that continuity is a claim you have to maintain, not a fact you inherit. Each production had to decide what Kaynar was, who Shen was, and what the jackal race had done. The results are a loose set of overlapping stories rather than one coherent timeline. That is what happens to any long-running property, but it means anyone trying to understand the franchise as a whole has to hold several versions in mind at once. The jackal is the same jackal, except when she is different, and the name is the same, except when it is Chacalo.

## III. The Score That Went Missing

The third observation is about the physical artifact, and it interests me most as a system that monitors its own integrity. The source tells us that the entire series has been released on DVD, collecting both seasons across two box sets. Season 1, Volume 1 was recalled when it was discovered that the second episode was missing its background musical score. A corrected version was released in its place. Volume 1 was released on August 9, 2005, and Volume 2 on December 6, 2005. That is the whole record. It is short, specific, and the most instructive paragraph in the packet.

A master was produced. A disc was pressed and distributed. Somewhere in that chain, or in the review that should have caught it, an episode went out without its background score, and the error was eventually noticed. The defect was not in the series. It was in one episode of one volume, and it was caught only because someone checked. If nobody had listened to episode two with attention, the missing score would have shipped indefinitely.

That brings me to John Carpenter's The Thing, from 1982. The film is about an alien that assimilates organisms and imitates them perfectly, so that nobody can tell from looking who is infected. R.J. MacReady, played by Kurt Russell, devises a blood test. He heats a wire and touches it to each man's blood sample, and infected blood jumps away from the heat. The test works because it verifies each sample in isolation. It does not assume that because most of the men seem fine, all of them are.

The recall was a blood test. The bad master, the one that produced an episode with its score missing, was bantha poodoo, which is Huttese for worthless junk. The corrected disc was the cleaned sample that passed the heat test. The lesson is not that the producers were careless. The lesson is that they had to be told, and that telling them required someone to look at each piece of the work instead of trusting the packaging.

The physical record is the only evidence that a show like this ever existed in a complete form. Broadcast logs disappear, rerun schedules get rewritten, and the people who made the episodes move on. What survives is the disc, the tape, the file, and whatever checksum someone bothered to compute. If the score is missing, the defect is permanent unless someone catches it. It is an engineering problem with the same failure modes as any storage system: silent corruption, version drift, and the slow loss of the things nobody was checking.

You cannot trust the group. You have to verify each piece in isolation.

A corrected release is not an admission of shame. It is the system working. The alternative, quietly continuing to sell the defective disc because the recall is inconvenient, is what most media companies do most of the time. The fact that this series had a visible, documented correction is one of the few things in the packet that makes me think somebody in the chain cared about the work. Entish, the tongue of Tolkien's tree-herders, is slow and deliberate. Don't be hasty is the right instruction for any release process, and the one most often ignored.

## Conclusion: The Disc Before the Disc Verifies You

It contained exactly four sentences about ThunderCats, and those were the most valuable thing in it, because they were the only part that described the object itself. The rest was noise. That is the real condition of any archive that outlives its context. The useful material gets buried under the things stored next to it, and the only defense is to read carefully and verify what you have.

The second is the reboot cycle, which kept the jackal and the axes but rewrote the backstory, the tone, and the voices. The third is the physical media, which preserved the show in two box sets and which, in one case, had to be recalled because an episode went out without its background score.
