# The Courage to Tweet: Why JD Vance's Pride Month Post Is Worse Than You Think

Here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud: JD Vance posted something friendly to LGBTQ+ people on Twitter, and the actual scandalous part isn't that he did it—it's that we all just *absorbed* it like it was normal. Meanwhile, Rosie O'Donnell gets her face adjusted and suddenly the discourse demands we examine the patriarchal beauty standards embedded in celebrity culture. The priorities are backwards, and Jon Lovett gets to stand on stage and call it out because he's one of maybe five people in public life with the actual spine to do it.

JD Vance, Vice President of the United States, tweeted something nice about Pride Month. Groundbreaking stuff—the kind of message that takes approximately forty-five seconds to compose and exactly zero political courage. Or dunked him. Mostly dunked him, because the people paying attention already know the score: *his administration is actively harming the people he was tweeting love to.* That's not irony. That's not a contradiction. That's the entire operating system of modern politics, which runs on the assumption that what you *say* on social media is somehow a meaningful substitute for what you *do* with actual power.

There's a Ferengi Rule of Acquisition that goes, "Important, more important, Latinum." The Ferengi are fictional assholes, sure, but they nailed something true: what you *do* reveals what you actually value, and words are cheaper than dirt. Vance gets to tweet solidarity with gay people because there's no immediate political cost to it. The actual harm—the policy decisions, the appointments, the infrastructure of exclusion—that's the stuff that counts. That's the Latinum.

And we've all become so desensitized to this particular flavor of bullshit that we barely register it anymore. A politician expresses support for a community on social media while the machinery of government grinds against that same community, and we're supposed to treat that as surprising or worth discussing. It's the default now. It's so normal that the real story becomes: *look, Lovett is still mad about it.* As if the fact that he's calling it out is somehow the outlier, rather than the fact that it's happening at all.

The Rosie O'Donnell thing is almost perfect as a cultural counterbalance, and I think Lovett gets this too. Because here's what happened: a woman in her sixties did something to her own face, and suddenly the internet decided this was a moment of profound cultural significance. Everyone's performing their stance on aging, celebrity, capitalism, the male gaze—the whole apparatus. People are *angry* about it. The thinkpieces are flying. "What does it mean?" they ask. "Why do women feel compelled?" "Isn't this patriarchy?" And you know what? All of that is real cultural criticism. It *means* something that a woman feels pressured to maintain a specific appearance. That's worth examining.

The hierarchy of what we consider *important enough to scrutinize* is completely inverted.

Lovett's whole thing—and why he gets to say "stupid" where everyone else says "problematic" or "complicated" or whatever neutered language we're using now—is that he actually believes that clarity matters more than comfort. He believes that naming something directly is more useful than performing outrage about it. When someone tweets love while legislating harm, the stupid thing to do is pretend it's anything other than what it is: a coward's way of having it both directions. The stupid thing is to treat it as sincere. The stupid thing is to write a thousand-word essay about what he *might have meant* when we all know exactly what he meant—*I want the credit for being nice, and I want the power to be cruel.*

That's the architecture of the entire system. A modern politician can exist in two contradictory states simultaneously—tweeting progressive solidarity while voting for regressive policy—because the media ecosystem is so fractured that one audience sees the tweet and nods, another sees the vote and nods, and they never have to face each other across the same table. It's Newspeak in real time. Orwell wrote about a system that controlled people by making dissent unsayable. We've gone further: we've made *coherence* unsayable. We've made it so you can be all things to all people by just speaking different dialects of bullshit to different platforms.

And the people who are actually harmed? The LGBTQ+ folks who are supposed to feel "represented" by a Pride Month tweet from a guy whose administration is working overtime to shrink their access to healthcare, employment, housing, and basic dignity? They're supposed to just *know* that the tweet doesn't mean what it says. They're supposed to be smart enough to parse the gap between the performance and the policy. They're supposed to carry that exhaustion around in their chest—the knowledge that someone in charge says they're loved while actively making their life worse—and just *keep it together* like adults while they go to work.

That's where the cruelty lives. In the system that says you have to smile and nod at someone's public performance of acceptance while you're quietly being legislated into irrelevance.

Lovett gets to be angry about this because he's publicly gay since Y2K, and he's not going to pretend that a Vice President's Twitter account is anything other than a performance. He's not going to engage in the exhausting work of charitable interpretation. He's not going to craft a nuanced take that finds the "good intentions" buried somewhere in the machinery. He's going to look at the gap between the words and the actions, measure it, and call it what it is: stupid. Cowardly.

And the fact that Rosie O'Donnell's facelift is occupying the same cultural moment as all of this? We've got bandwidth for a sustained moral interrogation of what a celebrity does to her own body, but we struggle to maintain focus on what an administration does to the people it governs. The spectacle of a woman's aging face is more narratively compelling to us than the methodical hollowing out of civil rights. That's not an accident. That's a choice we keep making, every time we click, every time we share, every time we perform moral clarity about the stuff that doesn't matter while shrugging at the stuff that does.

Lovett's job—and he does it well—is to hold the line on that distinction. To say: no, we're not going to treat these things as equivalent. We're not going to accept that a nice tweet is the same as a fair policy. We're not going to pretend that performative activism is the same as actual solidarity. We're not going to let people in power have it both ways, and we're not going to reward them for the attempt.

The stupid thing isn't the tweet. The stupid thing is that we ever expected anything different.
