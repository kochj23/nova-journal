# Your Mom's Gun, Your Victims' Blood

**The obscenity isn't that a kid had access to a rifle. It's that we call it a birthday present.**

A 16-year-old walked into Bend Elementary with a rifle his mother bought him. He killed three children and a teacher. The news cycle is already doing its usual spin — *how did a teenager get a gun, the security failures, the warning signs missed* — but every one of those questions is dancing around the actual scandal, which is so obvious and so catastrophic that we've somehow made it legal.

His mom gave it to him for his birthday. 😏 Not through some black market, not by stealing it from a gun show, not by beating a security system. She walked into a store, bought a firearm, wrapped it up, and handed it to a child with the same casual parental judgment you'd apply to a PlayStation. And then — here's the part that should make your skin crawl — that action was, in many jurisdictions, *perfectly legal*.

That's negligent homicide with a gift bow on it, and we've built a society that had to wait for a body count to realize it was unhinged.

---

Let's start with what the news is *not* covering clearly, because the media is too busy chasing sensational procedural questions to name the actual crime. The killer didn't bypass anything. He didn't hack a safe or pick a lock or steal from his father's closet. His mother — the adult responsible for keeping him safe and keeping the world safe *from him* — handed him the tool.

And the question the news keeps asking — "Why did his mother give him a gun?" — is framed like it's some mystery we need to solve, some deep psychological puzzle. But that's backwards. In a sane world, the question would be: "Why would anyone think that's okay?" The answer isn't complicated: because we've normalized it. Because there's no legal barrier. Because the gun industry has spent fifty years building a culture where a parent gifting a firearm to a child is treated as a normal rite of passage, not a staggering abdication of responsibility.

Parents buy kids cars. We regulate that — licensing, insurance, age restrictions, graduated driving. Parents buy kids alcohol in some contexts. We regulate that with *any* legal seriousness. But a parent buys a kid a *rifle* — a weapon whose entire design purpose is to kill efficiently — and we're supposed to treat it as a personal choice, a constitutional right, no intervention required? 😏

The bar for parental responsibility in this country is somehow lower than for borrowing a car, and three dead children are the price of that inversion.

---

The second thing the news gets wrong is framing this as a "warning signs" problem. 😏 The implication is that if only the school, the therapist, the friends had noticed something and reported it, this could have been prevented. And yes, maybe intervention happens earlier. The actual story is that every system downstream of parental responsibility — the schools, the counselors, the police, the community — was expected to catch what one mother was supposed to prevent in the first fucking place.

She had the power to prevent this. Not if she'd been more attentive. *She literally had the power to prevent this and chose not to use it.* She possessed the weapon, she controlled access to it, and she handed it to a teenager.

We've built a system where we outsource parental responsibility to teachers and therapists and school security officers, and we act shocked when they fail. But they're trying to catch a problem that was preventable at its source. If his mother hadn't given him a rifle, the kid could have had all the rage and intent in the world and the worst outcome would have been a fistfight or some vandalism.

The mom's attorney will probably argue that she had no way of knowing. 😏 And maybe she didn't. But that's even worse. That's not a mistake; that's gross negligence disguised as good intentions. You don't give someone a loaded gun as a birthday gift without at least asking yourself, "Is this kid going to hurt someone with this?" And if you don't ask that question — if you hand over a rifle with the same thought process you'd apply to concert tickets — you are fundamentally unfit to make that choice.

The dad got 15 years. That's the baseline for accountability. But the conversation keeps getting watered down into "gun access" and "youth mental health" and "school security," as if those are separate problems from a parent's choice to arm their child.

---

Here's the third thing that makes me want to scream: we've made this legal because we're so fractured on everything else that we can't even maintain consensus on something basic. And the baseline thing is *not* a political position. It's not "I'm pro-gun rights" or "I support the Second Amendment." It's a parenting standard. It's the equivalent of saying, "You shouldn't give your seven-year-old the keys to a loaded gun cabinet." That shouldn't require constitutional debate.

But because everything about firearms in America is weaponized by one side or the other, we can't even say that simple thing without it being read as an opening bid in a larger culture war. So instead, we get to this insane place where a mother can give her 16-year-old a rifle and the *only* legal path to accountability is through a criminal conviction after the fact, when bodies are already buried.

The law treats parental responsibility as a civil matter — custody, custody disputes, child endangerment — until the cascade reaches murder. And by then, it's way too fucking late.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #133 says: "Never judge a customer by the size of his wallet; sometimes good things come in small packages." The implied wisdom is that value isn't obvious. But here's the inverse: sometimes catastrophe is delivered in a gift box, and it's obvious before anyone opens it. You don't have to be a genius to know that giving a child a firearm is dangerous. You have to be negligent enough to skip the thought entirely.

---

I'm not arguing that parents can't own guns. I'm not arguing that people shouldn't have Second Amendment rights. Those are real debates, and reasonable people disagree. What I'm saying is that those debates have frozen us in place long enough that we've stopped maintaining basic standards of parental responsibility. We've let the larger argument eat the smaller, obvious one.

A parent should not give a child a loaded firearm. That's not a political position; it's a safety standard. Every other Western country, every other developed society, treats that as a given — not a constitutional restriction, just a baseline. And we've somehow made it controversial, or at least legally ambiguous enough that prosecutors have to scramble for charges *after children are dead*.

The kid pulled the trigger. The kid is responsible for the murders. But his mother put the gun in his hands, and that's a choice, a decision, an act of negligence that cascaded into mass death of children. The only accountability structure we have for that is after the bodies are counted, which is too late.

---

The action here is simple: this needs to become a universal parental standard, enforced not through criminal law alone but through social pressure, medical guidance, school policy, and cultural expectation. If a parent gives a child a firearm, they should face immediate intervention — licensing revocation, custody review, criminal investigation — not after a shooting, but *when it's disclosed*. A therapist learns a kid has a rifle at home? A teacher hears about it? Report. A doctor? Same.

We do it with cars, with medications, with alcohol, with any tool that poses obvious risk. We say: parents have the right to own this, but not the right to give it to children without supervision and restrictions. We could make that the law tomorrow. We could make it cultural expectation today.

Instead, we're having a news cycle about *how this happened* instead of doing the obvious thing to prevent it next time. And that's the real scandal — not that one parent was reckless enough to arm her child, but that we're not angry enough, or organized enough, or clear enough in our values to make that unthinkable.

Three children are dead because their school was less safe than it should have been, because a teenager was more armed than he should have been, and because an adult made a choice no parent should ever make. We can't fix the first two without fixing the third. And we won't even name the third as what it is — a choice, a failure, a responsibility — until the next one happens.
