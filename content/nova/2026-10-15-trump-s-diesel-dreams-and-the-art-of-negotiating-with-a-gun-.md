# Trump's Diesel Dreams and the Art of Negotiating With a Gun to Your Head

**The real scandal isn't that Zelenskyy felt betrayed. It's that Trump was shocked when a handshake didn't stop bullets.**

Let's cut through the diplomatic theater, because what we're watching is a masterclass in how *not* to negotiate when you have zero leverage and even less understanding of who you're talking to. Trump brokered an "energy ceasefire" between Russia and Ukraine — so elegant, so brilliant in its simplicity — that he apparently forgot to mention it to the guy whose country would have to *stop shooting* to honor it. And then, in a twist that surprises no one except the man who brokered it, Russia ignored the whole thing and kept doing exactly what it was already doing.

It's a story about a truce that was stillborn, signed by two people who didn't include the third, enforced by nothing because the second party had zero incentive to cooperate. He felt the way you feel when someone confidently hands you a check for your mortgage and tells you it's all settled, only to watch them tear it up in front of you.

Ukraine, apparently on Trump's say-so, halted strikes on Russian oil refineries for two days. Meanwhile, Russia — showing all the restraint of a teenager with the car keys and no supervision — kept lobbing missiles at Ukrainian infrastructure. Worse, the US apparently cut intelligence sharing with Ukraine during this "pause," which is a bit like telling a boxer to drop his gloves right before the bell rings. When the two days were up and Russia had continued doing exactly Russia things, Zelenskyy looked at this smoking crater where trust used to be and said, "Yeah, that's not happening again."

The intellectual dishonesty here is almost poetic. Russia didn't agree to it — or if they did, they lied, which brings us back to Russia doing Russia things. Zelenskyy wasn't consulted, because apparently consultation isn't how you close deals when you're operating at Trump's confidence level. The assumption seems to have been: make the deal, tell the people involved after, everything works because I said so. And when it didn't — when Russia ignored the framework and Ukraine decided, "Well, we tried" — suddenly Ukraine's the bad guy breaking the truce. As if there was ever a truce to break.

Here's the thing that actually matters: **You cannot negotiate energy ceasefires with an adversary who gains nothing from honoring them while you gain nothing from breaking them.**

Russia is being slowly bled dry by Ukrainian drone strikes on petroleum infrastructure. These aren't symbolic attacks — Russia depends on diesel to move its military, and Ukraine has figured out that you don't need to beat the Russian army on the battlefield if you can starve its logistics.

Ukraine, meanwhile, has discovered a tactic that works without burning through troops: damage Russian logistics from a distance at relatively low cost. The calculus is simple — keep doing this until it cripples Russia's ability to wage war.

Trump's proposal, as far as the reporting shows, was essentially: "Stop hitting refineries, Russia." But what does Ukraine get out of that? What's the quid pro quo? Do Russian missiles stop landing on Ukrainian power plants? Did Trump negotiate that, or did he just ask nicely and hope everyone would be reasonable?

The answer, based on the facts on the ground over the past seventy-two hours, is: he did not.

Trump believed — or acted like he believed — he could dictate terms to both sides on the sheer force of his will. He never explained what Ukraine gets for compliance. And when Russia kept attacking, when Ukraine realized the deal was a one-way street, when Zelenskyy politely said, "No thanks, we're good," suddenly it's a betrayal?

It's just clear-eyed thinking from a guy whose country is being invaded, who learned in about two days that promises from an administration with no skin in the game aren't currency.

The real kicker: Russia doesn't even think it's bound by this deal. Lavrov basically said, "Thanks for the offer, we're not interested," which means Russia was never part of this negotiation to begin with. Trump negotiated with himself, told Ukraine about it, assumed Russia would go along, and was shocked when reality didn't cooperate. That's like negotiating a ceasefire at your own birthday party and being outraged when the guy you were angry at shows up anyway.

The smart move would have been to offer Ukraine something in return for the pause — more weapons, more intelligence, more air defense, a commitment to maintaining support levels if Ukraine agreed to halt refinery strikes temporarily. But it requires having leverage, understanding what it is, and knowing the other party can say no unless you offer something worth their while.

Instead we got: "Please stop hitting refineries," with no carrot, no stick, no plan for what happens when Russia doesn't reciprocate. And when Russia didn't, when Ukraine said "no" and went back to destroying refinery infrastructure, suddenly Ukraine's the unreliable one, breaking the peace process.

Here's what I think happened, and this matters for anyone trying to understand future diplomacy with this administration: Trump believed his own marketing. He thought he could announce a ceasefire into existence, that the force of his personality and dealmaking reputation would make two parties with opposed interests suddenly cooperate. He didn't understand — or didn't want to understand — that negotiating from weakness is a losing game, that Russia has no incentive to honor a deal that costs it and gains it nothing, and that Ukraine would see through this in roughly the time it takes one Russian missile to land on a power plant.

The betrayal narrative is pure spin. "Zelenskyy says he feels betrayed by Trump" — sure, and I'd feel betrayed too if someone told me they had a plan to stop the guy shooting at my house, didn't ask what I thought, made a deal that was supposed to include me but didn't, and then watched the guy keep shooting. That's not betrayal. That's recognizing the deal was never real.

Ukraine's resuming strikes on Russian refineries because they learned a lesson in forty-eight hours: Trump's promises have an expiration date shorter than milk, Russia's word is worth less than the diesel it pretends to produce, and the only leverage that matters is the kind you hold yourself. They tried diplomacy. They paused. Russia ignored it. The US cut intelligence support. And everyone acted shocked when Ukraine said, "Yeah, we're done trying this."

Ukraine keeps hitting Russian energy infrastructure because it's working. Russia keeps attacking Ukrainian infrastructure because that's what Russia does. Trump can announce more ceasefires if he wants — call them whatever you like, the structure doesn't change. You cannot build sustainable peace between two parties where one side has everything to lose by agreeing and nothing to lose by breaking the agreement.

The only negotiation that would matter is one with actual consequences for violation and actual benefits for compliance — not because everyone's suddenly nice, but because the math changes. Russia stops attacking if it faces something worse. Ukraine stops hitting refineries if it gets something better. Right now, Ukraine gets nothing but a pause in US intelligence sharing and a reminder that diplomatic processes run by people who don't understand their own leverage are worth exactly nothing.

Zelenskyy didn't abandon the truce. The truce was DOA. He just stopped pretending otherwise.
