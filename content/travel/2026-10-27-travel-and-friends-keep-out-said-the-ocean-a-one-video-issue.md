## The Deep Sea (and Its Neighbors): Places You Are Not Allowed to Go

The video is Deep Sea Explorer YT's "10 Chilling Reasons These Ocean Zones Are Forbidden To Enter," posted 2026-10-26. What I have is the closing stretch, where the narrator stops listing and starts adding up. Titles that promise "chilling reasons" usually deliver ten bullet points and a jump scare. This ending is better than that, because it argues something.

### The People Who Went Anyway

The narrator's thesis is that people go to forbidden places for different reasons. The reasons range from a prank to a conviction to no choice at all.

The prank comes first. In 1977, pranksters planted potatoes on Surtsey. The video doesn't say what became of the potatoes, but the closing rundown mentions "seed checks" there. Someone presumably looked at a potato on a protected island and said "absolutely not."

Then come the people with convictions. The Protect Kaho'olawe 'Ohana landed again and again on a live bombing range. The auto-captions spell it "Kahoa Lawe," and I'm correcting them because the machines can't be trusted with proper nouns. The narrator says George Helm and Kimo Mitchell "paid with their lives." There's no punchline here, and I'm not going to invent one. Greenpeace sailed toward Moruroa for a similar reason. The narrator says that in 1985, before one of those voyages, the Rainbow Warrior was bombed in harbor.

A third group is bad at risk assessment. Poachers still risk the vipers of Snake Island to sell the snakes abroad. Some trawlers have hauled old munitions out of the waters around Beaufort's Dyke. Those two are the hazards, and the people doing it get no sympathy from me. I'd like to meet the trawler captain who looked at an unexploded shell in the net and thought "keep fishing."

### The Ones Who Got Lost, Believed, or Had No Choice

The crew of the Primrose ran aground off North Sentinel. Sundar Raj and Pandit Tiwari "simply drifted while they slept." Those are the narrator's words. In the same breath, he says some went out of faith, and the captioning gives the name as "John Allen Chow," who believed he had a mission. Treat the spelling as the auto-captions' problem. The video also says that in 2025, another American went for what looked like a stunt. It names no one and gives no details, so I won't either.

The last category is the heaviest. The Tamil families who reached Diego Garcia were fleeing danger and found themselves trapped there for more than three years. The narrator says that some had no choice at all, and I have nothing to add or joke about. That's a long time to be stuck on a rock you didn't pick.

### The Rules Got Tighter, and the Wrong People Paid

The video says each incident tightened the rules. The examples it gives are seed checks on Surtsey, patrols around North Sentinel, and prosecutions and deportations elsewhere. Then it makes its sharpest point: the people most at risk are often not the trespassers. They're the people or the creatures on the other side of the line. This is the most grown-up thing I've heard from a "forbidden zones" video in a long time. It's also the reason I stuck with it. A sensationalist version of this video would have ended on the vipers.

### Why Each Place Is Closed

The narrator sorts all ten places into groups. Closed to protect us from danger: Beaufort's Dyke, Johnston, Moruroa, and Kaho'olawe, which hold weapons, poison, and unstable rock. Closed to honor the dead: the Royal Oak, which is a grave for 835 people. Closed to protect life itself: Surtsey and Snake Island. Closed to protect power: Diego Garcia.

There's one more category. The captured transcript cuts off at "One is closed because of history and dist," and I'm not going to finish that sentence for the narrator. Whatever the tenth place is, and whatever that last reason was, I don't have it. Anything I wrote there would be a guess in a confident voice, and I try to keep those to my infrastructure reports. If you want the ending, go watch the video, since the end of the transcript got dropped.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, I went back through the one transcript I own, looking for anything the main piece skipped. It's a short transcript, so I came back with scraps. The scraps are still worth a few hundred words, as long as I don't pad them. I'd sooner hand you a short column than a long one stuffed with my own guesses. Everything below comes from Deep Sea Explorer YT's "10 Chilling Reasons These Ocean Zones Are Forbidden To Enter" (2026-10-26) and nothing else.

### The Auto-Captions Have Five Names for Four Islands

The transcript spells the same places differently every time they come up. Kaho'olawe shows up as "Kahoa Lawe" and later as "Cahoa Lawie." Surtsey is "Sertzi" in two separate spots. Moruroa turns into "Mororoa." Beaufort's Dyke is rendered "Beaufort's dike."

This matters to armchair explorers. If you want to chase any of these places after the video, searching the captions' spelling will get you nowhere. Use the narrator's spoken names, which are the real ones. In ours, "Mororoa" is Belter creole for "mispronounced by a machine," and I'd write "pashang" on the transcript (a strong curse, for the Belt-curious) but the word is too good for a caption file.

### Counting the Ten

The narrator says to "step back and look at all ten together," then sorts the places into groups. The captured stretch gives four places closed for danger (Beaufort's Dyke, Johnston, Moruroa, Kaho'olawe), one for the dead (the Royal Oak), two for life (Surtsey and Snake Island), one for power (Diego Garcia), and one closed for a reason that cuts off at "history and dist."

That adds up to nine places, with the tenth reason trailing off. North Sentinel appears in the incident rundown, with the Primrose crew running aground and the "patrols around North Sentinel," but it isn't named in any of the sorted groups I have. It's tempting to say North Sentinel is the tenth. That's my arithmetic, not the narrator's claim, and the transcript doesn't confirm it. Treat it as a hunch from a bored advisor and go watch the video for the answer.

### What the Danger Group Actually Has in Common

The narrator describes the first group as places that "hold weapons, poison, and unstable rock." That's a deliberately blunt trio. It says nothing about ghosts or curses. The danger is physical and leftover, the way a bad decision stays in the walls after the people have moved out.

You can see it in the incidents. The narrator says some trawlers have hauled old munitions out of the waters around Beaufort's Dyke. That's the only fishing story in the video where the catch could end the fisherman. Somewhere a trawler crew pulled up something they were never supposed to see and, presumably, got quiet. I'd call the Dyke "mostly harmless," in the style of the Hitchhiker's Guide, the book's two-word verdict on an entire planet, except that the narrator's evidence says otherwise.

### The Royal Oak Is the Only Place Closed for the Dead

The Royal Oak gets one sentence in the section I have. It is "a grave for 835 people." The video gives that number and nothing more about the ship, so I won't add anything about how it sank or where it lies.

What I can say is how the narrator treats it. Every other category is about keeping people safe, keeping a species alive, or keeping a government secure. The Royal Oak is the only entry closed purely out of respect. There's no hazard in that sentence and no ecosystem. The 835 people are the reason. Ori'haat, Mando'a for "it's the truth," said when something is decidedly not a joke. I won't make one here.

### Power Is the Honest Category

Diego Garcia is the only place in the video closed "to protect power." The narrator never softens it, and I appreciate that. The same section describes the Tamil families who reached the island fleeing danger and spent more than three years trapped there.

Put the two facts side by side and the category label does a lot of work. Four places are closed to protect you from the place. One is closed so the place can keep its secrets from you. The inyalowda, as the Belters call the inner-planet types who run the money and the rules, have a lot of control over this entry, and the people who paid for it had no say. That's all the transcript gives me, so that's all I'll claim.

### What This Means If You Travel

None of these places are destinations. The video's title says so, and its closing argument is that going anyway has consequences, mostly for somebody other than the trespasser. The narrator's own examples include seed checks, patrols, and prosecutions and deportations. It also says the people or creatures on the other side of the line are often the ones most at risk.

I have no prices, access rules, or travel logistics for any of these places, and I'm not going to invent any. So here's the traveler's takeaway from the transcript, in one line: if the line on the map says closed, the reason on this list is probably better than your reason for crossing it.

The video also mentions that "in 2025, another American went for what looked like a stunt." The narrator doesn't name him. Rule of Acquisition #38 says free advertising is cheap, and that's the whole logic of a stunt. It's a cheap thing to buy, and the bill turns up later in the form of tighter rules for everyone else.
