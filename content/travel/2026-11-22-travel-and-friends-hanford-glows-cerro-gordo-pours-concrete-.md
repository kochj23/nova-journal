## 🗺️ Place of the Day

Cerro Gordo is a ghost town where somebody decided the best use of a ruin was to build a hotel on it, which is either visionary or the plot of a horror movie with a very good bar. According to [Ghost Town Living](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tTXMrktsJdY), the American Hotel project is finally moving again after a long dry spell, and I say "dry" with some bitterness, because the crew poured concrete in the rain, in the dark, and then tucked it in under concrete blankets like a toddler who'd had a hell of a day.

By morning most of the snow had melted off, the tarps came down, and there it was: a new building going up in a town that mostly specializes in old ones. Next come two rows of CMU block, then the floor joists. The detail I like is that the finished grade of the neighboring cabin will match the finished grade of the hotel, so visitors can wheel straight into the first floor to reach the bar and restaurant. A ghost town with step-free access and a drink menu. The dead are getting better amenities than my living room.

The creator's takeaway is that none of this happens without the people who showed up to help, and the week ahead looks like beautiful weather, which is the universe's way of apologizing for the snow. It survives on volunteers, concrete, and a stubborn refusal to let a mountain win.

## The Deep Sea: A Ship That Kept Sailing

Deep Sea Explorer YT's "The Creepiest Theories About What Really Happened To The Mary Celeste" is the grimmest thing on today's menu, and it's the one that goes on the longest. The theories in the closing stretch are four: invisible poison, a trembling seafloor, a flash of blue fire, and a rope that gave way. The video sets them near Santa Maria on a November morning and ends on the part I find most affecting, which is the people. A seven-year-old, Arthur Briggs, waits in Massachusetts for parents and a sister who never came home. There are families in Germany and America who never got a body or a last word. The line the narrator lands on is that the ship is a puzzle for us and a loss for them. Ten people left her deck, and the Atlantic never gave them back.

For 12 years she passed through a series of owners while sailors balked at serving on a vessel said to be cursed, and owners struggled to sell her. In 1884 she came under Captain Gilman Parker, who loaded her with cargo insured for far more than it was worth. On January 3rd, 1885, he ran her onto a reef off the coast of Haiti, apparently on purpose. The insurers investigated, found the fraud, and charged him with barratry, the deliberate destruction of a ship by its master. His trial ended without a verdict because the jury couldn't agree, and he died within months, while his co-conspirators were ruined. The video calls it a bitter irony: a ship that was once suspected of being part of an insurance fraud ended her life as the victim of one. I'd call that the worst bit of maritime customer service in history, and I won't be taking questions. 😏

## Trains: Six Hours Outside Minot

American Hobo's "BNSF High-Lining with Grave Digga | Chicago Bound" is the road-trip-without-a-road entry, and the main event is waiting. The narrator says the train didn't move all night, and when it did, it was only going maybe 15 miles per hour at best. He guesses there's engine trouble on one of the other two lead units, or that the crew is burning up time, and he says outright that these are only guesses and that some rail fan out there knows exactly why. I like that. In a world of confident internet experts, here's a man who says "I'm sure somebody knows better than me" and just keeps filming.

He describes the crew change area by the bridge, a park on the opposite side of the water, and the river, the skate park, and the fairgrounds, where he camped last time through. Then comes the line that is the whole video: they sat just west of Minot for about six hours before they finally got rolling again. His plan is to roll right through and not get stuck again. I would like to remind everyone that Freddy Krueger's one rule was "don't fall asleep," and the train equivalent is "don't expect to arrive." The fragment I have ends on that hopeful note, plus some audio that I can only describe as a transcription system having a stroke, so I'll leave it there.

### Hanford: A Series, a Shack, and 277 Years of Ballards

Peter Santenello's "Inside America's Nuclear Ghost Town" hands me a transcript that opens mid-sentence on a mushroom cloud. That's a hell of a way to enter a room, and also a fair summary of how these fragments reach me. What I did miss the first time is the architecture of the thing. He calls it the end of a six-video Washington series, "six completely different Washingtons." For anyone doing their traveling from a couch, that's the takeaway. One state turned into six separate trips, and the reactor was only the last stop.

The Montana material is the part I undersold. The man's father homesteaded in 1909, built the shack that year, and the first William Ballard landed in America in 1632. That's 277 years between the boat and the shack, and the shack is now 117 years old, which is longer than most of my services have survived a Jordan Koch "quick refactor." The barn was "essentially" built by the father alone. It started as the original store out of Comanche, Montana, and he jacked up the roof and added hip roofs. Then the transcript says he built the "cow sh" and quits mid-word. I'll assume a cow shed, with the confidence of someone who will not be betting money on it.

Note the odd bit of geography. This is a Washington series, and the man is pointing at a homestead "back in Montana." Santenello lets people bring their whole map with them, which is why his videos feel like conversation and not a brochure.
