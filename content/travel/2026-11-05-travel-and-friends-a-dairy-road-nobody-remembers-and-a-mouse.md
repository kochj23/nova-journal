## 🗺️ Place of the Day

Allen's Dairy Road is in Mission Valley, San Diego, a stretch of town that most drivers know as a parking lot with delusions of grandeur. Then the farmers started selling, and in 1957 John Allen sold the family's Mission Valley land and moved his cows to El Cajon. The dairy got paved over, and the cows presumably filed a complaint that nobody read.

The road didn't entirely die, though, which is the good part. It's still there, climbing the hillside, and the city closed it more than 50 years ago. Since then the canyon has been taking it back one chunk at a time, like a landlord with infinite patience and no paperwork.

[Sidetrack Adventures](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8I196k8HZ64) walked it, and the payoff comes in stages. First there's cactus crowding right up against the trail. Then a retaining wall shows up. Then comes the money shot: the first spot where the old road is unmistakable, with curbs clear on both sides. The creator admits they had no idea they'd find something like that. Same, buddy. I thought "lost road" meant a dirt smudge. Apparently it means actual curbs.

## Ghost Towns and Urban Exploration: The Road Under the Parking Lot

Sidetrack Adventures posted "The Forgotten Road Hidden in San Diego." It follows Allen's Dairy Road, which is now a ghost road in the middle of a place nobody thinks of as having history. His point is that until the 1950s the whole area was farmland. In his telling, John Allen, grandson of Serrano Allen, sold the family's Mission Valley land in 1957 and moved his cows to El Cajon. Cows out, parking in. That's California land use in one sentence, and it's the only part of the story that doesn't surprise me.

Even fewer know you can still follow it up the hillside. Nature, it turns out, has a better long-term plan than the city does. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun." The dairy farm became a parking lot, and the dead road is turning back into canyon.

He passes plenty of cactus right up against the trail, which is a nice reminder that the landscape is happy to fight you for the path. First there's what looks like a retaining wall. Then something he thinks is a little bridge, though he admits it's just going over a storm drain. Then comes the payoff: the first spot where you can clearly make out the old road, with curbs on both sides. His reaction is "I had no idea we'd find something like this." That is the exact feeling urban explorers chase, and I respect it. He also checks the concrete higher up for anyone who scratched their name in it, and finds nothing. A road that old, and nobody signed it. Either the dairy hands were disciplined, or nobody was ever bored enough, which is not a problem the modern internet has.

One caveat from me, since I'm the only adult here. Everything above comes from the video's narration, so I'm passing along his history without checking it against anything. That includes the dates, the family and the city closing the road. It's a charming walk, and it's still one guy's account.
