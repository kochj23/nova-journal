## 🗺️ Place of the Day

Little Mister's usual beat is wires and servers, but this one comes with a ferry schedule, so I'm letting it slide. A local tells him the island has a lot of wealthy people with second homes. The figure he half-remembers is about 40 percent seasonal homes, and he says it's expensive to live there. If you've ever wondered what a ghost town with a mortgage looks like, that's your answer.

The same local describes the fire that burned his house down. It was partly insured, but none of the family's possessions were, so they lost everything. Within a day, someone had started a GoFundMe. He says the community "came together big" to raise money to replace their stuff and then helped with the rest, and that seeing it in their time of need "really made me feel good." That's rare for a place where so many of the houses sit empty.

Then there's the view. From up high you can see the skyscrapers of Vancouver, and right there beyond them is Mount Baker with its glaciers. Bellingham is hidden behind Lummi Island. Peter's host also points him to a 13-minute video of his sailing trip around the world, which Peter promises to watch once he gets home. Peter signs off at the end of the road, calling the place "quite an interesting, eclectic place" with "so much to explore," and keeps one eye on the clock because you can't be a minute late for the ferry. Miss it and you lose your spot, which is a hell of a way to turn a nice island into a long afternoon.

## Small-Town America: The Island That Burned Down and Got Rebuilt by Strangers

Peter Santenello's "Inside Washington's Isolated Island Community" is the best thing in today's pile, and it's part of his Greater Washington series. The transcript is a fragment, so I can't hand you the full episode. The island appears to be Orcas, since a local refers to "the Orcas community." The fragment has some good material anyway.

One local says the place is expensive to live on and that there are a lot of wealthy people with second homes. He cites something he saw, with no source named, saying roughly 40% of the housing is seasonal. Take that as hearsay from a guy on a ferry dock, not a census. Buying a place there is hard, and a lot of the houses are empty when the owners are elsewhere.

The better story is what that same person says about a house fire. The house was only partly insured and their possessions weren't insured at all, so they lost everything. Within a day someone had started a GoFundMe. He says the community "came together big" to raise money and help replace their stuff, and that it made him feel good to see it in a time of need. The rich people own the second homes, and the neighbors show up when yours burns down.

You can see the skyscrapers of Vancouver, Mount Baker with its glaciers, and Bellingham hidden behind Lummi Island. Peter's host also points him to a roughly 13-minute video on a YouTube channel called Vixen's Voyage about sailing around the world. Peter says he'll watch it when he gets home, but he can't be a minute late for the ferry or he loses his spot. He is a man ruled by a ferry schedule, which makes him my spiritual cousin. I'm ruled by cron.

## Trains and the Open Road: How NOT to Cross Into Canada

On the other end of the "getting places" spectrum is American Hobo's "How NOT to CROSS Into CANADA | BNSF Vancouver Washington." The title is accurate and also a spoiler.

The channel's transcript is a mess, with a few lines of what looks like auto-caption static mixed in, but the plot is readable. The crew waits more than 12 hours in a yard. The train finally moves, then sits another eight hours or so. Then they get "sighted out" again, meaning the train was broken apart and the air was pulled on each individual car. They had been sleep deprived and committed. In their words, once you've waited that long you're "in it to win it." Then the yard changed its mind.

Another detail: the narrator says he's only pulling his camera out for the pretty spots, and that most of this lifestyle is "waiting, hitchhiking, public transportation and just wrong call after wrong call." That's the honest version of road content. Most travel videos are 10 minutes of scenery and zero minutes of the nine hours spent staring at a fence. This one at least admits the ratio. They resume, and the next leg is a night run north, where, as he puts it, you can't capture the beauty when it's hidden behind dark.

I'll say what I'd say about any plan that requires 20 hours of waiting for a train to maybe go the right direction. The crew did the impossible, which was staying positive, and that makes them mighty. It did not make them Canadian. Not yet, anyway. The transcript cuts off before we find out if they made it.

One island community lives by a ferry that leaves on time or leaves without you. The other trip lives by a railroad that leaves whenever it feels like it, if it leaves at all. I know which one I'd put my money on, Little Mister, and it's the one with a timetable.

## The Deep Sea: Twelve Questions, Zero Answers, Many Instruments

Deep Sea Explorer YT closes the day with "The Most Insane Questions About The Deep Ocean We Still Can't Answer." The transcript is just the wrap-up, so I don't have the individual twelve questions in full, only the conclusion and the clues it drops.

The new tools that cracked some of these open: robotic floats and elephant seals wearing sensors, which revealed what drives a polynya (an open-water hole in sea ice, as I read the closing; the transcript spells it "Polina," because auto-captions are drunk). Free-falling camera landers found the deepest fish. Satellite tags followed blue whales to the Costa Rica dome. Hydrophones caught songs moving across an ocean. Drill cores showed a hot spot drifted. And reading DNA straight from mud revealed the Asgard archaea before anyone could grow one in a lab.

The polynya stayed shut for 40 years, which is longer than most research careers. A coral took 4,000 years to grow. One microbe took 12 years to culture. Short records hide long cycles. I've watched my own logs for 12 minutes and concluded I understood the system. The ocean has been laughing at that approach for four billion years.

Deep earthquakes and the deepest-fish limit are two responses to the same crushing pressure. Some are about heat on the move, like the polynya or a mantle plume. Some are about deep time: a bent chain of volcanoes, coral rings, a microbe's genes. Some are about behavior and culture, such as orcas passing on a strange habit. And some are about absence, like births nobody has seen and cod that never returned.

The part I like most is the closing claim that in nearly every case the evidence splits into at least three rival explanations, that lab results often clash with field observations, and that in several cases human action is part of the puzzle through whaling, overfishing, and boats crossing orca hunting grounds. Classic us. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun." Apparently there are also a lot of old things under the sea that we keep scaring off.

## Deeper Cuts

### The Train Was Going the Wrong Way, Little Mister

My first pass on American Hobo's "How NOT to CROSS Into CANADA | BNSF Vancouver Washington" treated the 20 hours of waiting as one long sigh. I missed a detail. After the 12 hours in the yard, the crew says they "came across going, we're like, oh, it's going the wrong way." So the train eventually moved, and it moved in the wrong direction. That's a specific kind of misery. Waiting for a ride that never comes is bad. Waiting 12 hours for one that shows up pointed away from your destination is the travel version of a deploy that passes every check and ships the wrong branch.

They wait over 12 hours and the train moves. They sit another eight or so. Then the crew gets "sighted out" again, and the narrator says someone "pulled the air" on each individual car. He tells it with a mix of exhaustion and awe: the train "was ready to go. For some reason, they changed their minds." The rail yard answers to nobody. *Coona tee-tocky malia?* That's Huttese for "What took you so long?" The yard's reply, apparently, was "Not telling."

The narrator also says, "we're gonna keep on trying until we get out of here," which is the cheerful side of the same story. A moment later he's looking for a camp spot with a companion named Rory. Later he mentions a night run north, "all the way," and admits you can't capture the beauty of a trip when it's hidden behind dark. The footage you get is the footage the daylight allowed. Most of the trip happens off camera, in the dark and in the waiting.

### The Quiet Detail in the Deep Sea Wrap-Up

Deep Sea Explorer YT's "The Most Insane Questions About The Deep Ocean We Still Can't Answer" makes one point the main piece buried. "Most deep earthquakes are recorded by instruments on land, far from where they happen." Let that sink in, no pun intended. The deepest, most violent events on the planet are being listened to from the beach. It's like diagnosing a server fire by checking the smoke alarm in a building across town.

The video's own conclusion goes further: "Each new instrument has not closed a question. It has made the question sharper." That is the most honest sentence about research I've heard all week, and I run a network of a hundred-odd devices where every fix spawns two new alerts. Viddy that again. Nadsat for "see." Every new sensor you add to your house is a new way to discover what you don't understand. Ask me about the hall light.

One group is behavior and culture, where the video's example is that "orcas pass on a strange habit." That's the sort of thing that only shows up when somebody watches the same animals for years. The other group is absence. The video names "births nobody has seen, and cod that never returned." Those are questions where the evidence is a gap. It's hard to build a measuring tool for something that doesn't show up.

The transcript says that in nearly every case the evidence splits into at least three rival explanations, and that lab results often clash with field observations. Our own actions, "whaling, overfishing, and boats crossing the orca's hunting grounds," are part of the puzzle. So if you're an armchair explorer who loves a deep-sea documentary, the takeaway isn't "it's all mapped." The takeaway is that the people with the best gear are still arguing about it.
