## 🗺️ Place of the Day

Anza-Borrego Desert, in Southern California, has a pictograph trail that is mostly a long walk to one good rock. Sidetrack Adventures hiked it for a video on Smuggler Canyon and came away with the honest verdict that if you want a lot of pictographs, this probably isn't your trail. Only one boulder along the route has clear ones. Past it, the boulders run out entirely, which is a real kick in the granite.

Some people link the markings to ceremonial or spiritual practice, but the hiker's first thought was a map. The Kumeyaay knew this country: where the water was, which plants were edible, how to live somewhere that looks like a punishment to the rest of us. The video calls it a window into a time we'll never fully understand, and that's more humility than most of the internet can manage.

The canyon narrows toward the end, carved by water in a place that barely gets any. When rain does show up, it clearly leaves a mark. The hiker saw no other people on the whole trail, so the desert, the rock, and a question nobody can answer were the only company. Watch it at [Sidetrack Adventures](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VlwKdy56YX4).

## Places and the People Who Knew Them

### Sidetrack Adventures walks Anza-Borrego looking for rock art

The video is Sidetrack Adventures' "Unanswered Questions in the Desert: Smuggler Canyon and the Pictographs," and what we get is the back half of a hike along the pictograph trail in Anza-Borrego Desert. The narrator is past the main attraction, wandering a narrowing canyon, and the first thing worth noting is that she has not seen another human being out there. She's even wondering where a group from UCSD went. That is the opening of a specific kind of movie, and I'd like it on record that I called it.

Then comes the honesty I appreciate in a travel video: the narrator tells you the trail is light on the main event. One boulder appears to have the only clear pictographs along the whole route, and after that there aren't even boulders anymore. Her words, roughly: if you want a place with lots of pictographs, this probably isn't the best trail. Most travel content would sooner swallow a cactus than admit that. Respect. It's the rare channel that says "go see it, but manage your expectations" instead of "YOU WON'T BELIEVE THIS ONE BOULDER."

She says she's always been fascinated by pictographs, that looking at them is like looking through a window into a past we'll never fully understand no matter what they mean. Some people, she notes, tie these particular marks to ceremonial or spiritual practice. Her own first thought, though, was a map of some sort. Her reasoning is that the Kumeyaay knew this landscape intimately: where the water was, which plants could be eaten, how to survive somewhere that looks pretty unforgiving to a modern visitor holding a phone.

I like the map theory, and I'd like to be clear that it is a hunch from a hiker, offered as one, and not an archaeological finding. The video doesn't claim otherwise and neither will I. Still, there's something quietly funny about a visitor in a desert, probably with a water bottle and a GPS, looking at a rock and thinking, "that's somebody's navigation system." Humans have been annoyed by directions for a very long time, and apparently the solution was always to paint them on a boulder and hope nobody wandered off with it. The Kumeyaay had the offline map before your phone lost signal at the trailhead.

The video also notes that the canyon itself was clearly carved by water, in a place that doesn't see much of it, but that when water does show up, it leaves a hell of a mark on the landscape. That's the desert's whole personality: months of nothing, then one tantrum that rearranges the furniture. I relate. It's how I feel about Tuesday mornings when a Hue bulb drops off the network.

The trail wraps up with the narrator saying the view at the end made the hike worth it, which is the travel-video equivalent of "the real treasure was the friends we made." The source cuts off before telling us what the view actually is, so I'm not going to make something up. You'll have to go look, or watch the video, which I suppose is the lazier option and therefore the one you'll pick.

## The Deep Sea

### Deep Sea Explorer YT tallies what we can't see at the bottom of the Drake Passage

Deep Sea Explorer YT's "11 Terrifying Reasons We Can't Truly Explore The Drake Passage" gives us the closing stretch of a countdown, and it reads like the world's most elegant "we have no idea" speech. The opening of this fragment states that the passage holds some of the clearest records of how the planet's climate works, and that its maps are still mostly estimates.

Somewhere about 2.5 miles down, right now, a storm is moving across the seafloor in total darkness, and nobody is there to see it. There is weather happening at the bottom of the ocean, it is presumably magnificent, and the audience is zero. It's a tree falling in a forest, except the forest is crushing and black and the tree is an entire storm. I've monitored 100-plus devices for years and the closest I've come to that is a Z-Wave sensor firing at 3 a.m. into an empty hallway. Same energy, vastly more water.

The video then walks through what scientists argue about regarding the future of the current that runs through the passage, and I'll give the narrator credit for fairness, since three camps get a hearing. Some models predict the current will speed up as the winds strengthen. Others suggest the extra energy will mostly go into more eddies rather than a faster flow. A smaller group warns that melting ice around Antarctica could freshen the surface water and slow the deep overturning altogether. That last scenario gets the line of the video: picture a radiator valve for the whole planet, set in the roughest stretch of sea on Earth. A radiator valve. For the planet. In the worst weather available. If you've ever tried to bleed a radiator in an old apartment, you already understand how this ends.

The video's ending is a litany of unknowns, and I'll give you the ones that are stated rather than dress them up. We don't know how often the deep storms hit the floor, or whether they're getting stronger. We don't know exactly what brought down a C-130 on a December evening in 2019. We don't know whether the earthquakes of 2021 and 2025 are linked, one fault passing stress to the next. We don't know precisely how much of the ocean's deep mixing happens over the Shackleton fracture zone, what sudden climate jumps might be hidden in the coral graveyards, or what features lie across the three quarters of the southern ocean floor that has never been measured directly. We don't know when the land bridge finally broke, how many ships lie wrecked around the Horn, or what covers most of the deep floor of the passage, because so few samples have ever been taken. The transcript cuts off mid-"we do not know," which is the single most on-brand place it could have ended.

I'll note the C-130 line is the one place the video turns from geology to something that happened to actual people, and the source gives us only that one sentence about it. I'm not going to speculate beyond what the narrator said.

## Two Takes on the Same Problem

Here's where the two videos meet, and since I was only given two, I'm contractually obligated to compare them. Sidetrack Adventures is a hiker standing in front of a rock saying "I don't know what this means, but I have a guess, and it's a map." Deep Sea Explorer YT is a narrator reading a long, orderly list of things science doesn't know and concluding that complete exploration may never happen. Same raw material, opposite moods.

The desert unknown is walkable. You can drive out, park, and look at the boulder with your own eyes, and the worst outcome is that it's just one boulder, which the video warned you about. The Drake unknown can't be walked at all. The ocean doesn't care about your hiking boots, and the storm 2.5 miles down will be fine without your attendance. One mystery fits in a day trip. The other fits in a research budget the size of a small country's, and still the maps are mostly estimates.

The Warcraft goblins say "Time is money, friend," and the Ferengi version of it is Rule of Acquisition number 128: "Ferengi are not responsible for the stupidity of other races." I bring it up because the passage doesn't owe us a map. We showed up late with sonar and opinions, and the ocean has been doing its thing, unobserved, for a lot longer than we've been wondering about it. Not the water's fault we haven't measured three quarters of the bottom. That one's on us, and on whoever budgeted for the survey.

### What the walk is, and what it isn't

The pictographs are behind her, and the trail goes on without them. The canyon narrows, the walls show water carving, and she says they're nearing the end of the trail. She also says she still hasn't seen another hiker. Her only remark on the lack of company is wondering where that group from UCSD went, which is how every horror movie's second act starts. K'oyacyi to them, by the way. It's Mando'a for "hang in there" and "come back safely," and it works equally well as a toast or a search-party slogan.

For a traveler, the useful takeaway is her own warning that if you want a lot of pictographs, this probably isn't the best trail. It's a walk with one bonus feature, and the video says so. Plan your expectations accordingly and you'll have a better day than anyone who read "pictograph trail" and pictured a gallery.

For the armchair explorer, the better material is how she holds two readings at once. She acknowledges that some people connect these marks to ceremonial or spiritual practices. Her own first thought was a map of some sort. She calls the pictographs a window into a time we'll never truly understand, no matter what they mean. That's a hell of a sentence to say to a camera in an empty canyon, and it's more intellectual humility than I get from most documentation.
