## 🗺️ Place of the Day

**Chose, a flooded mine where the water hit pause and never hit play**

Most ruins have the decency to fall apart in public. A flooded mine does the opposite. The water rises, everything stops in place, and the place sits sealed in the dark like the world's worst Tupperware. Cold, still water slows the rot of wood and iron, so a working mine turns into a time capsule nobody asked for.

Chose is the one in this video that gets under your skin. Divers there find boots sitting in the silt, which is the kind of detail that makes your stomach drop a floor. It's also a place that has cost a life: a diver named Weishu didn't come back. Families of the people lost there want names, and divers are often the only witnesses who can go looking. Recovery or leave it alone is the live argument, and it isn't a cute one.

The wider point from Deep Sea Explorer YT is that rebreathers, scooters and digital mapping now let small teams go further than whole expeditions could a generation ago. Many of the explorers who know these places best are getting older, though, and their knowledge needs writing down before it goes with them. Charming.

So no, you shouldn't go. But the full rundown on this and the other drowned workings (a truck dangling from a taut cable at Rosid, glass windows still intact at Bonterre) is in [The Most Chilling Things Divers Found Inside Flooded Mines](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iIlTY1RJAGg). Watch it from a dry sofa like a sensible adult.

## The Deep Sea (Except It's a Mine)

### A ruin that doesn't rot

A ruin, it says, decays in the open air, but a flooded mine is sealed. Nobody ever sold a weekend getaway with "everything stops exactly where it was."

Divers find boots in the silt at the places the captions render as Chose and Ojamo. A truck still hangs from a taut cable at Rosid. Glass windows survive at Bonterre, a spelling the captions bounce between several versions, while "Bone Tear" turns up elsewhere in the same narration. It's Pompeii, but wetter and with worse visibility.

### The ones who don't come back

The narrator says divers are often the only witnesses to these places, that they accept real risks to reach them, and that some, like Weishu at Chose, do not come back. 😏 I'd make a joke about this being the world's worst commute, but a man died, so I'll skip it and just say the video handles it with more restraint than its thumbnail probably does.

The video frames every site on the list as a balance of memory against danger, with a second argument layered on top: recover what lies inside, or leave it undisturbed? At Chose, families want names. At Bontere, visitors want to see a town. At Dorothea, rescuers just want people to come home. Three sites, three different kinds of wanting, and none of them is about the glass windows.

### Courts, microbes, and aging explorers

Bone Tear's future, we're told, depends on a court. The transcript gives me exactly that one sentence, so I'm declining to invent a lawsuit.

Microbes live in many flooded mines, in total darkness. Contaminated water can leak into rivers. And as climate patterns shift, water tables may change, drowning some workings deeper and exposing others. The mine is sealed, but the planet isn't holding still around it.

Rebreathers, scooters, and digital mapping now let small teams go further than entire expeditions could a generation ago. That's the hopeful bit. The less hopeful bit is that many of the explorers who know these places best are getting older, and their knowledge needs recording. If you've ever watched the one guy who understands the legacy system approach retirement, you know this dread intimately. Documentation, Little Mister. It's always documentation. Someone dives for decades, learns every passage in black water, and the institutional memory lives in one skull. I'd call that a single point of failure, except I'm the one who runs your single points of failure and I'm not enjoying the comparison.

## Deeper Cuts

### The video contradicts itself, and I'm delighted

Deep Sea Explorer YT's "The Most Chilling Things Divers Found Inside Flooded Mines" says that when the water rises, "everything stops in place." Ten seconds later it says cold, still water "slows the decay of wood and iron." Those are different claims. Stopped is a photograph. Slowed is a patient lawsuit against entropy. The narration treats them as the same thing, and the second one is the accurate one. The time capsule is on a payment plan, and the water table, the climate, and the microbes are all collecting.

That matters if you're an armchair explorer, because the boots in the silt are not forever boots. Nothing in the video gives a timeline, and I won't invent one. But the thesis of the whole piece is that these places are fragile, and the fragility runs in both directions. A shifting water table can drown some workings deeper and expose others. Something sealed today can be open to air tomorrow, and then it's just a ruin like any other.

### Three kinds of wanting, one kind of mine

At Chose, families want names. At Bontere, visitors want to see a town. At Dorothea, rescuers just want people to come home. Only one of those is a tourist's want, and the video is honest enough to put it in the middle, sandwiched between grief and rescue.

For anyone who likes this genre from the couch, that's the useful lesson. The video gives no access details, no tours, and no logistics, so I'm not making any up. What it does say is that divers are often the only witnesses. If you're watching from dry land, you're borrowing their risk secondhand, and some of them, like Weishu at Chose, didn't come back to post the footage. 😏 My usual brand of "that's what you get for going down there" is staying in the drawer. Watch the video, appreciate the footage, and don't treat it as a bucket list.

### The gear gap

The video doesn't compare specific rigs or cite any distances, so I can't either. The implication is still pretty clear: the barrier to reaching these places is dropping, while the number of people who know the passages by heart is shrinking.

The tools are getting better, and the oral history is getting older. Rogers' Rangers, Standing Order 1: "Don't forget nothing." The video is quietly begging the diving community to follow it. The people with the knowledge should record it before it ends up in the same black water as everything else.
