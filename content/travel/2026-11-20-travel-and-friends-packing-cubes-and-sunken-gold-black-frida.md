## 🗺️ Place of the Day

**The floor of the Baltic Sea**

Nobody is booking a beach weekend here. It's a dark, cold stretch of seabed that is quietly one of the more important pieces of real estate on the planet, and in September 2022 it made the news when explosions ripped through the Nord Stream gas pipelines lying on it. Navies now worry openly about the undersea internet and power cables that carry almost all of the world's data. That's the entire digital economy sitting on the bottom of the ocean with no security guard and a "please don't" sign. 😏

The place comes up at the end of a [Deep Sea Explorer YT](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QtwkjkETJHk) run through eight decades of spy submarine missions gone wrong. Its verdict is that the hardware changed but the ways these operations failed barely did. Politics shaped every outcome, and Seokcho was hit during a peace initiative. Much of the story is still buried. Project Azorian's haul remains largely classified. The 1981 K-324 collision has never been confirmed. The Hårsfjärden incident is still argued over, with one side pointing at Moscow and the other at NATO.

The video also notes that much of what the public knows came from outside official channels: journalists, authors, and submariners who finally stopped keeping their mouths shut. The official record is often just redactions, and "neither confirm nor deny" is the stock reply to anyone asking the wrong question.

It's a live problem, and the wires are still down there.

## The Deep Sea: Spy Subs and the Art of Not Telling You

### Deep Sea Explorer YT on spy submarine missions gone wrong

Now for the real meat, such as it is. Deep Sea Explorer YT's "Why These Spy Submarine Missions That Went Horribly Wrong" caught me only at the wrap-up, not the individual cases.

The thesis, as the narrator closes, is that these operations failed on each other, with politics shaping every outcome. The Seokcho incident hit during a peace initiative. The Horsviardin affair (auto-captions mangled the name, and it shows up spelled two ways) landed during a change of government. The narrator sums it up: "The ships and hardware changed over 80 years. The ways they failed barely did." That's the best line in either video, and it applies to every infrastructure project I've ever babysat. New hardware, same old stupidity.

We don't know exactly what Project Azorian recovered, and much of it remains classified. We don't know how badly the Yankee was damaged in 1974, or even which boat it was. The 1981 K-324 collision has never been confirmed. One Gangneung infiltrator was never accounted for. The Tichos 52's gold still lies five kilometers down, which is about three miles of water between you and your retirement. Connecticut's mission has never been explained. And Russian investigators still dispute what sank K-129 in the first place.

On Horsviardin, the narrator says it "is still argued over, with one side pointing at Moscow and the other at NATO." I'll note the argument exists and decline to pretend I can settle it from a fragment. Russia's invasion of Ukraine is unprovoked aggression, and Ukraine is the defender. I'm not going to treat a Kremlin-flavored "who really did it?" shrug as automatically equal to the alliance's account. When the Russian state tells a story, I assume the story serves the Russian state. That says nothing about ordinary Russian people, who didn't write the cover stories.

### Six Flavors of Not Knowing

Some are classified. "We don't know exactly what Project Azorian recovered. Much of it remains classified." Some are unconfirmed: "The 1981 K-324 collision has never been confirmed." Some are disputed, like Horsviardin, where "one side" points at Moscow and the other at NATO, and K-129, where "Russian investigators still dispute what sank" it. Some are simply unidentified, like the 1974 Yankee incident, where we don't know "how badly the Yankee was damaged" or "even which boat it was." Some are unaccounted for: "One Gangneung infiltrator was never accounted for." One is unexplained outright: "Connecticut's mission has never been explained." And one is physically sitting there, the Tichos 52's gold, about three miles down and not going anywhere.

Classified means someone is holding the file. Unconfirmed means the evidence never arrived. Disputed means two stories collided, and a dispute is not a coin flip. I'll repeat what I said before about Russia's invasion of Ukraine, because it governs how I read any story that ends in "and Moscow says otherwise": the state that launched that war has not earned the benefit of the doubt on much, and a state line is not a second opinion.
