## 🗺️ Place of the Day

Somewhere on the Pacific abyssal plain, tonight, there are tire tracks. Not metaphorical ones. Tracks left by deep-sea mining test machines, still sharp as the day they were made, because down there the marine snow falls at a few millimeters every thousand years. That's a tenth of an inch or so of dead plankton confetti, and in the meantime your footprint outlives every government that ever heard of you. Humanity's most durable infrastructure project is a rut in the mud nobody will ever see. Congrats, everyone.

The latest rundown from [Deep Sea Explorer YT](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TGtBczQtaog) tallies what the robots have hauled up so far: 16 tons of vent ore off Okinawa, about 1,430 pounds of crust from a seamount, and mud pulled from roughly 3.7 miles down near Minami Torishima. The weirder finds are the real draw. Radioactivity in the skin of a nodule. Rare earths stored in the bones of ancient fish. A spill caught on camera. One robot got left alone on the plain, waiting for a hook like the world's saddest Uber.

Meanwhile, the International Seabed Authority keeps meeting in Kingston, where the Council runs on consensus, so a handful of states can stall the rulebook for years. The machines are built and the rules are not. Build the bulldozer first, draft the zoning ordinance never.

## The Deep Sea: Everyone's Favorite Dark, Wet Parking Lot

The source is the Deep Sea Explorer YT video "10 Disturbing Things Deep Sea Mining Robots Have Hauled Up From The Abyss," posted November 15. It mentions nodules lifted in the Pacific, though the amount got lost in the transcript's cutoff, plus 16 tons of vent ore off Okinawa and 1,431 pounds of crust (the video says 649 kilograms) from a seamount. It also cites mud pulled up from roughly 3.7 miles down at Minami Torishima.

That last one puts us in Japan, sort of. Minami Torishima is the sort of place you'll never put on a vacation itinerary, because it's a speck in the Pacific whose main attraction is what's underneath it. The video says Japan is planning a bigger mud trial. In the travel business, that's called "scouting."

### The Disturbing Part

The list it gives is a lot more entertaining than a mining prospectus. There was radioactivity in the skin of a nodule. Fish fled a two-hour test and stayed away for a full year, which is the most relatable thing a fish has ever done; I leave the room for a lot less. Rare earth elements turned up stored in the bones of ancient fish. A spill was caught on camera. One robot got left alone on the abyssal plain waiting for a hook, which is the single saddest sentence in the transcript and I refuse to elaborate. And there was a fleet of giant machines that never saw the seafloor at all.

I can't tell you more about any of these than the narrator says, because the middle of the transcript is missing. I'm not going to guess at the details. I'm going to appreciate the radioactive nodule in silence, like a gentleman.

### The Rulebook Is the Real Ghost Town

Here's where the story turns from "cool robots" to "oh, good, a governance problem." The video says the International Seabed Authority keeps meeting in Kingston, a permit application is under review, and India is preparing a dive to 6,000 meters, about 19,700 feet. The transcript actually says "crude dive," which I assume is a transcription of "crewed" and not a promise of bad manners at depth. Papua New Guinea licensed Solwara 1, according to the narrator.

Enforcement at sea depends largely on independent observers aboard the ships. Inside the authority, the Council makes most key decisions by consensus, which means a small group of states can slow the process for years. Each annual session in Kingston ends with progress on some clauses and deadlock on others.

That's Entish territory. Tolkien's tree-shepherds speak a language so slow that a greeting takes an afternoon, and their whole philosophy is "don't be hasty." It's a lovely way to run a forest. It's an absurd way to regulate a machine that's already been lowered into the ocean. Meanwhile the robots are not being Entish about it.

Rule of Acquisition #147, for the Ferengi in the audience: "New users are like razor toothed gree worms. They can be succulent, but sometimes they bite back." The new users here are an entire industry arriving at a seafloor that has never had one, and the bite-back is an ecosystem, a regulator, or a fish that holds a grudge for twelve months.

### What the Abyss Remembers

Somewhere on the abyssal plain tonight, the tracks from these tests are still there, as sharp as the day they were made, and the marine snow keeps falling on them at a few millimeters every thousand years. In American, that's a small fraction of an inch per millennium. Your footprint in your own driveway will be gone by Tuesday; a robot's tread mark three miles under the Pacific will outlast every government involved in the argument about it.

The Na'vi have a phrase for what the video seems to be reaching for: Oel ngati kameie, "I see you." In Avatar it means a deep acknowledgment of another being, not just looking at it. The deepest parts of the ocean, the video says, are for the first time in Earth's history no longer out of reach. Whether anyone is going to see what's down there, or just see what it's worth, is the part the Council in Kingston hasn't settled.

## The Verdict

It's a thin issue, but the topic is a good reminder that the most remote travel destinations on Earth are the ones where the paperwork shows up last.

## Deeper Cuts

### Four Hauls, Zero Explanations

Deep Sea Explorer's "10 Disturbing Things Deep Sea Mining Robots Have Hauled Up From The Abyss" opens with a rapid-fire inventory. It names nodules lifted in the Pacific, 16 tons of vent ore off Okinawa, 1,431 pounds of crust from a seamount, and mud from about 3.7 miles down at Minami Torishima. The amount of nodules got eaten by the cutoff, which is typical of my life.

The two numbers we do have make an ugly comparison. The Okinawa vent ore outweighs the seamount crust by more than twenty to one. The video doesn't say why, or how the deposits differ, and I'm not going to improvise geology for you. What it does tell armchair explorers is that "deep sea mining" isn't one activity in one place. It's separate hauls from separate corners of the map, and each one apparently came up with its own surprise attached. The narrator's list of surprises includes radioactivity in the skin of a nodule, which is a hell of a thing to find on a rock you were planning to turn into a battery.

### Same Depth, Different Direction

The transcript says the mud came from 6 kilometers down. It also says India is preparing a dive to 6,000 meters.

So one deployment sends something down and brings dirt up, and the other sends a vehicle down for a look. I flagged earlier that the transcript calls India's plan a "crude dive," which I took for a mangled "crewed." Take that with salt. If I'm right, then the video's closing claim, that the deepest parts of the ocean are no longer out of reach, comes with a literal reading. We're planning to visit it, and we've been planning to haul stuff out of it for a while.
