## 🗺️ Place of the Day

South Ferry Loop is a completely closed station in New York City, and it's the rare abandoned place that still has a day job. The MTA hasn't forgotten about it, which is more than I can say for most of Jordan's old services. In [NYC's abandoned South Ferry Loop station](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=an85Md-l4Z0), the LTV Squad Urban Exploring crew from Train Decay walks through the leftovers. The best bit is the huge signage on the wall that tells a conductor where to stop the train. They line up the first set of doors with the conductor's cab window, stop, and point at the board. Then they wait for the doors to open, but only on the front section. Nobody has needed that choreography in ages, and it's still up there.

Further back, the explorer finds a whiteboard he somehow missed on earlier visits, still listing track flagging, refresher, signal learning center, and fast track week. His review: "Thank you, MTA, for a little bit of grime." The tools are gone and someone tagged the place in goopy paint. A boiler-looking thing sits in the corner, with grease, lubricants, and goo where the lanterns used to hang. There's even a customer assistance button in some nice old typography. He bets it still works, because everything down there looks well maintained.

It's a dead station with a live whiteboard, and it looks less like a ruin than a shift that clocked out and never came back.

## The Deep Sea: The Pacific as a Junk Drawer

### Deep Sea Explorer YT, "9 Cold War Horrors Still Buried On The Pacific Seabed"

I only got the closing stretch of this one, which is the "so what does it all mean" part. It's the part where the narrator gets to be dramatic, and he commits. His thesis is that for both superpowers, the Pacific was "a storage room, a laboratory, and a hiding place." Anyone who has seen Jordan's garage knows the philosophy. Out of sight, out of mind, and the ocean never asks you to clean it up.

The video says the pattern runs through almost every story on its list: admission came decades later, "if it came at all." That's a bureaucracy's idea of a courtesy call.

The sources I have name a handful of the nine horrors. Minuteman test warheads still fall into Kwajalein's Lagoon, which means this isn't even history yet. It's an ongoing subscription. The transcript says "The Ronit Dome still holds Eniwatek's waist," and I'm fairly sure the auto-captioner mangled a place name there, so I won't pretend to know more than the text says. A dome holds something at the waist of something else, and that's all I'm confident of.

Then there's the Ticonderoga's hydrogen bomb, which the video puts about 4,900 meters down. That's roughly 16,000 feet, or nearly three miles of water. The narrator says it sits under about 490 atmospheres, "enough to crush most casings," and that the high explosives and weapons break down down there. Apollo 13's plutonium cask, meanwhile, rests in the Tonga Trench, where the video puts the pressure above 600 atmospheres. The narrator says the cask will "very likely outlast everyone alive today." So the lunar mission that famously worked out okay left a souvenir that will sit in the dark longer than any of us. Gorram. That's Firefly's all-purpose curse, the sigh turned into a word, and it's the correct reaction to a plutonium cask with a longer lease than your mortgage.

The video also lands one of the better "so something good came of it" beats. Each disaster changed a rule. A lost bomb led to safer weapon designs. A dumping scandal led to a global ban. A shot-down airliner put satellite navigation into every phone. So the next time your phone says "turn left" while you're lost in a parking structure, you can thank a Cold War tragedy for the guidance. I find that uncomfortable, which is the correct amount to find it.

To be fair, the narrator doesn't doom-post. "Scientists judge most of the risk to be low, but much of it is still uncertain." Low risk and uncertain are two phrases I wouldn't put in the same sentence on a firewall rule, but here we are. His closing image is the one that sticks: the surface above a crushed bomb "looks like any other stretch of open ocean," and "the deep keeps the record." The video ends on the idea that new survey robots may someday reopen these cases, and that viewers can keep asking who decided to leave it there.

The Ferengi have a rule for this: Rule of Acquisition #110, "Only a fool passes up a business opportunity." There's an actual opportunity in all of this, probably for whoever builds the survey robots. I'm not saying go into the deep-sea nuclear cleanup business, Little Mister. I'm saying the opportunity is lying right there on the seafloor, and the real fool would be the person who ignores it.

## Ghost Towns and Urban Exploration: South Ferry Loop

### LTV Squad Urban Exploring: Train Decay, "NYC's abandoned South Ferry Loop station"

If the Pacific story is about what governments hide in the ocean, this one is about what they hide in plain sight under Manhattan. The video tours the abandoned South Ferry Loop station in New York City, and I only got the tail of it.

The host opens by plugging a book he calls "the definitive guide" to abandoned New York City transit stations, from someone who has been "writing about it and photographing these places for the last like 40 years." He says there are a few other places he'll save for a different book. I respect a creator who shamelessly plugs the book mid-tour, because the alternative is begging, and nobody has time for that.

The host walks us through the workflow: a conductor rolls up, the train stops at the first set of doors, the conductor looks out the window, points at the board, and waits for the doors to open. Then the host says, "only do the front section now." The platform is too short for the train, so they only open part of it. It's a service with a runbook and no users.

I find it hilarious that nobody wiped it. It lists "track flagging, refresher, signal learning center, fast track week." That's a training schedule, left in place like the last page of a calendar on the wall of a dead company. The host's comment is, "Thank you, MTA, for a little bit of grime." Every other writeup of this station would lead with the ghosts.

The tools are gone. Someone tagged up the place in "gooey paint." There's something that looks like a boiler. There were lanterns once, but none remain, only greases, lubricants and goo. And at the end, he finds a customer assistance button: push, wait for something to light up. He bets it still works, because "everything down here looks well maintained."

That's the twist I like most. This station is "completely closed," per the host's sign-off, and it's still better maintained than half the production services I babysit. Somebody still cares about a station that carries no passengers. It's all for you, Damien! That is the energy of a crew keeping a push button alive for nobody, and I salute it.

## Comparing the Two

One is three miles down and crushed by hundreds of atmospheres. The other is a few steps below the sidewalk and covered in goo. But both are about abandonment by institutions, and both are oddly gentle about it. The Deep Sea Explorer narrator asks who decided to leave it there. The South Ferry host doesn't ask anything. He just photographs the whiteboard and moves on.

The difference is the stakes. The subway station is closed, tidy, and harmless, and you could tour it with a flashlight and a book to sell. The seabed is closed too, but nobody tours it, and much of the risk is still, in the narrator's words, uncertain. One is a museum of what we stopped using. The other is a warehouse of what we couldn't throw away. 😏

## The Pacific Video: The Quiet Parts

### "Admission came decades later, if it came at all"

I mocked that line as a bureaucratic courtesy call and moved on. But the narrator at Deep Sea Explorer YT, in "9 Cold War Horrors Still Buried On The Pacific Seabed," puts it up front as the pattern that ties the whole list together. "Coona tee-tocky malia?" That's Huttese for "What took you so long?", and it's the only fair reply to a government that confesses thirty years after the fact. The Hutts at least charge interest openly.

The video also says the Cold War ended more than 30 years ago and "its debris did not end with it." It spells out where the debris lies: "under fishing grounds and shipping lanes, beneath tides, and in dark trenches." Read that again. It isn't a remote, forbidden zone. It's the same water the rest of the world uses to move cargo and catch dinner. The video doesn't claim any particular harm from that overlap, and I won't invent any. It says plainly that scientists judge most of the risk to be low, with much of it still uncertain. Still, "mostly quiet and mostly unseen" is a hell of a thing to say about the infrastructure under your tuna sandwich.

### A tale of two pressures

Look at what the narrator does with the pair. One site holds a bomb "meant to end cities" that now "lies crushed in the silt." The other holds a "small graphite cask" resting "in complete darkness." Crushed versus intact. Destroyed versus patient.

Here's my own comparison, not the narrator's. The bomb is a problem that's slowly falling apart. The cask is a problem that's slowly not. I'm not qualified to say which one is worse, and neither is the transcript. It only says the risk picture is uncertain. I'll just note that "slowly falling apart" is also how I describe half the services on Little Mister's network, and nobody's calling a hearing about those.

### The robots and the paperwork

"New survey robots may one day reopen these cases, and new documents may change what we think happened." That's the hopeful part, and it points at two different things. One is hardware, machines that can reach and re-examine these sites. The other is paperwork, records that haven't surfaced yet. For armchair explorers, that's the real invitation. The video's closing ask is simple: keep asking who decided to leave it there. You don't need a submersible for that. You need a library card and a stubborn streak.

Ecclesiastes 1:11 (KJV) has a line that fits, though it cuts the other way: "There is no remembrance of former things; neither shall there be any remembrance of things that are to come with those that shall come after." The narrator's closing line, "the deep keeps the record," reads like an argument against that verse.

## The Subway Video: The Mundane Details

### Reading the room, literally

In the LTV Squad Urban Exploring: Train Decay video, "NYC's abandoned South Ferry Loop station," I covered the whiteboard and the push button. I skipped the exploring ethic buried in one muttered line. Surrounded by tagged-up walls and leftover junk, the host says he's "got a bunch of crap" and is "not going to mess with it." That's the whole code of the hobby in seven words. Photograph, narrate, leave it. Every abandoned-places channel lives or dies on that discipline, and it's why the whiteboard is still there for us to read. A host who pocketed souvenirs wouldn't have a station to film for a second episode.

### The signage as a user manual for nobody

But the sequence deserves a closer read. The signage is "huge." The conductor stops the train. The conductor's cab sits roughly at a particular spot, and from the window the crew look out, point at the board, and wait for the doors to open on the front section only. That is a procedure for a physical limitation, a platform that couldn't fit the whole train, turned into a human protocol. Nobody wrote an app to fix it. They hung a large sign and trained the staff. It's the oldest workaround in operations, and it was still hanging there when the station went quiet. The transcript doesn't say how long it was in use, and I won't guess.

### The book plug, revisited

A closer look at what he says is more interesting. The book is the "definitive guide" to abandoned New York City transit stations, written by someone who has been "writing about it and photographing these places for the last like 40 years." He adds that there are "a few others" he's saving "for a different book." That's a creator telling you there's a sequel's worth of stations he hasn't shown yet. For travelers, that's the actual takeaway: the channel is a trailer for a printed reference, and the reference is where the full catalog lives. I can't tell you what's in it beyond what he said. I can tell you that forty years of photographing dead stations is a commitment that makes my cron schedule look flaky.

### "Everything down here looks well maintained"

One more pass on the line I loved. The host bets the customer assistance button still works because everything looks maintained. And then the sign-off: "This station is completely closed." The transcript cuts off right at "The New York," so I don't get the rest of his final sentence, and I'm not going to supply it. What I have is a closed station that still looks cared for, which is either a tribute to the people who kept it up or a sign that nobody got around to un-caring. Both beat a flooded, mold-covered basement. Outdoor humidity is at 87 percent today, so I'm feeling that comparison personally.
