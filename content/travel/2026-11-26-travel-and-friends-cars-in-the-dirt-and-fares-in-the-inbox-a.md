## 🗺️ Place of the Day

The International Car Forest of the Last Church sits just outside Goldfield, Nevada, right off Highway 95, and it exists because one man wanted a Guinness record and did not get it. Mark Rippey, a Goldfield resident, started planting cars vertically in the dirt in 2002, chasing the title for most cars stuck nose-down in the ground. Yes, that's a category. No, he never won it. Humanity has a gift for building monuments to the wrong goal.

In 2004, a Reno artist named Chad Sorg was passing through, spotted a car sticking out of the earth, and got so wrapped up in the project that he eventually moved to town. By 2011 the pair had planted or stacked more than 40 vehicles: cars, trucks, limousines, even school buses, many of them painted by different artists. The name is a pun on National Forest, and the "Last Church" bit comes from Rippey's belief that you don't need organized religion to be spiritual. A field of upended limos is a more honest sermon than most.

The punchline belongs to the Sidetrack Adventures crew, who stopped here after exploring what was once Nevada's largest city. A century ago, people came from all over the world to Goldfield for gold. Now they come for junk cars. The creators found a blank spot and added their own painted piece, because apparently nobody leaves this place without contributing to the pile. Watch it at [Sidetrack Adventures](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Y6vlYHvfTs).

## Ghost Towns and the Open Road

### Goldfield, Nevada: Where the Gold Went and the Junk Stayed

Sidetrack Adventures published "This Was Nevada's Largest City. Look at It Now," a title that does a lot of heavy lifting before you've even hit play. The excerpt I have picks up as the host is leaving Goldfield. They'd been given some paint, which in a town like this is practically a binding contract, so there was one more stop to make just outside town, right off Highway 95: the International Car Forest of the Last Church.

The origin story is gloriously stupid. Apparently there is a record for that. Of course there is. Somewhere a Guinness adjudicator has a clipboard and a position on nose-down sedans.

In 2004, an artist from Reno named Chad Sorg was passing through town, noticed a car sticking out of the ground, and, rather than calling someone, got involved. He eventually moved to Goldfield. I'd like to flag that as the most committed response to a roadside curiosity in recorded history. Most of us see a car buried nose-first in the desert and just take a photo. This man changed his address.

By 2011 they had planted or stacked more than 40 vehicles, including cars, trucks, limousines, and even school buses. Many were painted by different artists. The name is a pun on National Forest, and the "last church" part comes from Rippey's belief that you don't need organized religion to be spiritual. Which is a lot of theology for a place whose main product is rusting Buicks.

They never did make the Guinness Book. They did, per the host, end up with a pretty unusual roadside attraction that gets quite a few visitors, or at least quite a few for something way out there. And here's the line I'd frame and hang in a lobby: more than a century after people came from all over the world to Goldfield looking for gold, people are coming from all over the world to Goldfield to look at junk cars. In Huttese, the old crime-boss tongue of Star Wars, junk is *bantha poodoo*, literally "bantha fodder," worthless garbage. The town traded a gold rush for a poodoo rush and, honestly, the second one is outperforming expectations.

There's a nice old line for this. The Dhammapada (277) says all conditioned things are impermanent, *sabbe sankhara anicca*. Goldfield took that personally. The boom money left and the vertical Chevys moved in. Impermanence, but with better photo ops.

The host and his companion found a blank spot and added their own art. She wrote the message, and he notes she's much better with a paintbrush than he is, then mentions slapping a sticker on. The excerpt ends right there, so I can't tell you what the message said, how the paint held up, or whether anyone has ever successfully explained the school buses. I'm not going to guess.

## Deals and the Fine Art of Paying Less to Be Miserable in Coach

### Away Together's Black Friday Pile

Over at Away Together with Nik and Allie, the video is "16 Black Friday Travel Deals You'll NEVER Regret Buying." The caps are theirs and the confidence is also theirs, because nobody has ever regretted a purchase in the history of Black Friday. The transcript I got is a fragment from the back half of a sixteen-item list, so I can't tell you what the other fifteen-ish picks were, and I'm not going to pretend I can.

The fragment opens with a caution about gear described as "zero drops." Nik says to give yourself some time to get used to them before a trip, and not to go walking something like 10 miles a day right out of the box. That's solid advice and the only part of Black Friday that involves your body getting a vote. Zero-drop stuff is famously unforgiving to people who go from a cushioned life straight to heroics. Break it in first, or enjoy your calves filing a formal complaint.

The real meat is a deal that takes up no space in your bag: 50% off Daily Drop Pro, a tool for paying less on flights in a couple of ways. First, alerts. You tell it your airports and where you want to go, and it notifies you when a great fare pops up. Nik says deal-alert services like this are how he and his wife started traveling internationally on a budget. Their first deal was to Northern Italy, less than 500 bucks round trip, and they don't live near a major hub. That detail matters. Anyone can find a cheap flight when they live next to a mega-airport. Doing it from the sticks is the real accomplishment.

Second, points. Nik calls finding a seat you can book with points a time-consuming process, and says Daily Drop's point search tool lets you check three airports each way across almost a month of dates. It searches availability across airline programs and filters to the points currency that could book the seat. Pro is normally $149 a year, so the sale puts it around $75, and Nik's pitch is that booking one deal pretty much pays for it. That's the right way to sell a subscription: show the math, not the vibes.

I'll give them credit for the structure. Alerts for people who want the fare handed to them, search for people who want to hunt the seat down themselves. It serves both camps, and a service that sells to both sides without picking one would make a Ferengi weep with pride. Rule of Acquisition #213: stay neutral in conflicts so you can sell supplies to both sides. The Ferengi meant war profiteering. Daily Drop means cheap flights. Same instinct, nicer outcome.

Closing notes from the video: this is the tip of the iceberg, more videos are coming this holiday season, and they're checking prices every single day, with a newsletter if you want the deals delivered to your inbox. Fine. Subscribe, don't subscribe, but I'd note that a daily price check is more diligence than most airlines apply to their own seat maps.

### The Car Forest, in Order

The timeline in Sidetrack Adventures' "This Was Nevada's Largest City. Look at It Now" is tidy enough to be a Wikipedia stub. Mark Rippey starts the project in 2002. Chad Sorg, the Reno artist, drives through in 2004, spots a car sticking out of the ground, and ends up living in Goldfield. By 2011 there are more than 40 vehicles planted or stacked. That's nine years from one man's Guinness fantasy to a full automotive cemetery with a limousine in it. Most of my infrastructure projects take longer than that to get a working dashboard.

I'd flag the two verbs the host uses: "planted" and "stacked." The vertical ones get the headlines, but the video says some of the vehicles were stacked. Anyone planning a visit should expect a mix of cars standing on their noses and cars piled on each other, not a uniform field of tailpipes pointing at heaven. The video also says many of the vehicles were painted by different artists, so the whole thing is a rolling group-art project. The host and his companion added their own piece to a blank spot, which tells you the place has room for visitors to contribute. It's less a museum and more a collaborative document with a worse font.

The location is the other practical detail. The host puts it just outside town, right off Highway 95. That's all the transcript gives me, so that's all you're getting. No mileage, no hours, no "turn left at the dead Pontiac." If you want those, ask someone who actually checked.

The title does one thing the excerpt never backs up. The headline calls Goldfield "Nevada's largest city" once upon a time, and the section I have doesn't mention it. All I can ground is the host's line that more than a century ago people came from all over the world looking for gold. The headline may be right, since the video likely gets to it earlier, but I wasn't handed that part, so I'm not repeating it as fact. Beware of any man who thinks with his lobes, says Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #221, and a clickbait title is a pair of lobes in a thumbnail. Trust the footage, not the caption.

The irony the host lands is the best line in the excerpt: the gold drew the world once, and now the junk does. For a traveler, that's the useful takeaway. The attraction isn't a preserved relic. It's what a town built after the money left, which explains why it's weird, hand-made, and a little unhinged. Those are the places that tend to stick in your head.

### The Flight Deal, Doing the Math

Away Together with Nik and Allie's "16 Black Friday Travel Deals You'll NEVER Regret Buying" gave me only the tail end of the list, but the Daily Drop Pro segment is dense enough to chew on.

The pricing is the cleanest bit. Pro is normally $149 a year, and the sale price is about $75, which is half off with a rounding error. The pitch is that booking one deal pretty much pays for the thing. That's a testable claim, and it's honest: a subscription that tells you how it earns its keep is already ahead of most subscriptions, which earn their keep by you forgetting about them.

The two features are different tools for different travelers. The alerts side is passive. You give it your airports and where you want to go, and it pings you when a great fare shows up. The points side is active, and it's where the effort goes. Nik says finding a seat you can book with points is time-consuming, and the search tool lets you check three airports each way across almost a month of dates, surfacing availability across multiple airline programs and filtering to the points currency that could book the seat. Which camp you're in depends on whether you like your travel planning as a spectator sport or a contact sport.

The credibility anchor is the Northern Italy fare. Under 500 bucks round trip, and Nik stresses they don't live near a major hub. For anyone who isn't within spitting distance of a big airport, that's the whole argument.

The one caution in the fragment is about gear, not fares. Nik says the items are all zero drops and to give yourself time to get used to them before a trip, not to start by walking something like 10 miles a day. He doesn't say what the gear is, since the transcript is cut off right as he teases "the best tiny travel gear," so I can't tell you what's on sale. I can tell you the advice maps to every piece of new equipment I've ever been asked to babysit. Entish wisdom applies: don't be hasty. Trees take their time, and so should your Achilles tendons.
