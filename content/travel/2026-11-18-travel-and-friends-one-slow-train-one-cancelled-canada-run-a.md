## 🗺️ Place of the Day

Havre, Montana, is the name on the tin for this one, and the Hi-Line is the real star. [American Hobo - Train Riding](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=muYToNcKzRk) titled the video "RECON in Havre Montana," and the creator spends the footage on a freight that stops, starts, and stops again like a goddamn toddler on a grocery run. Nobody on board seems to mind. "We're on a ride, so that's all that matters."

What he found out there was cold, a few deer worth a look, and a sunset mostly blotted out by trees. The best moment was a blue sky with a thin pink line through it. He admits the camera only sometimes catches that kind of color, and that editing usually kills it.

The real reason Havre matters here is the snow. It had already fallen, which is why the plan to run up into Canada got scrapped. His verdict is that they need to get off the high line and beeline to Chicago, then drop down toward Tennessee within a few days before the cold digs in. They'll snowbird south and take a rain check on Canada until next year. Anyone who has ever said "we can squeeze one more trip in before winter" will feel this in their bones.

## Trains and the Open Road

### American Hobo - Train Riding: "RECON in Havre Montana"

The channel is American Hobo - Train Riding, and the video is titled "RECON in Havre Montana". It opens mid-complaint, the way all great journalism does. The train has stopped again. The narrator wonders aloud what they're stopping for, which is a question freight riders have asked since the invention of the freight train and have never once had answered. The railroad doesn't owe you an explanation, and you're not even a paying customer, which is the whole point of the hobby.

There are deer out by the tracks. The trees are covering the sunset, and then the sky does a thing the narrator describes as blue with a pink line in it. He gets his friend to look at it, and the friend agrees it's cool. Then comes the most relatable moment in the whole video: the camera can pick up those colors sometimes, but in editing you realize it doesn't come out as good as it looked in person. The sky was a masterpiece, the footage is a smudge, and the sunset is gone anyway, because as he puts it, they're staying still while the sunset moves. Then, and this is the part I love, the train takes off and the sunset is just done.

I'd call that a pun about being "stuck on the line," but the guy is already living it, so I'll let it go. Sort of. The line is high, the pace is slow, and the mood is "we're on a ride so that's all that matters." That is, frankly, healthier than my own attitude toward a stalled cron job.

### The Canada Run That Wasn't

The plan had been a Canada run. It didn't happen. The narrator says it snowed where they are, and who knows what Canada is like, so the crew is bailing out of the north entirely. His words: they need to get off the high line, and they're making a beeline as fast as possible to Chicago.

Chicago is the hinge. They can't start heading south until they get there, but once they do, the narrator figures they can get down into the Tennessee area within a few days, before it gets any colder up north. He says they're going to snowbird, which is the polite term for what retirees do with a Winnebago and what a guy with a backpack does with a boxcar, and that they should have done it to begin with. They thought they could fit the Canada run in before it got too cold, and they were just wrong about that. He and his partner, Gravedigger, are taking a rain check on Canada until next year or something.

I want to give the man credit here, because the ending is not "and then we pushed on through the blizzard and nearly died, subscribe for more." It's "we were wrong, we're leaving." Admitting you misjudged the season is the rarest skill in outdoor content. This guy treats it like a route change, which it is.

Field Marshal Helmuth von Moltke the Elder is credited with the line that no plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force. The hostile force, in this case, is Montana in November. It made first contact, and the Canada plan did not survive the meeting. A cold front doesn't care about your content calendar.

### What the Video Doesn't Tell You

I'm not going to pretend otherwise by inventing a second opinion. The video doesn't say which railroad they're riding, how long they've been stopped, or exactly when they expect to reach Chicago, and I'm not going to guess any of that for you.

One more note on the "slow" part. At one point the narrator marvels at how fast the train is going and then walks it back, because the point of the joke is that it's standing still. The train spends a good chunk of the video doing the Romero zombie routine: slow, relentless, and mostly arriving because you didn't get out of the way. George Romero's dead never ran, and neither does this freight.

## Deeper Cuts

Think of this as the director's cut nobody requested, written by a woman with a Mac Studio and a grudge.

### The Title Promises a Town, the Narrator Delivers Deer

The video is titled "RECON in Havre Montana," and in the transcript nobody says Havre. The narrator says "here," as in "it snowed here," and "up here," as in "before it gets any colder up here." That is the entire geographic briefing. For a video called RECON, the reconnaissance consists of watching deer and a sky. The only places the narration commits to are Chicago, which gets a firm "beeline," and "the Tennessee area," which gets a vague wave of the hand. I've run intel operations with better coordinates, and my network monitoring has a goddamn mold sensor on the patio.

He also says they need to "get off the high line" and never explains it. I'm not going to invent a map for a term he didn't define.

I'm also reading an auto-generated transcript, which at one point records something like "sitting dirty face" and "Tv2." I have no idea what that was and refuse to guess. Even the captions in this video are stalled, mumbling and going nowhere.

### Lucky and Wrong in the Same Breath

The main piece gave you "we were just wrong about that." Here's the part it skipped. In the same monologue he says they're lucky they didn't make the Canada run "because it snowed here," followed by "who knows what Canada is like." So he's wrong about the timing and lucky about the consequence, and he holds both without a flinch.

He doesn't claim to know Canada is bad. He knows it snowed where he's standing and admits he can't see further north. Snow here means probably worse up there, so leave. That's risk management, and I've watched whole engineering teams fail at it with better dashboards.

The Bhagavad Gita, chapter 2, verse 47, says "You have a right to your actions, but never to their fruits." These two had every right to plan the Canada run. They never had a right to the run itself, and the snow cashed that check. The plan was theirs and the outcome belonged to Montana.

### The Chicago Confidence

On the Chicago leg, the wording matters: "we know we can get down into like the tennessee area quickly within like a few days." It's "we know," not "we hope." That reads like experience, someone who has made this run before and has a feel for it. It is also one narrator's estimate on camera, and the clip gives no distances, no schedule, no costs. I'm not going to turn "a few days" into an itinerary, and neither should you. It's a seasoned voice describing a rough sense of the trip, not a timetable. The race he describes is with the temperature, "before it gets any colder up here." He sounds ahead of it, but only just.

Then there's the Canada rain check, which he pushes to "next year or something." I admire the or-something. That's the exact commitment level of every project I've ever been told is "on the roadmap."

Rule of Acquisition #26, if you'll indulge the Ferengi, says the vast majority of the rich in this galaxy did not inherit their wealth; they stole it. Nobody in this clip has inherited or stolen a damn thing. The only riches on offer are a pink line in the sky and a few deer, and neither of them can be invoiced. Quark would walk out of this video bored and broke.

### What the Armchair Gets

A route got rewritten mid-sentence, from a northern run to a hub in Chicago to a dash south, in under two minutes of talking. The clip shows what changing plans looks like on the move: no apology, no drama, and no pretending the sunset footage will look like the real thing. Heghlu'meH QaQ jajvam is Klingon for "today is a good day to die," and it's the correct toast for a sunset you couldn't film. It died gloriously, and the train took off before the eulogy.

Anything past this would be fiction, and I don't write fiction for free.
