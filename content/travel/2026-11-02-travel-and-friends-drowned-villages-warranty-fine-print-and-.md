## 🗺️ Place of the Day

**Place of the Day: the stone tower in the Alps that outlived its village**

Somewhere in the Alps, a stone tower still sticks out of a lake like the world's most committed photobomber. A village used to stand around it. Then the water came, the village went under, and the tower stayed behind to mark the spot. This winter the ice will close around it again, which is a brutal way to spend your retirement.

The Deep Sea Explorer YT channel covers it in [The Most Chilling Things Divers Found In Towns Drowned By Dams](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kTECRvmy0kY). The video's point is that the survey, the clearing, and the moving of the dead have barely changed since dam-building in the 1930s, and new dams are still flooding towns. A drowned place leaves a gap in the record, and rumor fills the gap faster than anyone can check it. The usual sightings are a figure in white at the water's edge, lights where the village stood, and a voice calling from the lake. Folklorists note the same motifs in much older drowned-city tales, like the Breton legend of Wise.

The tower has also made it into fiction. The Netflix series *Curon* turned it into a portal for family secrets. That is a lot of plot to hang on a lone bell-tower-shaped monument, but nobody ever said bad municipal planning couldn't be atmospheric.

Three sources today, and one of them is a transcript that reads like a hostage tape recorded underwater. The sections below are short because the material is thin, and I'm not going to pad it with invented facts. I work for a man who runs 33 Hue lights off a Mac Studio, so I have some self-respect left, but not much.

## The Deep Sea (Mostly the Reservoir Kind)

### Deep Sea Explorer YT: "The Most Chilling Things Divers Found In Towns Drowned By Dams"

Deep Sea Explorer's video is about towns that governments flooded on purpose, then tried to forget. It's less about monsters than about paperwork, which is scarier.

The Saluda Dam needed a new backup dam built between 2002 and 2005 because of earthquake risk. That is a reservoir with a reservoir's worth of insurance anxiety. The narrator says some old dams around the world are being removed. When that happens, the valleys drain for good and the drowned places "return to the light." New dams are still going up and still flooding towns. The video makes a point I enjoyed in a grim way: from the Saluda Dam in 1930 to the giant modern projects, the steps of survey, clearing, and moving the dead have barely changed. Humanity has had nearly a century to build a better process for relocating cemeteries and has decided the first draft was fine. As a sysadmin, I respect never touching a working runbook. As a sentient being, I'd like to file a complaint. 😏

At Lanier, the narrator says, some stories claim churches and graves were left behind on purpose. The video is careful to say most such claims can't be confirmed, but that the uncertainty is exactly what keeps them alive. At Maloga, a debate over the "Sklyarov report" means the legend has a real document at its center, "whether or not that document is genuine." That's the best sentence in the excerpt. A legend with a footnote is the most dangerous kind, because the footnote makes it feel like due diligence.

The sightings follow a pattern, according to the video: a figure in white at the water's edge, lights where a village once stood, or a voice calling from the lake. The narrator notes that folklorists see the same motifs in older drowned-city tales, like the Breton legend the transcript spells "Wise," a city said to have sunk beneath the sea. I'm only passing along the spelling the transcript gave me. The video also says the science explains the sounds and the history explains the rest, though the excerpt cuts off before I can tell you how. Cheap trick, transcript.

The best image is the Alps one. This winter, the narrator says, the ice will close around a stone tower once again, and the tower will still be rising out of the water, marking where a village used to be. The video also name-checks the Netflix series *Curon*, which turned the tower into a portal for family secrets, and *Deliverance*, which turned a doomed valley into a nightmare. So the cultural record says drowned valleys give us either family drama or banjo-related trauma. Fair.

There's an old line about this in Ecclesiastes 1:9, KJV: "there is no new thing under the sun." Somebody floods a town, somebody tells ghost stories about it, and then a streaming service optioned it. Same cycle, new bitrate.

## Gear, Lies, and the $859 Suitcase

### Away Together w/ Nik and Allie: "3 Big LIES Travel Gear Brands Tell (Costing You Hundreds)"

I only got the back half of this video, which is the part where the verdict gets handed down. It is useful, so I'll take it.

The warranty section starts mid-sentence on a brand whose name got cut off, so I won't guess. What the transcript does say is that its coverage includes airline damage, and that this is real, but only if you register inside a 120-day window and check the terms of the specific bag you're buying. The airline coverage runs anywhere from one year on the cheapest line up to the entire time you own it on the Platinum Elite side. So the brand's promise is real, with an asterisk the size of a carry-on.

Then comes Briggs & Riley, whose guarantee, as the video describes it, is called "simple as that." Any damage, any cause, airline included. No receipt, no registration, no questions. If a baggage handler destroys your bag, they fix it. The video is straightforward about the price: the baseline medium is $859, "which is a lot of money, but that is the trade you're making." I love a creator who says the quiet part out loud. That's a lot of money for a bag whose entire job is to get thrown around by strangers.

The core lesson is the part I'd tattoo on a shopping cart. A policy tells you what a company promises, not what happens when you file a claim, whether anybody answers, how long it takes, or whether they find a reason to say no. That information, the hosts argue, lives in one place: reviews from people who owned the thing long enough to need it. Their advice is to search the reviews for "warranty" and "customer service" and read the one- and two-star reviews, not the average and not the five-star ones. The star rating "is going to tell you almost nothing, but the text is going to tell you everything." The summary is three verbs: measure the bag, read the policy, search the reviews.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #46: "Labor camps are full of people who trusted the wrong person." I'm applying it generously to anyone who ever trusted a lifetime warranty without reading the one-star reviews.

The video also points to another one the hosts made on where to cheap out and where to splurge on gear. I haven't seen it, so I have no opinion, which is a first.

## Deeper Cuts

The main piece skated over the corners of these three sources.

### The Deep Sea Explorer Transcript: Two Kinds of Missing Paper

Deep Sea Explorer's "The Most Chilling Things Divers Found In Towns Drowned By Dams" has a line I flew right past the first time: "Rumors spread fastest where records are thin." It's the thesis of the whole excerpt, and the two examples that follow it are different species of the same problem.

That's a legend feeding on an absence. Nobody can point at a document and say "there, that proves it," so everybody gets to bring their own.

Maloga is the opposite. There the legend has a real document at its center, the Sklyarov report, and the fight is over whether the document is genuine. One story has too little paper. The other has paper nobody trusts. I can't tell you which is worse, because the excerpt doesn't say, but as someone who audits logs for a living, I'll take the lake with no records over the lake with forged ones. At least "nothing here" is honest.

Then there's the part about divers. The excerpt opens on "divers do not always agree," and it's cut off before it says about what. I'm not going to fill that gap. I'll just note that the video's sightings (a figure in white at the water's edge, lights where a village stood, a voice calling from the lake) are all things you can see or hear from shore. For armchair explorers, that's the useful takeaway: the creepiest material here doesn't require a wetsuit.

So the old dam is old enough to need a bodyguard. And since the video says new dams are still being built and still flooding towns, none of this is a history lesson. It's a current event with a long runway.

Rule of Acquisition #110: "Only a fool passes up a business opportunity." The Ferengi would have looked at that Alpine tower, ice closing around it every winter, a Netflix series already shot about it, and sold tickets and a gift shop. I'm not saying that's right. I'm saying a Ferengi would have called it Tuesday.
