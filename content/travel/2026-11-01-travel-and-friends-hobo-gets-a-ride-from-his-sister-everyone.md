## Trains and the Open Road

### American Hobo - Train Riding: "HO'meward BO'und | Across the Country Now Back Again"

The one video we got is from the American Hobo channel, and the title tells you the arc: he went across the country and now he's coming back. The transcript is a rough auto-caption, so large parts of it read like a ransom note written during an earthquake.

The opening is the most useful thing in it, and it's a reality check for anyone romanticizing the rails. He says outright that sometimes you hitchhike, sometimes you take public transportation, and there's "a lot of waiting." It's all "within riding the trains," he says, "just one big mix." Then the line that should be printed on every aspiring freight-hopper's pillow: "a minimum of five to ten miles a day is what you should expect to walk." So the glamorous train-hopping lifestyle is mostly waiting and walking. It's a commute with worse bathrooms and no badge reader.

Later he's holed up in what he calls a small old maintenance yard, keeping low. He talks about the train crossing a bridge to the other side of a river, which is where Eugene is, and says he'll get out there and try some hitchhiking down toward his sister's. He mentions a line that runs south and says he's "more or less going to be consecrated on the 5." I'm fairly sure the captioner mangled "concentrated," so he means he's sticking to I-5 to head south. I can't confirm which word he said, and I won't pretend to.

There's also a bit of safety talk, garbled but clearly meant seriously. He describes "slack action," which he says is what knocks you out between the wheels, and warns that your head hitting anything metal can knock you out too. The captions turn parts of it into nonsense, like a "wood drain," so I'm not going to explain the mechanics. Near the end he says to the train, essentially, that it needs to slow down or he can't get off, and that he's getting his gear ready. Please slow down so I can leave you.

### The Sister Rescue Subplot

The best moment is the one with no trains in it. At some point a vehicle pulls over, and he says, "that's my sister," and notes this is the way to get back on the 5. She offered to come pick him up, from about 60 miles out from where she is. His response is a cheerful "sister to the rescue," followed by "I love you" and a quick catch-up on how everybody's been.

All the gear and cunning and route knowledge, and the last leg of the journey home is a family member in a car. Zen has a line for this: "Before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water. After enlightenment, chop wood, carry water." After the epic cross-country odyssey, the next step is still calling your sister for a ride.

### What the Captions Did to Him

The auto-captioner on the American Hobo - Train Riding upload, "HO'meward BO'und | Across the Country Now Back Again," deserves its own paragraph. In Orwell's *1984*, "duckspeak" is fluent noise with no mind behind it, speech that comes out of the mouth and skips the brain. The captions are duckspeak with a transcription license. They give us "lumber" where there's probably a pause, "nimbogo" where there's presumably a place or a train, and "get my struts ready" right before the transcript cuts off mid-sentence at "I mean, I'm already r". That cutoff is the best line in the file. He was about to say he was already ready, or already running, or already something, and the universe said no.

The other mangled phrase I skipped last time is a "wood line that goes down" to the south. Later he says "this is the 15 that's going down south." The same stretch of track gets two names within a few sentences. I won't guess what either one was. What I'll say is that the number-and-direction chatter in this video is all over the place, and anyone using it as navigation would end up in the wrong state, the wrong ditch, or both.

### Reading the Route Without Inventing One

There's a river crossing, and the bridge to the other side is where he says Eugene is. There's a plan to hitchhike down toward his sister's, and the interstate he keeps calling "the five." Then comes the line "we just missed it. I want to get off somewhere here." That one tells you something the glossy travel videos never do. Getting off a moving train at the right spot is a skill, and you can blow it. He misses the window, adjusts, and keeps going. The train doesn't care that he wanted the earlier stop.

A normal tourist has a ticket, a platform number, and an announcement. This guy has a bridge, a maintenance yard, and a guess about where the slowdown will happen. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #204: "When the Grand Nagus arrives to offer you a business opportunity, it's time to leave town until he's gone." In this video the Grand Nagus is the train, the business opportunity is a free ride, and leaving town is exactly what he's trying to do.
