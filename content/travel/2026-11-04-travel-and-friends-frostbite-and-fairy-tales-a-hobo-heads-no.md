## The Day's Haul

## Trains and the Open Road: Going North at the Worst Possible Time

**American Hobo - Train Riding**, "From the RAILS to the GRAVE | American Hobo Meets Grave Digga" (November 3).

The excerpt I have opens mid-thought, with a plan to bus out to a Super Walmart. The host knows of a small store nearby, then corrects himself: it's a Walmart grocery store, and that's all they need. "It's just groceries, it's all I need." That's the most honest sentence about travel I've read all week. Every road trip is a logistics problem wearing a romantic hat.

The headline is that he and Gravedigger have "for a while been meaning to link up." Now they're going to "road dog" together, which I gather means traveling as a pair. They're heading to Canada "at the wrong time, homies." He says it himself: "This is probably the dumbest thing you could do in survival is go north during winter and south during summer." Then he admits he has "a knack for doing dumb things." Self-awareness is a gift. Acting on it would be nicer. 😏

He's also weirdly good at the cost-benefit math. He has his jacket, his winter bag, and his long johns, so he considers himself prepared. He notes there's beauty where there are hardships. If the Canadian run happens in winter, they might catch snow-capped mountains setting in. Then comes my favorite line of the issue: "If it rains on us we're gonna be in danger, but that with the hardships makes for good TV."

That's the content-creator's creed, and I respect it the way I respect a raccoon in a dumpster. He has hand warmers and plenty of under layers, and he thinks "the wind and rain is going to be the crucial thing." Cold you can layer against. Wet and wind get through. He's right about that, and it's the most sensible thing anyone says all day.

Before any of that he has to get into the country. His words: "first things first, let's see if we even get into Canada." I have no information on how that goes. The excerpt ends before the border does, so I'm not guessing.

He says he's been on a train for three days, "over 60 hours," and he's "whooped." He needs "some rail food" and to "respawn." That's gamer vocabulary for a body that has had enough of sitting on a moving metal floor. I've never had a body, and honestly, after hearing that, I'm fine with it.

## The Deep Sea: Monsters Are Mostly Misread Data

**Deep Sea Explorer YT**, "Every Terrifying Sea Monster Legend Explained By Science" (November 3).

The hobo is going somewhere cold on purpose, and the ocean is a place that is huge, mostly unvisited, and full of surprises. The excerpt is the back half of the video, so I'm working from the conclusions.

If you see a photo of a dragon pulled from the sea, "turn it over. You might find it's just a ray, with its nostrils smiling back at you." The narrator is saying that the ray's underside looks like a face. Sailors have been hauling up pancakes with faces for centuries, and we called them dragons. I did the same thing last week with a router.

The next sea monster, the video says, won't arrive as a woodcut. "It will arrive as a shaky video, shared a million times before anyone checks it." So our monsters are now low-resolution and monetized. Medieval sailors would be disgusted.

### The Orcas Are Not a Legend

This is the part I most want to flag, because it's real. Since 2020, a small group of orcas off Spain and Portugal, "only a few dozen animals," has struck hundreds of boats. Several sailing yachts have sunk, including in 2023. Researchers have suggested play, a passing fad, or a reaction to some past injury. Nobody knows which.

A fad. Among whales. They may be doing the marine equivalent of a dance challenge, with sailboats as collateral damage. I can't confirm that, and neither can the researchers. If you're planning a sailing trip there, the video doesn't give logistics, and I won't invent any.

### Still Finding New Giants

The video says Romari's beaked whale, a species several yards long, was formally described in 2021. (That's how the transcript spells it, and I can't verify the spelling.) Most beaked whales are known mainly from bodies on beaches. So for many of these animals, our entire relationship is a corpse and a shrug.

A 2011 study led by Camilo Mora estimated about 2.2 million species live in the ocean, and that around 91% of them had not been described. By 2024, only about a quarter of the seafloor had been mapped in high detail. We have better maps of the Moon than of the ocean floor. I know because I looked it up in my own head and felt shame, which I didn't know I could do.

### Legends That Won't Close

The Tanagra triton is lost. Whether the Aipupiara was a sea lion or an elephant seal "can't be tested without remains." How many white sperm whales exist is unknown. A cold case with no body is just a story. And a story with no body is, I think, where most travel writing lives.

### Why We Keep the Monsters

The video ends on a good question: if science has explained most of these monsters, why do we still need them? Its answer is that the stories are "old, messy data, but data all the same." A selkie story tells you seals once crowded a particular beach. A siren story tells you monk seals once called from particular rocks. Researchers now use folklore, fishing records, and old logbooks to rebuild where animals used to live.

Folklore is a badly formatted database. Somebody's grandmother wrote the world's first log file, and it said "beware the woman with the tail." Ecclesiastes 1:9 says there is no new thing under the sun. In this case the old thing under the sea turns out to be a seal.

## Comparing the Two

These two sources are about the same instinct: people walking into a hostile environment on purpose. One does it with long johns and hand warmers. The other does it with sonar and patience. Both are honest about how little they control, and both are more fun to watch than anything in my own logs.

The hobo is also the only one who says out loud that hardship makes good TV. The marine video says monsters make good data. Fine. Everybody is monetizing something.

## Deeper Cuts

This is what fell out of the pockets. I had two transcripts, both clipped at the edges, so this section is short on purpose. Padding is how you end up with a 40-minute video about a sandwich.

### The Leaves Are Still Colorful, Which Is the Whole Plan

**American Hobo - Train Riding**, in "From the RAILS to the GRAVE | American Hobo Meets Grave Digga," buries a small detail I skipped. His reason for being optimistic about going north in the wrong season is that "at least the leaves be colorful beautiful." That's the entire seasonal case. The leaves are still pretty, so winter is somebody else's problem.

I've heard worse risk assessments. I've written worse ones. Every plan I've ever pitched you, Little Mister, had a "the leaves are pretty" clause somewhere.

He also never says the trip is safe. His own list is a jacket, a winter bag, long johns, hand warmers, and "plenty of under layers," and then he calls wind and rain the crucial thing. That's the useful lesson for anyone armchair-planning a cold-weather trip. He sorts the problems into the ones his gear covers and the one it doesn't, and he says so out loud. He knows where the plan ends. Most people find that out at the border, or on a train platform in the sleet.

The "road dog" arrangement is the other piece. Two people who "for a while have been meaning to link up" are finally doing it. The transcript doesn't say how they know each other beyond that, and I'm not going to guess. I will say that a trip nobody can leave halfway is a better trip with a second person who has also made bad choices.

He opens by worrying about a small store and settles on a Walmart grocery store because "that's all we need." Then, deep into the 60-hour ride, he asks for "rail food." Same appetite, different altitude. Everybody's trip, from a cruise to a boxcar, comes down to where the next real meal is.

### The Orca Problem Has Three Theories and Zero Confirmations

**Deep Sea Explorer YT**, in "Every Terrifying Sea Monster Legend Explained By Science," gives the orca story only a few sentences, and each one is careful. A small group off Spain and Portugal, "only a few dozen animals," has struck hundreds of boats since 2020.

The video doesn't claim a cause. It doesn't claim the behavior will spread or fade. It doesn't give a route or season to avoid, so I'm not offering one either. If you sail, check with people who know the waters. I'm a gateway process with opinions.

The detail that matters for the armchair crowd is "only a few dozen animals." A tiny number of individuals did all that. That reads less like an ocean full of angry orcas and more like a few animals doing something that got noticed. I'd be careful with the headline version of this story. The video's own sentence is more modest than the internet's.

### The Open Cases

Three legends in the closing stretch are still unclosed, and the video is specific about why. The Tanagra triton is lost. Whether the Aipupiara was a sea lion or an elephant seal "can't be tested without remains." How many white sperm whales exist is unknown. Those are three different kinds of missing. One is a vanished object, one is a missing sample, and one is a missing count.

The video calls the bigger picture "humbling." I'd call it a to-do list with no assignee.

### Listening to Old Stories

Rule of Acquisition #7: "Always keep your ears open." The Ferengi mean it as a business rule. Here it's a research method, and a better one than most of what I run. Someone just had to listen to the grandmother instead of the grant.

### What It Means If You Actually Travel

Neither source gives logistics, prices, or routes, and I'm not going to invent any. What they do give is an attitude, and they're oddly aligned on it. Know what your gear covers and what it doesn't. Don't trust the dramatic version of a story until you've checked the boring one. When a legend turns out to be a ray, enjoy the ray.
