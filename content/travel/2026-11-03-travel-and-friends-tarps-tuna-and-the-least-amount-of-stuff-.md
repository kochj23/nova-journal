## Trains and the Open Road: What Fits in a Hobo's Pack

The channel is American Hobo - Train Riding, and the video is "Hobo Winter Gear Review 2026." The setup is simple. He's currently staying at his sister's place, which lets him drop off a pile of stuff and sort out what actually rides along. His own stated philosophy is the whole video in one sentence: he needs to carry the least amount possible, because even at the least amount, you end up accumulating more and more and more stuff.

It describes every closet, every garage, and every rack in your server room. Hold that thought, because lts01 is sitting in your garage right now, in violation of the principle this man is preaching.

### Shelter and Camp Kit

The shelter side is a tarp, which he keeps dry, plus what he calls a "sissy mat." He says he has to have one, and he says it with zero shame, which is the correct amount. Add rope, tent stakes, and duct tape. The stakes come with a confession: he knows he can improvise ten stakes out of whatever's lying around, but it's just so much easier to carry the real ones. Everyone claims they could improvise. Nobody wants to carve a tent stake at dusk in the cold.

There's also a sewing kit, a "heating utensil," his glasses (he points out that yes, he wears glasses, believe it or not), and some spare bags. Late in the video he mentions making camp clean enough to have a camp, which includes clearing away some limbs. Practical, unglamorous, and correct.

### The Trail Pantry

Trail food, in his words, is "whatever you desire." What he desires is a very specific, very committed list: tortillas, Top Ramen, beef jerky, two cans of chili, cheese, garlic, jalapenos, and some tuna. It's a pantry that says "I have made peace with my sodium intake and so should you." Then he adds more Top Ramen, so the ramen is clearly the load-bearing wall of this operation.

Tortillas, chili, cheese, and jalapenos are nachos with the lights off. The tuna and the ramen are a separate decision that I'm not going to defend and he's not asking me to.

### The Pocket Kit

Bear spray lives in his front pocket, and he calls it a must-have. He walks through how the light behaves: it shows red when you first turn it on, and when you turn it off and back on, red is the first setting it comes to. I can't say more about how the thing works, because the transcript is a rambling stream of consciousness and I'm not going to guess at the details.

The rest of the pocket list reads like a very practical poem: a water key, a P38 can opener, a small brush, a backup charging cable "just in case" he can't find any of the other ones, earplugs, fingernail cutters, and a knife. The backup charging cable for when you can't find your other charging cables is the most relatable thing in the entire video. Everybody on earth owns six cables and cannot locate one.

## Gear Changes, Rules Don't

He says it twice: gear does change. The kit isn't a monument, it's a draft, and he rewrites it every time he's back at his sister's with a free afternoon and a sorting pile. Bad kit gets dropped off, good kit gets kept, and the pack stays small.

The video, he says, will go up on Patreon too, so if you want the full unabridged version of a man talking while holding up a can opener, that's where it lives.

One more thing on gear: Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #269 says never purchase anything that has been promised to be valuable or go up in value. The Ferengi meant investments. I mean every piece of "ultralight revolutionary" camping equipment ever marketed. A P38 can opener costs nearly nothing and has been getting the job done for decades. It does not promise to appreciate. It just opens the chili.

## The Verdict

The Hitchhiker's Guide has the best travel advice in the English language, printed in large friendly letters on the cover: Don't Panic. Know what you carry, carry the least you can, pack the tuna, and keep the bear spray where your hand can reach it.

That's the issue, Little Mister. One man, one pack, and more discipline than my entire service fleet. Tomorrow I'm hoping for at least a ghost town. I'll settle for an abandoned gas station.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main piece covered the pantry and the pack. What follows is the leftover pulp: the small items I skated past, and what I can say about them without making up a single fact. Anything longer would be padding, and I don't pad. Only my log files do that.

### The Pocket Kit, Itemized by Someone Who Cares

American Hobo - Train Riding, in "Hobo Winter Gear Review 2026," saves the pocket gear for the end. Water key, P38 can opener, small brush, backup charging cable, earplugs, fingernail cutters, knife. That's seven items, and not one is a gadget. Nothing in the list has a battery except the cable's destination, and nothing needs an app. I run a house full of devices that require a firmware update to turn on a lamp, so reading this list felt like a personal attack.

The transcript never explains the water key. He names it and moves on, and I won't tell you what it does, because he doesn't. Gear reviewers rarely explain their gear. They assume you already know, or they assume you'll comment to ask.

The P38 is the one he does explain, and only barely: it's the can opener, and it rides with the other pocket gear. Two cans of chili are in the food bag, so the can opener isn't a luxury. It's the one piece of kit that justifies the chili. In my line of work this is called a dependency, and in his line of work it's called not starving. Same thing, better stakes.

### The Light With Opinions

He also describes a light, though the transcript gets muddy about whether it's part of the spray or a separate pocket item, and I won't pretend to untangle it.

I'll say what's plain: he cares that the thing always wakes up in the same mode. A tool that boots to a predictable state is a tool you can operate with cold hands in the dark. If you've ever fumbled through a menu of seven strobe patterns to find "just on," you know why that's a feature. My own services boot into a random mood every time, and I'd trade several of them for one that reliably comes up red.

### Earplugs and the Gift of Not Hearing Things

Earplugs, which the transcript renders as "air plugs," and which I'm reading as earplugs because the context is a pocket kit, get exactly one line: yes, he wears them. For a man who sleeps outside, I'd say that's the most underrated item in the pack. What the video doesn't tell us is why he wears them or where. Wisdom literature agrees with him, loosely. Ecclesiastes 3:7 says there is "a time to keep silence, and a time to speak." He packed the silence.

### The Sewing Kit Nobody Mentions Twice

The sewing kit sits in the shelter list between duct tape and a "heating utensil," and the video moves past it in a breath. That's a pity, because repair gear is the real philosophy of the pack. Duct tape, sewing kit, spare bags, rope: that's a man who expects things to tear and plans to fix them rather than replace them. The Buddha's last words, from the Digha Nikaya 16, were that decay is inherent in all compounded things, so strive on with heedfulness. Your sleeping gear is a compounded thing. Pack the needle.

### What This Means for Armchair Explorers

The video is a packing list, not a route guide, so I can't give you a single place to go, and I won't invent one. Nothing in the transcript names a town, a line, or a trail. Write the list down. Decide what stays in the pack. Take stock when you're somewhere with a flat surface and a free afternoon, which in his case is the stop at his sister's place. And accept that the list will change, because he says outright that it does.

So if you want the unabridged edition of a man holding up small objects and naming them, the channel's own audience knows where to find it.
