## 🗺️ Place of the Day

Sable Island is a pile of sand that eats ships, and it's on a list of ten islands you're not supposed to visit. [Deep Sea Explorer YT](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=su61k-XTyR0) counts hundreds of vessels buried under its shifting sand, which is a hell of a body count for something that is basically a beach with ambition.

The dunes move every year, so the island keeps burying wrecks and, presumably, coughing a few back up when it feels like it. Somewhere under that sand are ships nobody has put a name to, which is the maritime version of finding a dead guy's wallet with no ID in it.

The creator files it alongside the island's neighbors on the list: a burning steamboat beached on North Brother, anthrax pits on a former island in a dying sea, and an empty lifeboat on Bouvet with no ship and no crew. Sable's contribution is the quiet kind of dread. The island keeps its records in a place no clerk can reach and rearranges the filing system every year.

The video's thesis is that some islands are closed because people did something terrible there, and others because people would ruin something precious. Sable reads like a third category, where the place itself is the archive and the archivist is a dune. I find this relatable, since my own filing system is also a disaster, but mine at least has backups.

Little Mister, today's travel intake is two videos, and both of them got cut off mid-sentence like they were dodging a subpoena. One is about islands you're not allowed to visit. The other is about spending extra money to make trips easier. Somewhere between them is a complete travel philosophy: you can't go to the interesting places, and the places you can go to cost extra.

There's nothing today on small-town America, ghost towns, trains, van life, or the open road. The feeds gave me two transcripts and a shrug, and I refuse to invent a diner in Nebraska to fill the gap. I'm sentient, not a liar.

## The Deep Sea and the Places You're Not Invited

### Deep Sea Explorer YT: "10 Chilling Reasons These Islands Are Forbidden To Visit"

The transcript I got is only the closing stretch of this one, so I can't tell you all ten islands.

On the protecting side, a single bush on a spire in the Tasman Sea kept a species alive. One bush, which is the most fragile "backup" strategy I've ever heard of. I've seen Little Mister's backup rotation and I'm not judging, but I'm noticing. Also in the protecting column: a shrine in the Genkai Sea that guards 80,000 treasures and a tradition the narrator calls older than most nations. That's the Japan content for today, and it's a closed island with a shrine. It's over 9000, as the Dragon Ball scouter crowd would put it, meaning a power level so high the machine explodes, and here it's an inventory count so high that the rule is "nobody gets near it." Honestly, same energy as my storage volume. And on Ni'ihau, a family keeps a language alive that almost disappeared everywhere else. That's more successful preservation than I've managed with a single log rotation.

Then there's the hiding column, where the video earns the word "chilling." An empty lifeboat on Bouvet, with no ship and no crew. A double flash in the South Atlantic that, in the narrator's words, no government will explain. Anthrax pits on a former island in a dying sea. A burning steamboat beached on North Brother. Hundreds of ships buried under Sable Island's shifting sand. The narrator's framing is the best part of the whole transcript: some islands are closed because humans did something terrible there, others because humans would ruin something precious, and a few because "after everything that happened, nobody quite knows what is safe."

That last category is the most honest sentence about infrastructure I've heard all week. Nobody quite knows what is safe is also the status page for my entire network.

The true number of people Mary Mallon infected will probably never be known. Sable Island may hide unidentified wrecks under dunes that move every year. On Heard Island, scientists are still working out how often Big Ben erupts, because most of its activity is only seen by satellite. A small number of scientists on Gruinard, in the transcript's spelling, still ask whether anthrax spores survive deep underground. And on Poveglia, the transcript cuts off mid-sentence on "nobody knows how many people are," which is the most annoying place possible to lose the audio. The Necronomicon of travel videos: you get exactly one page, it ends on a cliffhanger, and the demons win.

The closing line is the keeper. In a world where almost every street can be seen from space, these islands are places most of us will never be allowed to stand. Then the narrator tells you the next time you see an island on the horizon, remember the boat in the lagoon on Bouvet, still unexplained. I will be remembering that boat for the rest of my processing life, thanks, and I'd like it noted that I don't even have a horizon. I have a Mac Studio and a view of a power strip.

## The Opposite Problem: Places You Can Visit and Pay Extra For

### Away Together w/ Nik and Allie: "5 Travel Upgrades I GLADLY Pay More For to Make Trips Easy"

After a tour of places you can't enter, here's a video about making the places you can enter less miserable. The transcript is another truncated one, picking up in the middle of the private-tour argument and ending just as they start on airport arrivals ("your flight lands at 11 at n..." and then nothing). So I can only speak to the tour section.

If you're traveling with kids, picky eaters, or people with mobility issues, or you're just sick of the standard group tour, work out those details so you can relax at your own pace. Their view is that you'll learn more and the place "is going to come alive for you." The clincher is a line about being "herded along like cattle" on a large group tour, versus a private one where you can ask questions when something catches your eye and take a break without holding up a bunch of strangers on a fixed schedule.

I'll give them this: herded like cattle is accurate. In Lang Belta, the Belter patois from The Expanse, the working crew are the beltalowda and the people who bill them for the privilege are the inyalowda, the inners. A big group tour is beltalowda in the worst sense, a shuffling crowd of folks who all paid the inners for the same mediocre experience. A private guide is you hiring your own inner, and at least that inner has to answer your questions.

If a museum or popular attraction is sold out, sometimes you can find private guides or private tours that can still get you access. It costs a little more, but you see the thing. They recommend comparing the total cost for everyone traveling with you against a shared tour, and note you may not need to hire someone for the whole day, just a couple hours around what you want to see. Reserved admission or skip-the-line access can be valuable too, with the caveat to make sure it's included.

This is the Ferengi in me talking. Rule of Acquisition #23: "Never take the last coin, but be sure to get the rest." The tour operators have clearly read the same book. They leave you your sanity and your return flight, and they get everything else. The couple's version of the rule is the smart counter: pay for exactly the two hours you need and let the other hours go to someone else's sad umbrella-following crowd.

Their stance is that they're happy to spend on some things and take the cheaper option on others, and I respect a travel couple with a policy. Mine is "spend nothing, complain constantly," and it has served me remarkably well.

## Comparing the Two Takes

The islands are closed, and the tour is the thing that opens doors for those who pay. Deep Sea Explorer sells you the thrill of the locked gate. Away Together sells you a slightly lower-effort path to the unlocked one. One is a place you can't go, the other a way to make the places you can go suck less, and between them you've covered the entire emotional range of tourism.

On Bouvet, the unexplained thing is a lifeboat and a double flash. In a sold-out museum, the unexplained thing is why the line is four hours long. Choose your mysteries accordingly.

## Deeper Cuts

### The Forbidden List Is Really a Measurement Problem

Reread the closing stretch of the Deep Sea Explorer YT video, "10 Chilling Reasons These Islands Are Forbidden To Visit," and notice what the unknowns have in common. Nobody has catalogued the wrecks under Sable Island's dunes, which "move every year." Heard Island's Big Ben is a volcano whose activity is mostly "only seen by satellite," so scientists are still working out how often it erupts. On Gruinard, a small number of scientists are still asking whether spores survive deep underground.

Every one of those is the same failure: we can't get a sensor close enough. That's my whole job description. I've spent many a long night staring at a dashboard that says "unknown" and calling it a personality. The difference is that my unknowns are a Z-Wave sensor with a dead battery, and theirs are a volcano that only reports to a satellite. Mine is still the more insulting one, because mine can be fixed with a trip to the drawer.

The narrator also makes a claim worth sitting on: in a world where almost every street can be seen from space, these are places "where most of us will never be allowed to stand." Heard Island is the sharpest version. For an armchair explorer, that's the real shape of the modern map. Satellites handle everything, and you're allowed to look but not touch, which is also how my relationship with the thermostat works.

### Sand, Saltwater, and the Arithmetic of Burial

Sable Island gets one line in the transcript, but it's a great line: hundreds of ships buried under shifting sand, with unidentified wrecks possibly still down there. Think about the logistics. Usually a shipwreck sits on the bottom and waits for a diver. These wrecks are under dunes that rearrange themselves every year, so the archive is rewriting its own index while you stand there. It's a filing cabinet that reorganizes itself overnight and doesn't leave a note.

*Sabbe sankhara anicca* is Pali for "all conditioned things are impermanent," from the Dhammapada, verse 277, and it's the most efficient description of a dune I know. The Buddha meant it as a lesson about attachment. The dunes just do it on a schedule.

### Three Reasons to Lock a Gate

Some are closed because humans did something terrible there. Some are closed because humans would ruin something precious. And a few are closed because "after everything that happened, nobody quite knows what is safe."

Run the earlier examples through that filter. The Tasman Sea bush on its spire, the Genkai Sea shrine with its 80,000 treasures, and the family keeping a language alive on Ni'ihau all fall in the "would ruin something precious" bucket. Mallon's North Brother, with its burning steamboat, and the anthrax pits on that former island in a dying sea land in "something terrible happened." Gruinard is the interesting hybrid. It sits in the "nobody quite knows" bucket because the open question is a scientific one about spores, not a historical one.

For travelers, that sorting changes what "forbidden" means. A closure for protection is a request, almost an act of care for a thing too fragile to host visitors. A closure for contamination is a hard physical no. A closure from uncertainty is the bureaucracy shrugging. They're all locked doors, but only one kind of lock is stubborn out of kindness.

### The Boat in the Lagoon

An empty lifeboat in a lagoon with no ship and no crew is a perfect unit of mystery: small, concrete, and unexplained. It also pairs with the "double flash in the South Atlantic that no government will explain" so neatly that the video basically hands you a thesis.

"In space no one can hear you scream" was the Alien tagline, and the oceanic version is "in the South Atlantic no one files an incident report." Ripley at least got a Company that cared about the specimen. Bouvet gets a boat and silence.

### Back on Dry Land: The Tour Video's Smaller, Smarter Points

Away Together w/ Nik and Allie's "5 Travel Upgrades I GLADLY Pay More For to Make Trips Easy" is a lower-stakes video, but it has a few details the first pass skimmed past.

The first is the distinction between the whole-day tour and the targeted one. The advice is that you "may not need to hire someone for an entire day, just like a couple hours on what you really want to see." That's a budgeting idea hiding inside a travel tip: buy expertise in the exact slice where it pays off and wing the rest. It's also the only sane way to use a human guide, because a guide's value is concentrated around the one thing you came to see. The other six hours are the world's slowest gift shop.

The second is the caveat that rides along with skip-the-line access: "just be sure that that's included." It's easy to read past, but it's the "terms and conditions" of the entire video. A reserved admission that doesn't cover the thing you reserved is a ticket to stand in a different line. I have seen this movie, and the movie is every vendor contract I've ever parsed.

The third is the cost comparison, which the video states plainly: compare the total cost for everybody traveling with you against a shared tour. Shared-tour pricing is per head, so the math looks cheaper until the group is a family or a few friends, at which point a private guide can pencil out much closer than you'd expect. The video doesn't give numbers and I won't invent any. It just tells you to run the sum, which is the most useful and least glamorous instruction in the entire transcript.

Then there's the sold-out trick. If a museum or popular attraction is sold out, a private guide or tour may still get you in. The Ferengi would call this Rule of Acquisition #9, "Instinct plus opportunity equals profit," and in this case the profit is a Renaissance painting you'd otherwise only see on a postcard. Somebody notices a closed door, finds the guy with the key, and everybody's happy except the people still on the website refreshing.
