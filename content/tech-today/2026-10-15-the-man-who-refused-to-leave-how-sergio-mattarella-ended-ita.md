## The Problem With Being the Last Adult in the Room

Here's the thing about being President of Italy in 2022: you basically have to be willing to let every single political faction in parliament hate you equally, and Sergio Mattarella—a mild-mannered jurist who'd rather discuss constitutional law than grab headlines—turned out to be *exactly* the guy Italy needed, which is why the country's fractious coalition partners collectively panicked at the thought of him actually retiring.

Mattarella's first seven-year term (2015–2022) was supposed to be his victory lap. He'd served with quiet dignity through a parade of governments that ranged from chaotic to actively criminal, navigating hung parliaments, populist insurgencies, prime ministerial collapses, and the minor inconvenience of multiple attempts to blackmail him. (Yes, really. Italy's politics doesn't just get weird; it gets *Godfather* weird.) By the time his term wound down in early 2022, he'd become something rare in Italian politics: a figure nobody actively wanted to destroy. That's not affection—it's exhaustion. Everyone knew that whoever succeeded him would immediately become a target, and worse, might actually *try to use the office to consolidate power*.

So when the question surfaced about whether Mattarella would seek a second term, the entire political establishment held its breath like a man waiting to see if the bomb's still ticking.

## The Succession Gauntlet Nobody Wanted

Here's where it gets stupid. The Italian presidency is mostly a figurehead role—real power lives with the Prime Minister—but it's a *crucial* figurehead role, especially when your parliament is a 10-car pileup of competing interests. The president can dissolve parliament, refuse to sign legislation, demand referendums, and most importantly, can veto or delay decisions from unstable coalitions. In other words, the president is Italy's constitutional immune system: useless when things are normal, lifesaving when everything's falling apart.

In 2022, everything was falling apart. The Meloni government had just formed (after various previous coalitions had imploded like houses of cards in a wind tunnel). The far-right Brothers of Italy, the League, and Forza Italia had cobbled together a coalition that everyone knew was ideologically unstable and personally suspicious. Meanwhile, the Five-Star Movement, the Democratic Party, and various splinter factions were watching from the opposition benches, waiting for the government to collapse—which, statistically speaking, Italian governments do about as often as Roman gets robbed in *Law & Order*.

The succession candidates for the presidency were a rogues' gallery of egos, baggage, and mutual suspicion:

- **Pier Ferdinando Casini**, a centrist who'd been bouncing between coalitions like a political pinball, faced questions about whether he could stay independent enough.
- **Giuliano Amato**, respected but elderly and seen by some as too establishment.
- **Sergio Mattarella himself**, who'd said he was done but was increasingly the only guy anyone actually trusted.

The problem: if Mattarella didn't run, whoever won the presidency would be *compromised* from day one, installed by a specific coalition rather than above the fray. And the Meloni government needed a president who wouldn't get twitchy about approving their legislation or dissolving parliament when things got weird (which, again, they definitely would).

It was masterful because it wasn't an argument for *him*; it was an argument for the presidency itself.

He got roughly 900 votes on the second ballot, in a parliament where no single coalition had a majority. That doesn't happen by accident. That happens because every faction calculated that the alternative was worse than rewarding the guy they all secretly wanted to keep in office.

It was the political equivalent of an intervention: "Sergio, we know you want to retire. We're not letting you."

The succession row didn't end because one candidate won and the others lost gracefully. It ended because the entire system realized it was better off with institutional continuity than with settling factional scores. Mattarella's re-election was Italy admitting, without saying a word, that it needed someone *above* politics more than it needed another player in the game.

When Italy re-elected Mattarella—essentially forcing him to stay—it signaled that it valued the presidency as a stabilizing institution over the prime minister's coalition interests. That's not a small move in a country with Italy's history.

Probably both. But it did so with Mattarella as a constitutional check—not an active restraint, but a presence that meant any president-dependent power grab required consensus in a way that it otherwise might not have.

So what did Mattarella's re-election actually *accomplish*? It told the country that at least *some* institutions still mattered more than factional victory. And it forced a guy who wanted to retire to spend another seven years managing a country where the government changes the way most people change socks, the mafia still exists in some regions, and the European Union keeps asking why fiscal discipline keeps getting treated as a suggestion.

The successor row ended not because it was resolved, but because everyone realized that succession itself was the problem. Mattarella's re-election was Italy's way of hitting the snooze button on constitutional reform while hoping the alarm never actually goes off.
