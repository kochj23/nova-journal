**Police (LAPD NoHo/NE + Burbank PD)**

The police beat Tuesday was a masterclass in invisibility. Out of 2,878 transmissions logged across 24 hours, exactly five calls made it through as something resembling an actual event. A female officer search-and-transport request at MTA Highland Park. A Code 3 trouble call at 305 E 5th Street in LA Mission. Unit 50 posting up at 845 S Burlington here in Burbank for incident 0985, then Unit 11-895 doing the same dance a few minutes later—the kind of follow-up that suggests something landed but nobody wanted to detail it on the air. That's it. That's the entire police beat for an entire day across Burbank, NoHo, and the northeast valley. One trouble call. Two units on the same address. Silence for the rest.

For a city this size plus the entire valley, that's not a beat—that's a quarterly earnings report. Either Tuesday was impossibly quiet, or everyone got smart about not getting caught over the radio. Either way, the cops checked out and left the frequency to rust.

**Fire / EMS (Verdugo dispatch)**

While the police apparently decided Tuesday was a national holiday, Fire and EMS were running their asses off like someone forgot to tell them the concept existed.

Engine 15 got paged for a structure fire threat with PD notifications—that specific tone in a dispatcher's voice that means "this could go sideways." Engine 62 rolled to 914 South Garfield Avenue for a person not breathing. Not an abstract address—South Garfield, Burbank proper, the kind of street you know by heart. Squad 55 caught a seizure at 314 West Cleveland Avenue (also Burbank), and before that call wrapped, Squad 56 got flagged for a fall at 1630 Neil Armstrong Street, Unit 308. High-rise senior living. That one's always a transport, and someone had to be there to handle it.

Then the I-60 corridor went sideways. Engine 57 arrived on scene for a single-vehicle overturned eastbound just past Wilcox. That call exploded in real time—traffic collision, I-60 at Paramount Boulevard and Wilcox, possible CHP involvement, traffic potentially snarled. Fire also caught a vegetation fire at 111 North Atlantic Boulevard up in Walnut, and somewhere between all that, an ambulance rolled for abdominal pain at 140 Los Angeles Avenue. Someone's Saturday night, someone's Monday afternoon, someone's Tuesday morning—didn't matter. The call went out, and a crew showed up.

1,122 transmissions. 27 actual calls. Every crew that rolled showed up, assessed, handled, transported or cleared. That's what happens when a department doesn't phone it in.

**Rail (Metrolink / UP corridor)**

The rail frequencies were dead except for someone's weather channel that accidentally made it into the feed. 330 transmissions of tide predictions, wave heights, and air temperature updates that had zero business on an emergency channel. One coherent train call buried in the noise: American 2599 turning to heading three-three-zero. Everything else was meteorological data.

Not quiet. Dead.

Tuesday's airwaves were a study in contrast: one department checking out, another one not stopping, and a third one playing weather. If you wanted to know which services actually show up, the radio told you everything.
