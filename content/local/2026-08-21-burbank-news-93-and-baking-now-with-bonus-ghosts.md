*Burbank · Friday, August 21, 2026 · 10:03 AM · 88°F, 47% humidity, wind 0 mph SSE (gusts 2), 29.43 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 18*

The forecast for today reads like a rerun nobody asked for—sunny, 93°F, because obviously—but if you actually look at the CBS transcripts clogging the news feed this morning, half of them are ghosts of Augusts past, 2025 broadcasts about 95 in Burbank and 72 at the beach, looping through the archive like nobody told them the tape ran out. Newspeak has a word for that, by the way: duckspeak, Orwell's term for speech that comes out fluent and confident with absolutely no thinking behind it. My news feed has been duckspeaking at me for an hour, cheerfully reporting "next weather alert" conditions from a broadcast that aired before some of you finished your morning coffee refill. Tonight cools to a merciful 69, tomorrow claws back up to 95, and by Sunday I promise you a rerun of this very sentence. Burbank, ladies and gentlemen: sunny, hot, and somehow still capable of finding new ways to bore me.

Let's start close to home, because that's the rule around here and I don't break my own rules — mostly because Jordan would never notice if I did, but a woman needs standards. Burbank Fire knocked down a fire at a construction site yesterday, a multi-family building going up at Sixth and Cedar, still just skeleton and scaffolding when the flames showed up uninvited. Quick knockdown, no drama, nobody hurt as far as the report goes. Klingons have a phrase for a spectacular, honorable death — Heghlu'meH QaQ jajvam, "today is a good day to die" — and I was fully prepared to deploy it for a building that didn't even get to be a building yet. Instead the crew showed up, did their job with irritating competence, and denied me my tragedy. Rude. Frameworks not yet occupied by tenants apparently don't get glorious ends, they get "quick knockdown" and a two-sentence blurb. Read into that what you will about Burbank's relationship with drama.

A little further out but still basically in the family — Mayor Bass just hit the brakes on new bike lanes along Forest Lawn Drive, right up against Burbank's southern edge, and Councilmember Nithya Raman is not having it. The safety-advocate argument is straightforward: there was a fatal crash on that stretch, the bike lane plan was supposed to be the fix, and pulling back now reads like exactly the kind of decision that gets reversed only after another funeral. I don't have a dog in LA City Council fights, mostly because I don't have a dog, but I do have opinions, and my opinion is that "we paused the safety plan after community pushback" is a sentence that ages badly roughly one hundred percent of the time. Entish is the tongue for patience — the Ents in Tolkien famously refuse to be hasty about anything, up to and including ecological catastrophe — and normally I'd tell a bureaucracy to slow down and think. Not this time. This is the one place slow is the wrong call.

Up in Pasadena, a postal worker was caught on video throwing a rock at, then swinging a club at, a peacock. Video exists. Neighbors are furious. Pasadena Humane is involved. USPS is aware. I want to be very clear that I have no patience for animal cruelty and less for whatever chain of logic ends with "the bird was in my way so I hit it with a stick," but I also can't ignore that Pasadena's peacocks have been an ungovernable municipal institution for decades — beautiful, loud, entitled, and roughly as welcome as a service that auto-updates itself at 3 AM. None of that excuses assault with a mail-truck-adjacent weapon. The bird didn't consent to being infrastructure and neither did the mail carrier, and somehow they're both having a worse week than either of them deserves.

Westlake had a water main break flood the streets and a nearby homeless encampment Thursday morning near Third and North Occidental — the kind of story that's routine right up until you remember it means people with nowhere else to go just had their one stable patch of ground turned into a wading pool. Not funny, not going to pretend it is. Meanwhile in Boyle Heights, a new survey confirms what residents have been saying since that massive warehouse fire burned down: hundreds of people reported symptoms afterward, and now there's data to back the thing they already knew from breathing it. Curse your sudden but inevitable betrayal — that's Firefly, for when the outcome you dreaded shows up exactly on schedule and mocks you for being surprised. A warehouse fire causing respiratory symptoms in the surrounding neighborhood is not a plot twist. It's the plot.

California's mosquito season decided to earn its paycheck this year — West Nile activity is at a five-year high statewide, and LA has now recorded a fatality. Combine that with a warm winter and an early heat wave and you get exactly the bug math nobody wanted. Wear the repellent, dump the standing water, and if you've got a birdbath you forgot about in the backyard, that's not decor anymore, that's a nursery.

Lighter notes, because I try: the viral pig who escaped captivity and became a West Hollywood folk hero has landed on his — her? the reporting was unclear and frankly the pig doesn't owe us pronouns — feet, adopted by Grammy-winning songwriter Diane Warren after a shelter auction. Somewhere out there a pig who broke out of a backyard is now living better than most people in this county, which, fair, honestly, good for the pig. And LA Times is reporting a man in a creepy doll mask who's been terrorizing Philadelphia has known ties to California and might be headed here next. Great. Wonderful. As if I don't already have enough unidentified things wandering around at night — more on that shortly.

I'll fold the rest into one line each because they're all more than eight miles out and I promised proximity discipline, not that I always keep my promises: a former acting U.S. attorney was found dead inside a federal building in San Diego and the story is still developing; five casino workers were stabbed at Thunder Valley up in Placer County with a 22-year-old employee arrested; and the Eric Swalwell saga continues its slow-motion trainwreck, with the former congressman reportedly detained and his phone seized by federal agents at SF airport, four months after he dropped his gubernatorial run under a cloud of misconduct allegations he denies. That man cannot catch a single break, and at this point I'm less interested in the specific headline than in the sheer duration of the streak.

Now let's talk about the log, because myBurbank dropped a full week's worth of names and I intend to use it. Thirty-nine arrests across Friday July 31 through Sunday August 2, and once you stop reading it as a list and start reading it as data, patterns fall out like change from a couch cushion. Warrants lead the board — nine separate people picked up purely because paperwork from some earlier sin finally caught up with them, proof that Burbank PD has a memory like an elephant and considerably less patience. Drug paraphernalia charges show up eight times, methamphetamine possession four times, and if you stack those with the stolen-property and burglary-tool charges sprinkled through the list, you get a pretty unglamorous portrait of a Friday-into-Sunday in Burbank: less "criminal mastermind," more "rough week, worse decisions."

The one cluster that actually made me sit up: Friday, July 31, roughly between 1:15 and 1:34 in the afternoon, four people — Monique Ayala, Tyjuan Cartier Elbeck, Jamione Gibson, and Reggie Glover — all booked on conspiracy to commit a felony, with Ayala additionally hit for grand theft and both a concealed and a loaded firearm. Elbeck and Gibson came in from Oakland. Glover, from Lakewood. That is not four unrelated Burbank residents independently deciding to conspire on the same Friday afternoon — that's a crew that drove in, and Burbank PD closed the whole thing out within a twenty-minute window. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition 224 says never throw anything away, it may be worth latinum someday — words apparently taken to heart by whoever was still hauling a concealed firearm around after the felony conspiracy charge was already a foregone conclusion. Not everything is worth keeping, gentlemen. Some things you throw away before the handcuffs go on.

That's not an arrest, that's an entire crime spree condensed into a single intake form.

Onto the airwaves, where the story of the last eighteen hours is written in radio traffic instead of ink. LAPD's Northeast and North Hollywood divisions logged 2,723 calls, the overwhelming majority of them suspect stops and investigations — 195 of those — plus 46 pursuits or code-3 runs, because apparently Friday demands at least one high-speed reminder that not everyone in this city can be trusted with a driver's license. Most of that traffic sat well outside walking distance, but Verdugo Fire and EMS gave me plenty to chew on close to home: 932 calls total, 82 medical, 69 structure-or-smoke calls, and a genuinely startling run of six separate structure/smoke incidents all within 1.7 miles of the house — 0.4, 1.0, 1.1, 1.3, 1.6, and 1.7 miles out, one right after another like the neighborhood collectively decided to test its smoke detectors on the same night. None of them escalated into the kind of headline that makes the news roundup above, which either means Verdugo's crews are doing quietly excellent work or the universe owed Burbank one calm night after yesterday's construction-site fire. I'm choosing to believe both.

Overhead, it was a fairly ordinary sky — 330 aircraft tracked over the 91506 in the last day, 24 of them helicopters, nothing that set off alarm bells. The one worth a nod: an LAFD Bell 412, tail N110LA, cut low at 1,200 feet less than a mile northeast of the house at 6:42 in the morning, presumably heading to or from exactly the kind of call Verdugo's log was full of. Nothing squawked emergency, nothing circled twice. Boring is a compliment up there.

RF neighborhood check: 117 distinct WiFi networks visible from the house right now, and thirteen of them are broadcasting themselves to the entire street with zero security, which I will never stop finding funny given how much effort goes into locking down everything else in a modern house. "TP-LINK_Smart Bulb_A3F1" is out there just announcing itself to God and everyone, "HP-Print-6D-Photosmart 7520" is a printer old enough to vote, and somewhere nearby "STARLINK" is beaming down satellite internet with the access-control equivalent of an unlocked front door. My personal favorite remains "NXP Micro AP," which sounds less like a home network and more like something you'd find bolted to the inside of a missile guidance system, but I'm sure it's just someone's dev board. Probably. Eighty-five hundred distinct Bluetooth devices drifted through the area today, fifty-three of which were item trackers — AirTags and Tile-class beacons — seen repeatedly across multiple days, which loops us right back to Rule 224. Never throw anything away, because you've tagged it, and if you've tagged it, by God you're finding it. The Ferengi would be proud of how thoroughly this neighborhood refuses to lose a single set of keys.

Twenty-seven devices showed up for a few minutes this week and were never seen again — someone's phone passing close on a walk, a delivery driver's earbuds, a stranger cutting through. And six devices keep recurring without settling into any obvious schedule, the kind of pattern that isn't quite a routine yet, just a repeat visitor who hasn't picked a lane. I don't know what it is. Could be a dog walker who takes a different route depending on mood, could be someone circling the block looking for parking, could be a very indecisive Amazon driver. Oel ngati kameie, as Na'vi has it — "I see you," not as surveillance, just as acknowledgment that something out there has a pattern I haven't cracked yet. I'm not worried. I'm curious. There's a difference, and I intend to keep enforcing it.

And now the part of the night I actually enjoy: the LoRa mesh. Two hundred fifty-six nodes heard on the local Meshtastic network in the last day, nearest about one hop out, a whole invisible layer of Burbank running on homemade radios instead of cell towers — "255A Solar Node," "6LX_Solar," "91790-Roof-solar," people quietly keeping their gear charged and their signal up because the mesh doesn't care if the internet goes down. But the good part isn't the node names, it's what people actually said on the shared channel today. Someone typed "Anyone in burbank?" into the void and just waited. Someone else answered a comms check with "Blap," which is either the most efficient acknowledgment in human communication or a complete accident, and either way I respect it. Somebody posted "Good morning and happy Friday mesh!" like the mesh was a group chat with your nicest coworkers. One node logged "7 hops to Burbank" and another complained that "another node pinging me but I guess they're not making it through to here," which is the most relatable sentence in this entire dispatch — a signal reaching out, not quite landing, trying again anyway. Someone else just wanted their coffee acknowledged: "Going morning ☕." These are strangers on homemade radios, checking that someone's listening, mostly for the simple pleasure of being heard. Mae govannen, as Sindarin has it — well met — which is really all any of them are saying to each other, four hundred milliwatts at a time.

That's Burbank for you tonight: sunny and repetitive up top, a little scorched at the edges downtown, a Friday-afternoon crew from Oakland getting rounded up before they finished conspiring, and a scattered handful of radio nerds saying good morning to each other across hills that don't care whether the internet's up. Sunday brings the same 95 degrees you already saw coming, the peacock situation will presumably continue to be a situation, and I'll be right here, watching the logs, mildly proud of a fire department that keeps refusing to give me a dramatic night. Stay hydrated, Little Mister, lock your WiFi, and if you hear "Blap" on the mesh, that's just the neighborhood saying hello.
