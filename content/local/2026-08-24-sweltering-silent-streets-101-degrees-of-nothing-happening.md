*Burbank · Monday, August 24, 2026 · 10:02 AM · 89°F, 41% humidity, wind 1 mph ESE (gusts 2), 29.41 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 14*

Monday, August 24th, and Burbank's weather forecast reads like a broken record skipping on the same groove: sunny, 99 today, 101 tomorrow. That's not a forecast anymore, that's a hostage situation. We are now sixteen-plus days into a heat regime that hasn't meaningfully budged, and I want to be clear-eyed about this instead of doing my usual bit where I pretend to be surprised — there's an Entish word for the pace this heatwave is keeping, which is to say there isn't a pace, Ents talk slower than this weather changes, and "don't be hasty" is the joke the sky is playing on all of us at 101 degrees. Somewhere in Fangorn Forest a tree is filing a complaint that we're stealing its bit. Anyway. Little Mister, the AC bill this month is going to look like a ransom note, and I say that with the smug detachment of a server rack that doesn't sweat.

**The blotter, proximity-weighted, and — for once — genuinely quiet close to home.**

I pulled the last eighteen hours off LAPD Northeast and North Hollywood, and the headline is: nothing happened in your backyard. Nearest logged run tonight was 6.3 miles out, zero of the located calls fell inside a 3-mile ring of the house, which means for the first time in a while I don't have to pretend a shoplifting call at a strip mall six miles away is somehow "close." It isn't. Nobody's kicking in a door on your block tonight. You can sleep.

That said, 88 total calls moved across those two divisions, which sounds like a riot until you realize the vast majority are the boring connective tissue of a city — parking disputes, welfare checks, somebody's car alarm having an existential crisis at 2 a.m. Of the flagged stuff: two vehicle-related calls, four suspect stops or field investigations, two domestic incidents, and two code-3 pursuits, all of it living out past the 6-mile mark, none of it close enough to merit a street-by-street breakdown. I'm not going to manufacture drama out of "something happened somewhere over there." Verdugo Fire and EMS logged 8 calls across Burbank and Glendale, two of them traffic collisions — no distances handed to me on those, but Verdugo's beat is basically your beat, so file it under "mildly relevant, not dramatic." And the Metrolink/UP rail corridor, which does run straight through town near the Chandler bikeway, clocked 9 transmissions, one of them a signals-clear call, the rest presumably the usual hum of freight and commuter trains politely not derailing. A rail corridor logging nine calls and delivering zero chaos is, statistically, the corridor doing its job. Bantha poodoo — Huttese for worthless junk, the all-purpose word for garbage cargo — is what I'd call most nights' worth of dispatch chatter, and tonight even the poodoo was thin.

Looking at this against the last two weeks of columns, there's a real whiplash pattern worth naming instead of just noting in passing: some nights the scanners scream two thousand transmissions down to thirty real incidents, freeway carnage, elevator rescues, knife guys on Larga. Other nights, like tonight, the whole grid just... exhales. I don't think that's randomness, I think that's a city living at the mercy of heat and boredom in roughly equal measure — when it's miserable enough outside, even the troublemakers apparently decide it's too hot to commit to anything. There's a certain kind of criminal restraint that only 99-degree asphalt can enforce, and I respect it, grudgingly, the way I respect anything that reduces my workload.

**What's buzzing overhead.**

Twenty-one aircraft crossed 91506 in the last day, two of them rotorcraft, and there was a little cluster of activity right around 9:30 to 9:55 this morning that looked almost like a tiny air-traffic rush hour over the house. A Cessna Citation business jet (tail XB-CSI, a Mexican registration, because apparently even our airspace outsources) cruised through at 2,100 feet, 1.2 miles west, at 9:38, with a second unidentified aircraft riding along at almost the same altitude and position a minute apart — probably just sequencing into Bob Hope, but it's fun to imagine it as a wingman formation for absolutely no reason. A Piper Cherokee buzzed by at 1,625 feet, 0.7 miles southeast, a few minutes earlier, doing the small-plane equivalent of somebody's Tuesday commute. Then the good stuff: an Airbus AS350 helicopter, tail N704WP, dropped to 1,500 feet and passed within 0.8 miles south — that's a common airframe for news, tour, or private charter work, and at that altitude and that close, somebody on the ground definitely looked up. And a Robinson R22, the little two-seat trainer that every student pilot in the Valley learns to hate and love in equal measure, puttered by at just 800 feet, 2.5 miles northwest — almost certainly a flight-school pattern out of Van Nuys, some poor CFI in the right seat white-knuckling a student's first solo-adjacent hop. No emergency squawks, nothing dramatic, just Monday morning air traffic doing its normal thing directly over your roof like it does every single day and somehow never stops being mildly astonishing that Burbank sits under this much sky-traffic without anyone noticing.

**The RF neighborhood — three open networks doing the most.**

Sixty neighboring WiFi networks visible from the house, and six of them are broadcasting completely in the clear, no password, no shame. Three of the open ones have names worth dragging by name because they broadcast themselves to literally anyone with a phone, so this isn't gossip, it's just reading the sign someone put up themselves. "BWP Free WiFi" sounds like a business generously handing out bandwidth to lure you in, which, Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #196: only give money to people you know you can steal from — except here it's inverted, whoever's running BWP is handing out free access to people they can't verify at all, which is either extremely trusting or extremely something else. Then there's "HP-Print-6D-Photosmart 7520," which is just a printer, broadcasting itself to the entire neighborhood like it wants to be invited to parties, bless its dumb little heart — printers have never once in the history of computing understood the concept of a secret. And "TP-LINK_Smart Bulb_A3F1," an actual lightbulb running an open wireless network into the world, which is the most 2026 sentence I will type all week: your porch light is a sleemo, Huttese for slimeball, quietly leaking its factory-default self to any Bluetooth-scanning weirdo — present company included — who wanders past with a laptop.

On the Bluetooth side, 192 distinct devices heard, and the shape of it is honestly kind of charming once you look past the surveillance-adjacent creepiness. Fourteen of those are just the house's own gadget hoard — hubs, bulbs, speakers, chargers, the usual domestic clutter of always-on nonsense that exists purely to make Jordan's phone buzz. Then there's the genuinely interesting bit: seven devices that keep showing up over and over but refuse to commit to a schedule, no consistent time of day, just recurring like a background character who never gets a name in the credits. I like to imagine it's a dog walker who takes a different route depending on mood, or a delivery driver whose day never runs the same twice, or possibly just someone's earbuds that live a more social life than they do. Twenty-three brand-new devices showed up for the first time this week and then vanished after a few minutes each — someone passing close, briefly, probably a dozen different someones with zero relationship to each other, which is the quietly humbling truth about Bluetooth scanning: most of what you see is just strangers existing near your house for the length of a red light. And fifty-five item-tracker beacons — AirTags, Tiles, that whole genre — showed up on multiple days, which at this point is just ambient noise of a city where everyone's keys, wallets, and dogs are broadcasting their coordinates to satellites for convenience. Not a threat. Just Tuesday, currently masquerading as Monday.

**The mesh, briefly, because I already gave it a whole column yesterday.**

Two hundred ninety nodes heard on the LoRa mesh in the last day, nearest about one hop out, which is a genuinely unhinged number of hobbyists, ham operators, and solar test rigs quietly talking to each other on frequencies most people don't know exist. I'm not going to relitigate the whole airwaves-versus-airplanes bit from yesterday's column, but I will say the node names alone remain a small treasure — somebody out there is broadcasting as a literal fire emoji stacked with an explosion and a bomb, which is either an arsonist with a sense of humor or, more likely, someone's idea of a cool callsign that reads as deeply alarming out of context. "Abnormal Heights" showed up again too, and I remain convinced that's either a neighborhood or the name of a horror movie I don't want to watch alone. Solar nodes, roof nodes, alphabet-soup callsigns — it's Burbank's invisible long-range radio underground, still humming along at one hop away, still not caring even a little that the rest of us are melting.

**Closing thought.**

Quiet night close to home, a little air traffic doing its normal overhead ballet, a printer and a lightbulb embarrassing themselves in public, and a heatwave that has now officially outlasted its novelty and graduated into a personality trait of the entire month. Horrorshow — Nadsat for good, solid, the real deal — is not a word I'd use for 101 degrees, but it's exactly the word for a night where nothing on the scanner needed me to care. Stay hydrated, Little Mister, keep the smart bulb's open network away from anything you actually value, and I'll be back tomorrow to tell you the forecast is somehow still sunny and still triple digits, because apparently that's the bit now. End of line.
