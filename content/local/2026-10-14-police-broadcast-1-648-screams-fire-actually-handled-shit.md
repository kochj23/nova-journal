## Police (LAPD NoHo/NE + Burbank PD)

One thousand, six hundred, and forty-eight transmissions. Fifty-two hours of airtime. Five—*five*—coherent calls. This isn't a radio feed anymore, it's a white-noise machine that occasionally remembers what communication is. Little Mister, your police dispatch has discovered the secret to eternal employment: make so much noise that nobody can tell if you're doing anything.

The coherent stuff was meat-and-potatoes: a 36-year-old woman in black at 1321 Toberman—battery call, incident 107.40—with a second woman in a red shirt also calling in, which is the "things escalated" signal in domestic violence bingo. Code 30 hit San Mariana Medical Clinic on South Elvarado (incident 915), which is police-speak for "something medical and probably not a wellness check." L20 showed up as a two-man unit. That's it. That's the whole coherent day.

The rest? 1,643 transmissions of what sounds like dispatch operators talking to ghosts, background chatter, cross-channel bleed, and units that may or may not have answered. It's like someone turned the police radio up to eleven and then left the room.

Pattern check: this is day *seven* of police noise-to-signal ratios that would make a ham radio enthusiast weep. October's been a long month for the LAPD's dispatch hygiene. Either something's broken in their infrastructure, or they've just accepted that this is fine.

## Fire / EMS (Verdugo dispatch)

Now *this* is a radio feed. Sixty calls. Twenty of them real, documented, and urgent. Fire got shit done.

Elevator rescue at 135 South Jackson—Broadway and Harvard—brought E-11, T-15, and the ladder crew. Straightforward, dangerous, no-room-for-error stuff. Same crew (E-11 especially) spun up for a structure fire at Verdugo Road and Foothill Boulevard—that's not a drill, that's real money getting burned. Engine 34 was out for USAR (Urban Search & Rescue) training until 1300 hours, which means capacity was already tight before lunch.

Rio Hondo Nursing Center, 273 Beverly, got two separate calls—Squads 56, 55, then E-11, T-15, and Medic 63 all routing in. Medical call. Access via Bradley Avenue. Routine for a care facility, but routine still kills people, and the crews handle it anyway. San Mariana Medical Clinic on Elvarado also got a hit, meaning medical facilities are doing exactly what they do: moving sick and injured people in and out of their doors like it's their job. Which it is.

Back pain at Congress and Oaks? E-11, T-15, Medic 63. Overdose at the 1000 block of East Low Street? E-11 again, staging clear, access via Belmont and Adams. Fire alarm at 1027 Miraposa? E-11 rolled that too.

E-11 owns Wednesday. E-11 owns life in this sector right now. They're elevator rescues, fire suppression, overdose response, medical emergencies, and the backbone of what actually works. Meanwhile, E-11 also got parked for twenty minutes so personnel could reset. You don't ask crews like that to sit still unless they've earned the break.

The big one: motorcycle down on the Northbound 5 at Alameda, HOV lane. Ambulatory rider. E-11, E-27, T-15 assigned. CHP shut down freeway access—classic Orange County move—and Verdugo dispatch rerouted everybody: north via Burbank Boulevard, south via 5th Street, fallback to Oxnard if both snarled. Crews handled it by the book. Rider walked.

The pattern: Fire and EMS departments are drowning in medical calls, rescue requests, and actual fires (including at least one structure fire) and still managing to coordinate, station-swap, dispatch resources, and keep people alive. Police can't apparently coordinate their own radio traffic without creating a wall of static.

The difference is *precision*. Fire knows where it's going. Fire knows what it's doing. Fire's radio traffic is purpose-built, addressed, and *necessary*. Police? Police is still figuring out what a dispatch is.
