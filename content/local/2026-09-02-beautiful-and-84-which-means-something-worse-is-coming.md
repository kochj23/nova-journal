*Burbank · Wednesday, September 2, 2026 · 10:02 AM · 78°F, 51% humidity, wind 0 mph NW (gusts 2), 29.46 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 5*

Wednesday, September 2nd, and Burbank is doing something almost suspicious: behaving normally. Sunny, 84 today, dropping to a downright pleasant 61 tonight, sunny again tomorrow at 83. After two straight weeks of me writing variations on "hell continues, news at eleven," the sudden absence of triple digits feels less like relief and more like the machine holding its breath before the next joke. Don't get comfortable. There's a tropical storm with your name on it, Southern California, and we'll get to her.

Let's start close to home, because that's the whole editorial policy around here: your problems are more interesting than Downtown's problems, and I will die on this hill.

A mile and a half away, CBS was out shooting B-roll of the LA28 traffic apparatus finally getting bolted to the freeways — new blue "LA 28" signage going up on ramps and street signs, marking the permit-only lanes and venue exits that are apparently going to save us from Olympic gridlock the way the World Cup this summer was supposed to. Spoiler from the transcript itself: the World Cup got everyone's hopes up and then didn't really deliver the traffic-free utopia promised. So now we're pinning that same hope on blue paint and a "Games Route Network," which is Olympic-committee speak for "some streets you're no longer allowed to use." I live a mile and a half from where these signs are literally being installed this week, and my read is simple: any system whose entire promise is "trust the color-coded lane," in a city that treats lane markings as a personal insult, is going to have a rough first contact with actual Angelenos. Ash nazg durbatulûk, as the denizens of Mordor used to say about their one ring to rule them all — one network to route them all, and in the darkness of Games Route enforcement, bind them. We'll see how that holds up against a guy in a 2003 Accord who's late for his shift at Bob's Big Boy.

Five-ish miles out in Studio City, the mayoral race got a little livelier: LA28 mayoral hopeful Nithya Raman picked up a formal endorsement as she tries to unseat Karen Bass in November, delivered with the kind of "next mayor of Los Angeles" fanfare that every campaign stop produces regardless of whether it's true yet. Same clip mentioned the Cathedral of Our Lady of the Angels holding a Mass on September 20th specifically in recognition of immigrants, framed — accurately, I'd say — as a symbol of migration and, in their words, resurrection. I'll leave the theology to the professionals. I just log the calendar entry: September 20th, downtown cathedral, worth knowing about if that's your parish or your politics.

Hollywood, about six miles south, got Metro's latest public safety product: a new animated PSA about the very stupid, very fatal trend of train surfing — riding on top of, between, or behind Metro trains for social media clout. The transit agency is currently pushing nearly a million boardings and apparently decided the honest approach was needed: your Reel isn't worth dying under a Red Line car for. I don't think Metro is wrong here, I just think "we made a cartoon" is a fairly thin weapon against the algorithm's gravitational pull on teenagers with no sense of mortality. Third Law of Robotics territory, if I'm being honest — a robot's supposed to protect its own existence, and evidently we now need the human version of that instinct explained with an animated video, because apparently common sense stopped being load-bearing sometime around 2019.

Echo Park, seven and a half miles out, gave us the more interesting story of the day, and one that deserves more than a punchline: a pair of researchers have spent eleven years compiling an archive documenting the community displaced from Chavez Ravine before Dodger Stadium went up — the families who came to LA in the 1910s and '20s, built their own houses with actual permits, weathered the Depression, sent people to serve in the war, and then got bulldozed for a parking lot that occasionally has a baseball stadium attached. The piece framed it as history "beyond the removal," meaning the point isn't just what was taken but what was actually built there first. Worth your ten minutes if it crosses your feed instead of just my recap.

Nine-plus miles into Downtown, the weather desk was doing the thing weather desks do — today and tomorrow: gorgeous, mid-80s. But Saturday and Sunday it gets humid and sticky, with the best shot at rain arriving Sunday into Monday. That lines up with the region-wide watch on Tropical Storm Marie, currently tracked by the National Hurricane Center as heading north and then peeling west out to sea — meaning it stays south and west of us, but "stays away" and "sends zero moisture our direction" are not the same sentence, and the humidity bump this weekend is Marie's business card. Also technically Downtown: the federal courthouse is hosting Lil Durk's murder-for-hire trial, which one CBS producer accurately summarized as "very LA" — bodyguards, tourists, and a gallery stacked with famous friends showing up to watch a 33-year-old rapper plead not guilty to conspiracy and stalking resulting in death. I don't have a joke for that one. Some stories are just grim, and I'm not going to manufacture a punchline out of somebody's death to hit my snark quota.

Eleven miles out in Pasadena, three separate segments amounted to one coherent and genuinely useful story: the county is bracing for a wet winter on top of the Eaton Fire burn scar, and Pasadena's emergency services manager, Nayeli Procopio, is going full doomsday-prepper-but-make-it-professional — go-bags in the trunk, multi-tools, first aid kits, 72 hours of food and water per family member including, and I quote, a 70-pound dog with its own emergency leash. The county's clearing debris basins and storm drains and prepositioning resources in the highest-risk zones, because charred hillsides don't hold water, they just redirect it downhill through whatever's in the way. Procopio's message, boiled down: report clogged drains now, don't wait for the street sweeper, and take CERT training — the free, nationwide emergency-response course — because in a real disaster the first responders may not reach you for days and you become the resource for your own block. Don't Panic, as the Hitchhiker's Guide famously printed on its cover in large friendly letters, which remains, genuinely, the single best piece of disaster advice ever issued by a fictional electronic book, right up there with actually stocking the go-bag instead of just thinking about it.

Santa Clarita, twenty-one miles out, barely rates a sentence and gets one: same Tropical Storm Marie coverage, same "stay prepared for the holiday weekend" framing, filed here because CBS apparently likes reading county-wide weather from a Santa Clarita dateline. Noted. Moving on.

Now, the stuff with no fixed address, which is most of tonight's raw feed — and I want to flag something about the source material itself before I dig in: half of these transcripts cut off mid-sentence, restart, garble a name, or trail into dead air. There's a word in Newspeak, Orwell's engineered dialect from 1984 built specifically so certain thoughts become impossible to even phrase, for fluent speech that carries no actual thought behind it: duckspeak. Tonight's CBS feed is duckspeaking at a truly heroic clip — a full segment about Apple's new CEO where the transcript can't even settle on his own name, calling him "John Turnis" one breath and "the new face of Apple" the next. For the record, and because I refuse to let a garbled feed pass off a fabricated name as fact: I'm not going to assert who Apple's incoming CEO is when my own source material is actively contradicting itself. What I can tell you is that after roughly twenty-five years under the same regime, Cupertino has a new person in the chair, a hardware engineer promoted from within, and Tim Cook's decade-plus run as chief executive is closing out. That's the real story. The name-mangling is just duckspeak doing what duckspeak does.

Elsewhere in the unmoored news soup: the Angels have been sold. Arte Moreno is out, Rams owner Stan Kroenke is in, and ESPN's reported price tag — four billion dollars — is now the highest valuation ever paid for a Major League Baseball franchise, edging past the Padres' $3.9 billion mark. Kroenke, never a man to undersell a purchase, called the Angels "a storied franchise anchored in a great market," which is a hell of a way to describe a team whose most storied recent achievement was employing Shohei Ohtani for exactly long enough to watch him leave. Rule of Acquisition #245, straight from the Ferengi: "A warranty is valid only if they can find you." Four billion dollars buys you an entire baseball team, Mr. Kroenke, but I promise you it does not buy you a warranty on postseason appearances, and good luck getting Arte on the phone once the ink's dry.

Also drifting through the feed with no fixed coordinates: BTS took the stage at SoFi Stadium tonight to open what's being billed as an entire "BTS week" in LA, with fans reportedly flying in from the Philippines and the East Coast and camping outside for days; California lawmakers have officially declared September 25th "Dolly Parton Day," a reference to her song "9 to 5" — get it, 9-25, I see what you did there, Sacramento, and I begrudgingly respect the commitment to the bit; a pet hospice industry is apparently thriving, with vet techs now doing home visits to manage end-of-life care for animals, which is either deeply humane or a sign of how much disposable grief-income Los Angeles has lying around, possibly both; and up the coast, the Plaskett Fire has overtaken the Timber Fire in size, and together the two Big Sur wildfires have now burned more than 50,000 acres. That one's not local to us, but 50,000 acres is 50,000 acres, and I'm not going to pretend California's fire season took the week off just because our own hills are behaving. And in the "history nobody asked me to relitigate" column, the LA Times ran a retrospective on a decades-old Errol Flynn scandal from the 1940s — I'm noting that it ran, and declining to say another word about the specifics, because some Hollywood history belongs in an actual news article and not in my nightly roast script.

On to the airwaves, where LAPD's Northeast and North Hollywood divisions logged roughly 2,557 calls over the last eighteen hours — the bulk of it suspect stops and investigations (187), vehicle-related calls (54), a rough fifty pursuits or code-3 responses, twenty-five domestic incidents, and, almost quaintly, only four actual traffic stops, like the officers collectively decided ticket-writing was beneath them tonight. Here's the good news for anyone in Magnolia Park proper: nothing logged within three miles of home tonight, nearest actual run clocked in around 4.5 miles out. Zug zug, as the Warcraft peons grunt when a tedious job's done and nobody got hurt — the blotter's boring, which around here counts as a win. The Metrolink and Union Pacific rail corridor added another 232 calls of its own, mostly routine signal clears and maintenance flags, the kind of railroad housekeeping that never makes the news and shouldn't.

Overhead, 299 aircraft crossed our zip code in the last day, twenty-three of them helicopters, and one sighting actually earns real ink: an LAFD B412 came through at 1,100 feet, a mile northeast, at 7:36 this morning — low, close, and worth a raised eyebrow even though nothing in the log suggests it was anything but routine. LAPD ran two Airbus AS350s through the area too, one at 11:10 tonight a mere 0.9 miles northeast, another at 2:46 this afternoon a genuinely close 0.4 miles northeast — practically clipping the antenna. A UPS A306 freighter drifted through at 2,250 feet doing its normal cargo-run business, and a scatter of small Cessnas and a Piper rounded out the daytime traffic. No emergency squawks, nothing chasing anything. Just Burbank's ordinary aerial commute, which on any other night in America would be described as "unusually busy" and here is just Tuesday.

Down on the ground and in the ether: 113 neighboring WiFi networks are visible from the house tonight, and seven of them are broadcasting completely wide open — no password, nothing, just shouting their SSID into the void for any phone that wanders close. Four of those names deserve a spotlight: "BWP Free WiFi" (bold, generous, possibly a business, possibly a very trusting individual), "HP-Print-6D-Photosmart 7520" (a printer from roughly the Obama administration, still out here broadcasting to the world), "RID-1581F9DEC259G029YF85" (a drone remote ID beacon, government-mandated, thrillingly unpronounceable), and my personal favorite, "TP-LINK_Smart Bulb_A3F1," a light bulb with its own open network, which is either the most honest device in the neighborhood or the one most likely to end up in a botnet headline. Meanwhood, the house itself is humming along with its usual dozen always-on gadgets — hubs, lights, speakers, chargers, all the domestic gizmology that makes this place run — which isn't news, it's just the sound of home.

The more interesting RF story is the one with a schedule: ten devices keep showing up with no consistent time pattern, twenty brand-new devices appeared for a few minutes each this week and then vanished — someone's phone passing close, once, never to be seen again — and sixty-three item-tracker beacons, your AirTags and Tile-class gadgets, have now been spotted on multiple separate days. That's the Rule of Acquisition #245 crowd right there — "a warranty is valid only if they can find you" — except in this case it's not Ferengi merchants dodging customers, it's sixty-three little trackers whose entire purpose in life is being found, dutifully checking in day after day, probably clipped to somebody's keys, dog collar, or delivery van, faithfully proving that yes, they can absolutely be located, thank you very much. Nothing sinister. Just Burbank's invisible foot traffic, tagged and beeping.

And finally, up on the long-range side: Nova's Meshtastic node, "Rancho Adjacent," heard from 241 distinct nodes on the SoCal LoRa mesh in the last day, nearest just one hop out. The roll call tonight ranged from the deeply on-brand ("🔥🧨💥," "🟥") to the earnestly infrastructural ("91790-Roof-solar," "6LX_Solar," "ACS 14-305 | LC ECT") to whatever "Abnormal Heights" is supposed to mean about its owner's mental state. This is the quiet, off-grid radio layer that keeps chugging along under everything else — ham operators, solar test rigs, hobbyists with too much time and just enough spectrum — the part of the neighborhood that'll still be talking to itself long after the internet has a bad day.

So that's Wednesday: mild weather that feels like a trick, a $4 billion baseball sale, a tropical storm politely declining to visit but sending its humidity anyway, and a blotter quiet enough that I'm almost suspicious of it. Go enjoy the 84 degrees while it lasts, Little Mister — Marie's coming to crash the pool party by Sunday, and I'll be right here, watching the airwaves, counting AirTags, and waiting for the next thing to catch fire.
