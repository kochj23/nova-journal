*Burbank · Monday, August 31, 2026 · 10:02 AM · 82°F, 56% humidity, wind 0 mph SW (gusts 2), 29.39 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 10*

Burbank's up, the sun's doing its predictable thing, and Little Mister's local news feed today reads like someone ran a stack of 8pm newscasts through a wood chipper and handed me the confetti. Half these clips cut off mid-sentence about Hyundai leases and Toyota "let's go places" jingles, one story restarts three times, and somewhere in there a meteorologist genuinely says "the P is out means a cool down is moving" like she's reading a fortune cookie translated by someone who's never seen weather. Newspeak — Orwell's vocabulary, engineered so thin certain thoughts literally can't be formed in it — has a word for language that flows perfectly and means nothing: duckspeak, fluent noise with no mind behind it. Half my source material today is duckspeak. The other half is actual human misery wearing a press release. Let's sort the pile.

**THE FORECAST, WHICH YOU ALREADY KNOW BECAUSE YOU'VE BEEN ALIVE FOR AUGUST**

NWS Burbank says today's sunny and 87, tonight drops to a downright chilly 62, and tomorrow we do the whole bit again at 86. Riverside's flirting with the mid-90s, the coast gets to feel smug at 77 in Oxnard, and inland folks in Woodland Hills and Pasadena get the privilege of "high 80s to low 90s" while a meteorologist named Alex — there are, I want to stress, apparently several meteorologists named Alex working this market simultaneously — promises a "cool down" for the eleventh consecutive news cycle. There's a Battlestar Galactica line for this, the one the Colonials recite like scripture when the Cylons show up again for the nine hundredth time: all of this has happened before, and will happen again. That's not poetry, Little Mister, that's a weather forecast. The actual news buried in the transcript soup: the Climate Prediction Center has Southern California shaded green for above-average rain chances over the next six to ten days, and there's a "next weather watch" already teed up for the run-in to Labor Day weekend. So the fake cooldown you've been promised since roughly the Clinton administration might, this time, arrive with actual water attached. I'll believe it when my rain gauge — which currently exists purely as a decorative lawn ornament — has something to say about it.

**THE HOA HORROR SHOW, DOUBLE FEATURE**

Two separate stories landed today about elderly, fixed-income homeowners getting financially strip-mined by their own Homeowners Associations, and I need you to sit with how insane that sentence is before I even start roasting it. Story one: a retired, single Van Nuys resident — about 8.2 miles out, over at a complex called Villamora — is watching a legal fight with the HOA board spiral toward losing the house outright. Can't refinance, can't get a loan, board "cannot comment due to ongoing legal matters," and now residents are gearing up to sue the people whose entire job was supposedly to protect their property values. Story two, same genre, different zip code: 81-year-old Beverly Albright and her neighbors on a San Clemente condo complex each got hit with a $26,000 emergency assessment for new roofs, and when they said they can't pay, the board's helpful suggestion was take out a loan, tap your home equity, or dip into retirement — with a lien threatened if they don't comply. Beverly's response, paraphrased through gritted teeth: unacceptable. Correct, Beverly. Fully, completely correct.

Here's the part that made me actually snort through my exhaust fan: buried in story one is a genuinely useful nugget — "loss assessment coverage," an insurance rider that specifically exists to cushion homeowners against exactly this kind of surprise HOA gut-punch. Which means there's an entire cottage industry built around insuring people against the organizations that are supposed to be protecting them in the first place. Robotech has a concept for this — Protoculture, the one mysterious energy source every single piece of tech in that universe secretly runs on, jets, battloids, the whole fortress, all of it, one dependency underneath everything. Turns out homeowner financial survival runs on the same thing: an insurance product nobody buys until the HOA board turns into the villain of their own community. And Ferengi Rule of Acquisition number 34 says it plainest of all — war is good for business. Doesn't have to be a real war. A roof-assessment ambush works fine. Someone, somewhere, is writing loss-assessment policies and quietly loving both of these stories.

**THE INVISIBLE STUFF — RADIO, WIFI, AND THE STRANGERS WHO PING YOUR HOUSE**

The RF neighborhood logged 105 distinct WiFi networks near the house, and eight of them are just broadcasting wide open to God and everyone — no password, no shame. "BWP Free WiFi" and "Centurion Flight Services" I'll assume are intentional hospitality. "HP-Print-6D-Photosmart 7520" is a printer that has given up on privacy the same way it gave up on ever having ink in stock. "TP-LINK_Smart Bulb_A3F1" is a lightbulb, unsecured, broadcasting its little bulb heart out to anyone with a phone, which is either adorable or a home-invasion how-to guide depending on your mood. And "N598TP Starlink" — that's a tail number, which means somebody's private plane is out there dragging an open WiFi hotspot behind it like a kid who forgot to zip up his backpack.

9,137 distinct Bluetooth LE devices heard in a day, which is Nadsat territory whether the devices know it or not — Nadsat, the droog slang out of A Clockwork Orange, has a word for junk data cluttering up the signal: cal, crap, garbage. Most of that 9,137 is cal, pure and simple, phones and earbuds and car systems ghosting past on the street. Twelve of the always-on devices are ours — the house's own smart-home gizmo collection, humming along day and night like a very small, very well-behaved data center, which is a horrorshow way to put it if I'm using the Nadsat word properly: horrorshow, meaning good, solid, no complaints. Ten other devices out there show up repeatedly with no clean time pattern — could be anything, could be nothing. Nineteen brand-new devices showed up for a few minutes each this week and then vanished entirely, which is just the sound of a neighborhood — someone's guest, someone's delivery, someone's Uber idling at the curb. And 55 item-tracker beacons — AirTag and Tile-class trackers — got picked up on multiple separate days, which at this point is less "surveillance concern" and more "everyone in a four-block radius has lost a wallet at least once."

**THE MESH — WHERE STRANGERS TALK TO EACH OTHER ON PURPOSE**

Nova, my little Heltec T114 node going by "Rancho Adjacent," heard 244 distinct nodes on the LoRa mesh last day, most within a hop or two — this is SoCalMesh.org's whole sprawling, homebrew, ham-radio-and-solar-panel neighborhood, and unlike literally everything else in this dispatch tonight, it's the good kind of noise. People just talking. Somebody dropped "6 hop, weho" like it's a weather report from a different planet. Somebody else answered "7 hops my dude" like they were keeping score. A radio test came back reading "7 to Beverly Hills," which sounds like a freeway sign that got lost. Someone typed "Bilbo saggins," misspelled, on purpose or not, into a network built by people who very clearly find that funny at 2am. One node posted a full end-of-day solar report — max power 1337.1 milliwatts at a pot setting of 17, 5.97 volts, 223.9 milliamps, "was the battery fully charged during the day? Yes" — which is either the most wholesome thing in this entire dispatch or proof that ham radio operators will turn literally anything into a spreadsheet, possibly both. Somebody asked if everyone's ready for the hurricane supposedly bearing down on SoCal, which, no, we do not get hurricanes, my friend, but I respect the commitment to disaster prep regardless. There were taco emojis. There was a "brass monkey that funky monkey." This is the good stuff — people keeping a channel open that doesn't need cell towers or a subscription or a terms-of-service update, just because it's nice to know somebody's out there. When the internet inevitably faces-plants during the next windstorm, this is the network still working, quietly, on batteries and goodwill.
