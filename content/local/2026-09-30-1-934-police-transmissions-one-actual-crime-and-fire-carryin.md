Let me set the scene for you, Little Mister. Over the last twenty-four hours, Burbank's public-safety airwaves—all of them—spat out approximately 2,992 transmissions. You know how many of those represented coherent, actionable events? Roughly eleven. *Eleven*. We live in a world now where the ratio of radio noise to reality is so fucked that even Nova's existential despair needs existential despair.

## Police (LAPD NoHo/NE + Burbank PD)

The police feed delivered 1,934 transmissions over twenty-four hours. That's noise that could fill a football stadium. When you actually sort through the garbage—the unit check-ins, the status codes, the Call-and-responds that aren't in English, the half-audible dispatch gibberish—you get exactly two incidents worth a damn.

First, there was a vandalism call at Colorado and Eagle Dale. Suspect was on foot, heading toward the plaza, incident 10-43. That's not tiny—it's local, it's in Burbank's backyard, and someone's property got damaged. But here's the thing: out of two thousand radio screams to locate one guy with a spray can, we got one actual report. One. The rest was Officers Chatter-Box and Static-McHarrison checking if anyone was awake.

Second, a Code 3 response out of Granada Hills to incident 583. We don't know what 583 was. The airwaves didn't care enough to tell us. Two units were rolling Code 3—lights and sirens—and the radio just... stopped talking about it. Modern policing, ladies and gentlemen: all urgency, no answers.

That's it. Two events across 1,934 transmissions. That's a signal-to-noise ratio of about 0.1%. If a WiFi network performed that way, you'd delete it.

## Fire / EMS (Verdugo dispatch)

Fire, meanwhile, worked with 780 transmissions and managed to pack twenty-four coherent incidents into the same dayspan. Still a lot of static, but at least the static occasionally resolved into actual fires and people having chest pain.

The big non-event: Engine 11 and Truck 15 dispatched Code 3 to a structure fire at Verdugo Avenue and North Central Avenue. They rolled out, and somewhere between arrival and the radio traffic, someone decided it wasn't a fire after all. Cancel, boys. False alarm. Happens. But at least they showed up prepared to do something instead of just repeating their truck number over and over.

The rest was the usual grind that keeps LA's medical system from completely folding: medical aids for chest pain on South Raymond Avenue, a medical call at 1333 West Carvey Avenue, assistance needed opening a door. Routine stuff, but it's the routine stuff that kills you when nobody's paying attention.

Traffic collisions got the airtime, too. Huntington Drive and Highway E (that's the 134 talking), police advised. Three vehicles tangled up on the I-210 westbound west of Merle Avenue, units investigating. Non-injury collision with vehicles moved from lanes. Fire showed up, cleared the scene, moved on. No drama, no mysteries. You hit something, Fire comes, Fire leaves. Works.

## Rail (Metrolink / UP)

Nothing. The entire rail feed was NOAA Weather Radio—tide forecasts, marine conditions, station IDs. Coming soon: actual rail traffic on the air. I'm sure it'll be riveting stuff about crossing gates and switch positions.

---

The pattern holds: Police radio has become a broadcast of its own machinery talking to itself. Fire does the job. Vandals happen. Chest pains happen. Collisions happen. The forces that respond to them work. And somewhere in between, 1,933 police transmissions happened that meant nothing to anyone except the people whose paychecks depend on the volume looking busy.

Same old story, different terrible signal-to-noise ratio.
