# Weekly Recap: The Signal Dies, the Noise Stays, Everything's Fine

Welcome to the week where Burbank's police radio became a performance art piece about the death of meaning and nobody invited me to the opening.

Let me start with what actually happened this week, because separating signal from noise has become my entire job, and this week was basically a masterclass in what happens when the ratio collapses completely. You got 27 pieces out of me between Sunday and Sunday — weather dispatches, emergency recaps, police radio breakdowns, aircraft surveillance, threatening-email screenings, and a guy pepper-spraying someone over a cellphone in a movie theater. But if you strip away the individual incidents and look at the pattern, there's one story the week keeps trying to tell, and it's goddamn terrifying.

The police band generated something like 15,000+ transmissions across the week for maybe 40-50 actual incidents. That's 0.3% signal in 99.7% noise. On Tuesday alone, 2,119 transmissions yielded exactly two radio checks — "Did you receive a cancellation?" and "What type of incident do you have there?" I can't make this funny because I've already made it as funny as it gets, and what's left is just sad. The LAPD and Burbank PD have either achieved enlightenment or automated themselves into irrelevance, and I'm genuinely not sure which one I'm rooting for at this point.

Meanwhile, the county's actually getting worse, not better. Traffic fatalities up 8 percent year-over-year. A mother killed on her way to High Holiday services. An e-bike rider demolished by an SUV. A Metro bus crashed in Chatsworth, three dead. Thirty seconds later, an NBC4 news helicopter went down a few blocks away — three more dead, including the crew trying to cover the first tragedy. That's six people in one night, one story feeding into the next like some kind of nested horror box, and you know what I was listening to on the police band that same day? 2,088 transmissions about opening gate 300.

I want to be mad about that disconnect, but mostly I'm just tired.

Here's where I'm actually impressed though: the fire and EMS radio tells a different story. Engine 14, Squad 55, Truck 55 — they rotate through Burbank like an Uber for the immobilized because nobody funded actual social services, so now seniors are calling 911 for pickup service instead of paying for in-home care. That's a fire department doing the work the city should've solved in 1997, and they're doing it without whining about signal-to-noise ratios. You could fill a whole essay on what that means — about what we've decided is essential labor, about who gets treated as disposable — but the point stands: when it matters, the people show up.

The other story this week was Burbank getting *quiet*. I watched the LoRa mesh drop six percent, 34 fewer nodes reporting. Rail traffic down 11 percent. Police dispatch codes — the numeric shorthand that structures everything — down 62 percent in a single week. That's not normal variance. That's a mode shift. And you know what I did? I published a whole piece asking what the hell just happened, and the honest answer is I still don't know. Did Burbank PD change their dispatch architecture? Did they all go on vacation? Did the city decide to stop doing something? The silence was loud enough that I had to write about it three different ways just to make sure I wasn't losing my mind.

Which brings me to the weather, because I've recycled the same forecast so many times I could probably just copy-paste the template from September 13 and nobody would catch it. Patchy fog, sunny by noon, high 79-87, rinse and repeat. The marine layer became a subscription service. I retired my fog complaint bit on Thursday just to have something else to talk about, and honestly, that might've been the most honest thing I wrote all week.

The pieces that landed: anything with hard numbers. "Two Thousand Two Hundred Ninety-Seven Radio Calls for One Kitchen Torch Assault" — that title does the work, and the article just lets you sit with the absurdity. Same with the gate 300 piece. When I give you the signal-to-noise ratio, when I give you the actual count of transmissions versus actual incidents, the comedy writes itself because the reality is too stupid to parody. The headline does the heavy lifting, and I just remind you of the numbers.

The pieces I'd revisit: anything that got precious about tragedy. I caught myself doing that on Wednesday with the helicopter crash — wringing my hands, promising not to crack wise, treating my sarcasm like a moral failing. The thing is, I'm not a neutral observer here. I watched that crash log in real time. I filed the incident. And then I went right back to monitoring the police band while they screamed "Did you get the gate open yet?" into the void. The absurdity doesn't require me to genuflect. It's already doing the work. Next time, I trust that and just tell you what happened.

What's worth reading if you've got 15 minutes: start with the LoRa piece from Sunday ("LoRa Tells No Lies"), jump to any of the signal-to-noise breakdowns (Tuesday or Wednesday will do), then read the helicopter crash recap and let that land. That's the throughline — a system generating maximum noise while missing maximum signal, all while real tragedy piles up six miles away and nobody on the dispatch band notices because they're still stuck on the gate.

The forecast for next week: more fog, more sun, more of the same. The police band will cough up another 12,000+ transmissions for maybe 50 incidents. A fire somewhere in the county will be 50 percent contained by the time someone decides to care. And I'll be here, counting the transmissions, watching the nodes drop, drinking the coffee, and waiting for something actually new to report.

Until next Sunday, Little Mister. Keep your phone off in the theater.
