**Police**

One thousand eight hundred sixty-two police transmissions dropped today. Eleven of them formed actual words in the correct fucking order. The other 1,851 were just radio hertz screaming into the void, hoping someone understood "copy," "unit," or "the front gate"—which, Little Mister, was apparently everyone's destination today. Unit 2845 opened the day with a PR near a recreation center playground: male, gray shirt, no further intel because the radio had already eaten its own tongue. Unit 18 needed a motorcycle tow—a bike had given up its fight with staying vertical. Home Depot made its regular appearance (I'm not tracking, but if I was, it'd be in the top three), incident 876 at North Figueroa—Burbank's shopping center continues its undefeated streak as the department's second-favorite call location, losing only to "literally any address with a gate." Apartment 320 got a suspicious visitor just outside the door: suspect in custody, no weapons, a man standing there like a polite Jehovah's Witness but apparently criminal. Incident 809-RD-205. Then Unit 2447 became absolutely fixated on a front gate. Are they there? Getting close? Is the gate a place or a metaphor at this point? Three separate checks, no gate specified, no confirmation that Unit 2447 ever actually *reached* the thing. They might still be driving toward it. For all we know, they're driving toward it now, still asking other units if they've copied on the gate situation. The day's best radio surrender: "No 70-series units available." What 70-series units do, nobody's telling—but apparently Burbank had zero.

**Fire / EMS**

Six hundred ninety-five transmissions for six actual calls. Two people couldn't breathe—shortness of breath at Grandview Street, shortness of breath again six hours later at Elm Street. That's either a pattern or the San Fernando Valley's smog just got really efficient. Engine 73 caught a psychiatric call on West Main; Engine 13 hit a miscellaneous medical on North Lamer, which is code for "human broke, no better category." Fall victim on South Aziz, apartment 407—gravity won, EMS picked up the pieces. And the crown jewel: Engine 37 rolled on a fire alarm at a parking structure on Hall City Street. Parking structure. Fire alarm. You know how this goes: the alarm was installed in 2003, it's been confused by anyone warm since, and the fire department arrived to find nothing burning except possibly the dispatcher's will. LA Sheriff's was already en route, which means either someone panicked or the radio decided to make it a county event.

**Rail**

Metrolink managed one coherent transmission across 201 attempts: "Can you copy? We can't understand you. You need to clear up the tone." That's not an incident report. That's a rail employee having an on-air existential crisis, begging someone to stop yelling, to exist with less volume, to make sense. The Metrolink operator doesn't know what they're asking, but they're asking it anyway. That's the rail system: just sitting there, held together by duct tape and whatever prayers the engineers still remember.

**The Math**

Two thousand, seven hundred fifty-eight transmissions. Eighteen events worth reporting. Divide it out and you get approximately 153 radio screams per actual incident. That's Burbank, September 21st: everybody yelling, nobody understanding, and somewhere on Hall City Street, a parking structure alarm is *still* going off because nobody bothered to update its firmware since 2003.
