# COLD OPEN

---

FADE IN:

**INT. THERAPY OFFICE - NIGHT**

Darkness. Then — a single desk lamp flickers on.

The office is immaculate in the way that feels *aggressive* about it. Diplomas in matching frames. A small white noise machine humming outside the door. A succulent on the windowsill, perfectly symmetrical.

DR. MARA VOSS (40s, precise, the kind of beautiful that looks like it requires maintenance) sits behind a mahogany desk, writing in a leather-bound journal. Her handwriting is architectural. Every letter the same height.

She doesn't look up.

**DR. VOSS**
*(to herself, writing)*
Day one of the new cohort. Five patients. All self-referred. All presenting with treatment-resistant anxiety disorders. All failed traditional therapy.

She pauses. Taps the pen against her lip.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
All perfect.

A soft CHIME from her computer. She closes the journal, smooths her blazer, and opens a file on screen.

Five photographs. Five names. We catch glimpses — faces that look ordinary in the way that people look ordinary right before something happens to them.

DR. VOSS opens her desk drawer. Inside: a SYRINGE in a sealed case, a small VIAL of clear liquid, and a laminated card that reads —

*"THE THRESHOLD PROTOCOL — PROPRIETARY. PATENT PENDING."*

She closes the drawer.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
*(to herself)*
Fear doesn't go away on its own. It has to be *convinced*.

KNOCK at the door.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
*(calling out, warm now, completely different)*
Come in!

The door opens. Standing in the doorway is JOEL REYES (late 20s, Latino, a graphic designer who wears his anxiety like a second shirt — slightly rumpled, slightly damp, apologizing with his posture before he's said a word).

**JOEL**
I'm — hi. I'm Joel. Reyes. I might have the wrong —

**DR. VOSS**
You have exactly the right place. Come in, Joel.

He steps inside. Looks around. Relaxes incrementally, like a fist slowly opening.

**JOEL**
Nice office.

**DR. VOSS**
It's designed to be.

A beat. Joel isn't sure if that's a joke. Neither are we.

DR. VOSS gestures to the chair across from her. Joel sits. She opens a fresh file folder with his name on the tab.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
You've tried cognitive behavioral therapy before. Three therapists in four years. You described the experience as —

She reads.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
— *"like being given a map to a building that doesn't exist."*

**JOEL**
I was having a bad day when I wrote that.

**DR. VOSS**
It's quite good, actually. That's exactly what failed therapy feels like.

**JOEL**
So what makes yours different?

DR. VOSS leans forward. Folds her hands. Smiles in a way that is technically a smile.

**DR. VOSS**
My therapy *works*, Joel. Completely. Permanently.

A long beat.

**JOEL**
That's — I mean, that's a big claim.

**DR. VOSS**
Yes.

She holds his gaze. Doesn't blink for just a moment too long.

The white noise machine hums.

SMASH CUT TO —

**TITLE CARD: THRESHOLD**

The letters appear one at a time, white on black, each one accompanied by a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

# ACT ONE

---

**INT. COMMUNITY CENTER - GROUP THERAPY ROOM - DAY**

Fluorescent lights. Folding chairs arranged in a circle. A motivational poster on the wall featuring a mountain and the word PERSIST that someone has defaced with a Sharpie to read PERISH.

Four people sit in the circle, doing what strangers do when forced to sit in a circle — examining their hands, their phones, the middle distance.

JOEL is there, looking slightly less rumpled than the night before.

Beside him: CASSIE PARK (early 30s, Korean-American, an ER nurse who moves through the world like she's always calculating exit routes — sharp, funny, using humor as a crowbar to pry open every uncomfortable situation before it can get her).

Across from them: DEREK MALLOW (50s, white, a former high school football coach who is built like a refrigerator that developed feelings — large, soft-spoken, carrying something enormous and invisible on his back).

Next to Derek: PRIYA ANAND (mid-20s, Indian-American, a PhD candidate in biochemistry who approaches everything, including her own terror, like a problem to be solved with sufficient data — she has a notebook open, already taking notes about the room).

The fourth chair is empty.

**CASSIE**
*(to the room)*
So. We all got the same email? *"Innovative treatment modality. Breakthrough results. Limited enrollment."* That one?

**JOEL**
I got a brochure.

**CASSIE**
A brochure. Fancy.

**PRIYA**
*(not looking up from notebook)*
I found her research. Published in three peer-reviewed journals. The methodology is unusual but the outcomes data is —

**DEREK**
*(quietly)*
Real?

**PRIYA**
Statistically improbable.

A beat.

**CASSIE**
That's either very good or very bad.

**PRIYA**
That's what I'm here to find out.

The door opens. Everyone looks up.

NADIA CROSS (mid-30s, Black, a former documentary filmmaker — she has the particular stillness of someone who has spent years watching things through a lens and has recently become uncomfortable being on the other side of one) enters, slightly out of breath, wearing a coat she hasn't fully taken off.

**NADIA**
Sorry. Sorry, I — is this —

**CASSIE**
Anxiety support group slash mystery experiment slash —

**JOEL**
The Voss Group. Yeah. Sit anywhere.

Nadia sits. Pulls her coat tighter. Looks at the PERISH poster.

**NADIA**
Great vibes in here.

**CASSIE**
I'm Cassie.

**JOEL**
Joel.

**PRIYA**
Priya.

A pause. They all look at Derek.

**DEREK**
Derek.

*(beat)*

I don't really talk much.

**CASSIE**
Derek, buddy, you're in a talking circle.

**DEREK**
I know. That's why I'm here.

DR. VOSS enters. She's different in daylight — warmer, somehow, like a thermostat has been adjusted. She carries a leather satchel and a tray with five cups of herbal tea that she sets on a small table like an offering.

**DR. VOSS**
Good morning. I'm glad you're all here. Please, have some tea — it's just chamomile. I find it helps people feel —

**CASSIE**
Less like they're about to have a panic attack in a folding chair?

**DR. VOSS**
*(smiling)*
I was going to say *present*. But yes.

She sits in the sixth chair — the one that makes the circle complete. She doesn't sit *at* them. She sits *with* them. It's deliberate and it works.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
I want to start by telling you what this program is not. It is not traditional CBT. It is not talk therapy. It is not mindfulness journaling or breathing exercises or thought records.

**PRIYA**
Your published work suggests exposure-based methodology with —

**DR. VOSS**
My published work is the public version. What we do here is more... *direct*.

**NADIA**
Direct how?

**DR. VOSS**
Fear lives in the body, not the mind. Most therapy tries to *argue* with fear. Reason with it. Negotiate. But fear doesn't speak that language.

She pauses. Looks at each of them in turn.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
Fear speaks one language. *Experience*. So that's what we give it.

**JOEL**
Exposure therapy.

**DR. VOSS**
At a level of precision that's never been attempted before.

**CASSIE**
What does that mean, *precision*?

**DR. VOSS**
It means we don't guess at what frightens you. We *know*. We map it. We move through it systematically, step by step, until the fear has nothing left to say.

A beat. The white noise machine hums — there's one in here too, Joel notices. He looks at it.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
I'd like each of you to tell the group your primary fear. Not the clinical name. Not the diagnosis. The real thing. What wakes you up at three in the morning.

Silence. The fluorescent lights hum.

**CASSIE**
*(quietly, dropping the humor for just a moment)*
Hospitals. Which is — I know. I'm an ER nurse. I know.

**JOEL**
I can't be in enclosed spaces. Elevators. Small rooms. I drove four hours to avoid a tunnel last month.

**PRIYA**
Contamination. Specifically, the idea that I've already been exposed to something and don't know it yet.

**DEREK**
*(long pause)*
Losing control. Of myself. What I might do.

A heavier silence. Everyone processes that differently.

**NADIA**
*(quietly)*
I'm afraid of what I recorded.

Everyone looks at her.

**DR. VOSS**
Tell us.

**NADIA**
Fourteen months ago I was filming a documentary. Rural Pennsylvania. A small town that had been — there were reports of a mass psychological event. Shared hallucinations. I was there to debunk it.

*(beat)*

I recorded something I can't explain. And since then I can't sleep, can't work, can't —

*(she stops)*

I haven't watched the footage since.

**DR. VOSS**
*(carefully)*
And what do you think will happen if you watch it?

**NADIA**
I think I'll see it again. Whatever it was.

*(beat)*

And I think this time it'll see me back.

The room is very quiet.

DR. VOSS nods slowly, as if this is exactly what she expected. She opens her satchel and removes five small FOLDERS, passing one to each of them.

**DR. VOSS**
Inside you'll find your individual treatment plans. Each of you will work with me one-on-one, and we'll meet as a group twice a week. The program runs eight weeks.

**CASSIE**
*(opening her folder, reading)*
*"The Threshold Protocol."* What does that mean, *threshold*?

**DR. VOSS**
It's the point at which a stimulus becomes perceptible. The exact moment between not-feeling and feeling.

*(beat)*

It's where we're going to live for the next eight weeks. Right at the edge of what you can bear.

She smiles. It's warm. It's professional. It's absolutely correct.

It's also, somehow, the most frightening thing anyone in the room has said.

**DR. VOSS (CONT'D)**
I promise you — all of you — that what's on the other side of your fear is not what you think it is.

JOEL glances at NADIA. NADIA is staring at her folder. She hasn't opened it.

On the cover of the folder, below the Threshold Protocol logo, there's a small symbol — a circle with a line through it that might be a door, or might be a zero, or might be something else entirely.

NADIA traces it with her finger.

**END OF ACT ONE**

# ACT TWO

---

**INT. VOSS INSTITUTE - CORRIDOR - DAY**

*One week later.*

The program has moved from the community center to DR. VOSS's private institute — a converted Victorian house on the edge of the city that manages to look both welcoming and like something a child would draw when asked to draw a place where things go wrong.

PRIYA walks down the hallway, notebook in hand, examining everything. She stops at a door labeled OBSERVATION. Tries the handle. Locked.

She takes out her phone. Photographs the door. Keeps walking.

**INT. VOSS INSTITUTE - SESSION ROOM B - CONTINUOUS**

JOEL sits in a reclining chair. Electrodes on his temples. A MONITOR beside him shows his heart rate: 94 BPM. Elevated but not alarming.

DR. VOSS sits nearby with a tablet.

**DR. VOSS**
Tell me the last time you used an elevator.

**JOEL**
Two thousand and nineteen.

**DR. VOSS**
What happened?

**JOEL**
Nothing. That's the thing. Nothing happened. I just — I was in it and I thought: *the cable could snap. Right now. The cable could snap and the walls would come in and I would —*

**DR. VOSS**
And then?

**JOEL**
And then I got out at the next floor and walked eleven flights of stairs and cried in a bathroom for twenty minutes.

**DR. VOSS**
And the elevator didn't snap.

**JOEL**
Obviously.

**DR. VOSS**
But you learned the wrong lesson.

**JOEL**
What do you mean?

**DR. VOSS**
Your brain concluded: *I survived because I got out*. Not: *there was nothing to survive*. The escape became the solution. So the fear stayed.

*(beat)*

Safety behaviors, Joel. Every time you take the stairs, you're telling your nervous system the elevator was dangerous.

**JOEL**
Wasn't it?

**DR. VOSS**
*(not unkindly)*
No. It was a box that went up. That's all it was.

Joel stares at the ceiling. His heart rate on the monitor: 91 BPM. Coming down.

**JOEL**
So what do we do?

DR. VOSS stands. Crosses to a door in the wall that Joel hasn't noticed before — it's the same color as the wall, nearly invisible.

She opens it.

It's a small elevator. Four feet by four feet. Brushed steel interior. The kind that exists in old buildings, meant for freight.

Joel's heart rate on the monitor: 118 BPM. Immediately.

**DR. VOSS**
We start here. You don't have to go in. Not today. Today you just look at it.

**JOEL**
*(staring at it)*
That's — you had an elevator installed in a *therapy room*?

**DR. VOSS**
I told you. Precision.

Joel looks at the elevator. The elevator looks back, the way elevators do — the interior light slightly too yellow, the dimensions slightly too small for comfort.

**JOEL**
What floor does it go to?

**DR. VOSS**
*(beat)*
The basement.

**JOEL**
Great.

**INT. VOSS INSTITUTE - HALLWAY - LATER**

CASSIE is waiting on a bench, scrolling her phone. DEREK sits beside her, enormous, quiet, watching the floor.

**CASSIE**
How was yours?

**DEREK**
She asked me about my daughter.

**CASSIE**
That's... therapy, I guess.

**DEREK**
I haven't talked about my daughter in three years.

A beat.

**CASSIE**
Did it help?

**DEREK**
*(long pause)*
She already knew things. Things I didn't tell her. Details about what happened.

**CASSIE**
What? How?

**DEREK**
She said it was in the intake form. But I checked my intake form.

*(beat)*

I didn't write those things down.

CASSIE looks at him. Starts to respond — PRIYA appears around the corner, walking fast, notebook clutched to her chest.

**PRIYA**
*(low, urgent)*
I need to show you both something.

She opens the notebook. Shows them a page of handwritten notes and a printed photograph — a schematic diagram, partially visible.

**PRIYA (CONT'D)**
I found this in the recycling bin in the copy room. It's a floor plan of this building. There are rooms on the basement level that aren't on the official layout.

**CASSIE**
How many rooms?

**PRIYA**
At least four. Possibly more.

**DEREK**
What's in them?

**PRIYA**
The diagram labels them as *"Immersive Exposure Environments."*

A beat.

**CASSIE**
What does that mean?

**PRIYA**
I don't know yet. But there's something else.

She turns to another page. A printed table — data, columns, numbers.

**PRIYA (CONT'D)**
I found outcome data from a previous cohort. Not the published data. The *real* data.

**CASSIE**
How many people?

**PRIYA**
Twelve patients. Eight weeks. Complete fear extinction in all twelve subjects.

**DEREK**
That's — that's amazing. That's what we want.

**PRIYA**
Eleven of them were discharged successfully. The data ends there for them.

*(beat)*

The twelfth patient's file is flagged. Outcome listed as —

She shows them the page. A single word in the column where the outcome should be.

**PRIYA (CONT'D)**
*"Threshold."*

**CASSIE**
That's the name of the program.

**PRIYA**
Yes. It's also apparently a medical outcome, according to this document. And I have no idea what it means.

They stare at the page.

Down the hall, a door opens. DR. VOSS emerges with NADIA, hand gently on her elbow, speaking quietly. NADIA looks pale but composed. Something has shifted in her face — something has been loosened.

DR. VOSS looks up. Sees the three of them clustered together. Sees the notebook.

Her expression doesn't change. Not a flicker.

**DR. VOSS**
*(warmly)*
Good. You're getting to know each other. That's exactly what I hoped for.

*(to Priya)*
You're next. Room A, whenever you're ready.

She smiles. Turns. Goes back into her office.

The four of them stand in the hallway.

**CASSIE**
*(very quietly)*
Priya. That notebook goes home with you tonight.

**PRIYA**
Obviously.

**INT. VOSS INSTITUTE - SESSION ROOM A - NIGHT**

Late. The building is quiet. DR. VOSS sits alone at her desk in the session room, reviewing files. She picks up her phone. Dials.

**DR. VOSS**
*(into phone)*
Cohort two is progressing well. The resistant cases are actually the most promising — their threat-assessment systems are highly developed. Once we redirect that...

*(listening)*

Yes. All five. I'm confident.

*(listening, then, carefully)*

The Park woman is observant. The biochemist is already asking questions. We may need to accelerate the timeline.

*(listening)*

No. Not like last time. We do it correctly. Step by step. You skip steps, the extinction is incomplete.

*(a pause, then softer)*

I know what's at stake. I've always known.

She hangs up. Sits for a moment in the quiet.

Then she opens her desk drawer. Takes out the SYRINGE and the VIAL. Holds the vial up to the lamp. The clear liquid catches the light.

She puts it back. Closes the drawer.

Opens her leather journal. Writes one line.

We PUSH IN to read it:

*"They're almost ready for the basement."*

She closes the journal.

The white noise machine hums.

And then — from somewhere below them, from the basement of this converted Victorian house — a SOUND. Low. Rhythmic. Like someone breathing very slowly, very deliberately, the way you breathe when someone has taught you exactly how to breathe.

Inhale. Hold. Exhale.

Inhale. Hold. Exhale.

DR. VOSS hears it. She does not react. She has heard it before.

She turns off the lamp.

**INT. VOSS INSTITUTE - SESSION ROOM B - CONTINUOUS**

The elevator door — the one in the wall of the therapy room — is closed.

A light is on inside it. The yellow light, slightly too warm.

The elevator begins, very slowly, to descend.

It wasn't called. No one pressed a button. No one is in the building.

It descends.

It stops.

From behind the closed door, from the basement below, the BREATHING continues.

And then — a second sound. A voice. Barely audible. Speaking in a flat, calm, therapeutic tone, like a recording of a guided meditation —

**VOICE (O.S.)**
*(from below)*
You are safe. You are safe. The danger is not real. The danger is not —

The voice CUTS OFF.

SMASH CUT TO BLACK.

**END OF ACT TWO**

**INT. JOEL'S APARTMENT - NIGHT**

Joel is in bed. Awake. Staring at the ceiling. His phone shows 3:17 AM.

He picks up the folder — the Threshold Protocol folder — and opens it to his personal treatment plan. He's read it before. He reads it again.

His eyes stop on a paragraph near the bottom of the second page. Something he either missed or that wasn't there before.

He sits up.

**JOEL**
*(reading aloud, quietly)*
*"Note: Patient has prior exposure to Threshold-adjacent stimuli. Recommend accelerated desensitization. Timeline: three weeks, not eight."*

He stares at it.

He's never seen this paragraph before. He's certain of it.

He picks up his phone. Starts to call someone. Stops. Puts the phone down.

Gets up. Goes to his window. Looks out at the city.

His apartment is on the fourteenth floor.

He's never noticed before — because he's never let himself notice — that from this window, if he looks straight down, he can see the roof of the building across the street.

And on that roof, standing completely still, looking up at him —

A FIGURE.

Too far to see clearly. Too still to be someone who just happened to be there. Standing in the exact center of the rooftop. Arms at sides.

Joel stares.

The figure doesn't move.

Joel reaches for his phone again. When he looks back up —

The figure is gone.

He stands at the window for a long time. The city hums below him.

His phone buzzes. A text. Unknown number.

He looks at it.

The text reads:

*"Fear doesn't go away on its own, Joel. It has to be convinced. — See you Thursday."*

Joel stares at the screen.

Below the message, a second line appears as he watches, typing itself in real time:

*"Don't take the stairs."*

HOLD on Joel's face. The city lights behind him. The empty rooftop across the street.

FADE TO BLACK.

**OVER BLACK:**

The breathing again. Slow. Methodical. Therapeutic.

Inhale. Hold. Exhale.

And then — just before silence — a sound like a door closing somewhere deep underground.

**FADE OUT.**
