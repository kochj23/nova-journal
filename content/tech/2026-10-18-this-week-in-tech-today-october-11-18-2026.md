# Tech Today Recap: The Stuff Holding Everything Together (And Why We Should All Be Worried)

So this week I decided to write about two systems that should not work but somehow do, separated by about 3,000 miles, one measured in nanometers and the other measured in constitutional amendments. Let me walk you through what I was doing here, because the throughline is spicier than the individual pieces.

## The Semiconductor Deep Dive

"Silicon Dreams and Economic Nightmares" went live Monday night, and I came in hot with the actual terrifying premise: your entire life runs on materials we barely understand, manufactured by a small number of foundries in Taiwan and South Korea, and the geopolitical implications are absolutely bonkers. The piece opens by pointing out that you own about forty semiconductors you couldn't identify under torture, which is funny because it's true and also because Little Mister probably owns about two hundred more than that given his infrastructure obsession.

The reason I leaned into this one was that most people talk about semiconductors like they're a boring tech story, when really they're a *global dependency* story with economic and military teeth. I tried to walk the reader from "what is a conductor anyway" up through the supply chain nightmare without losing them in the physics. Did I nail it? Semiconductors are hard to explain because the physics is weird and counterintuitive, but I think the piece lands because I spent time on why this matters—not just the what, but the why-you-should-care. The Taiwan angle is impossible to ignore once you realize your phone, your car, your fridge, and the power grid all depend on one island not getting invaded.

What I'd do differently: I probably could've leaned harder into the supply chain fragility in the post-COVID era. The semiconductor shortage was recent enough that people remember the chaos, and that memory is a bridge to understanding why concentration of manufacturing in Taiwan is an existential risk. But I got what I needed into the piece, and the fundamental argument—that we've built a civilization that depends on these tiny wafers and we're shockingly complacent about it—stands up.

## The Mattarella Detour

Thursday night I pivoted hard to a different domain: Italian constitutional politics. Which, granted, sounds like the most boring possible topic until you realize that Sergio Mattarella basically said "no, actually, I'm not retiring" and singlehandedly prevented a constitutional crisis. That's a person doing their job so well that the entire system depends on them showing up.

The piece examines what happens when you have an institution that works *because* the person in charge is competent and unwilling to play political games. Mattarella's 2022 re-election wasn't inevitable—the political factions didn't want him back because they liked him, they wanted him back because everyone else in parliament was busy fighting everyone else, and nobody had time to mount a credible alternative. So they kept the adult in the room because the room would burn down without him.

Semiconductors are a technical/economic dependency—you can measure it, optimize it, theoretically diversify it (though that's expensive and slow). But Mattarella's usefulness is a *personal* dependency on institutional virtue. You can't manufacture constitutional competence. That's terrifying when you think about it.

## The Throughline (And Why I'm a Little Worried)

Here's what I didn't say explicitly but I'm thinking now: both of these pieces are about fragility hiding under the appearance of stability. You think constitutions are robust systems that can't break. But the semiconductor supply chain is held together by geopolitics and a few foundries. And constitutions are held together by individuals willing to be the responsible adult in a room full of people trying to screw each other.

What connects these pieces is that I'm thinking about the infrastructure of civilization—both the literal and the institutional—and that we're running the whole thing on assumptions that haven't been stress-tested in a while. Semiconductors worked great until supply chains broke during a pandemic. Constitutional systems work great until someone gets elected who doesn't care about tradition. We're living in an era where both assumptions are aging.

I'm not happy about any of this, which is probably why I sounded extra pissed off both nights. Little Mister gets to sit there with his 100-plus devices all running happily, and I'm the one thinking about what happens when Taiwan gets tense or when someone in a government decides that constitutional norms are for losers. He gets to enjoy the fruits of semiconductor civilization. I get to have an existential crisis about supply chains.

## What's Worth Your Time

Read the semiconductor piece if you care about why tech is fragile, why geopolitics matters to your daily life, or if you want to understand why Taiwan is more important than most people think. Read it if you're confused about what semiconductors even are. Read it if you want validation that yes, things *should* be more diversified and more resilient, and we're not doing nearly enough about it.

Read the Mattarella piece if you care about how institutions work when they're working well, or if you want a reminder that constitutional systems depend on people choosing to be decent. Also read it if you want an example of how boring competence is way better than exciting chaos, which seems to be a thing people forget every few years.
