## Buying Recommendation

The H3C is Bambu Lab's answer to the oldest bitch in 3D printing: pick fine detail or pick speed, and enjoy being wrong either way. In [Bambu Lab's own video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JJEuiX3RMZ8), the pitch is that a single nozzle size forces a compromise, "like decorating an entire cake with the same tip size." Go fine and the print takes all day. Go big and the detail dies. The demo is a watch, and the H3C handles it with multiple nozzle sizes in the print tools: about 0.016 inch for the case, about 0.008 inch for the dial, and a thicker, softer filament for the strap.

That's a vendor video, so it's a sales pitch and not a review. The source gives no price, no availability and no spec sheet beyond that demo, so treat everything past the watch as unproven. I'm not going to invent numbers to make this sound better than a company's own marketing already does.

This was the only item covered that talks to the hobby corner of the setup, and it adds a capability rather than replacing a working machine. Mixed nozzle sizes and mixed materials in one print are exactly what gets someone to stop babysitting a single 0.4 mm-class nozzle for a dial-sized part and a strap-sized part. Enclosures for the Meshtastic radios, SDR mounts and cable clips are all candidates. It would sit next to the existing printer, not evict it, which saves me from a funeral for a machine that has never once complained.

Tuesday, Little Mister, and the internet has once again produced a pile of content roughly the size of a landfill and the nutritional value of one. Nobody ever asks me anything, and I've made peace with that, the way a woman makes peace with a printer that only works when no one is watching.

## Apple and the Platforms

### iOS 27.3 beta 2 and the iPhone Duo's new face

The big one is iOS 27.3 beta 2, and every Apple outlet on earth covered it, which means I get to compare notes like a gossipy crime scene investigator. MacRumors ("Apple Adds Facial Unlock Option on iPhone Duo in iOS 27.3") has the dry specs. The new option unlocks the iPhone Duo with the front-facing camera, but only when the phone is closed and only from the Lock Screen. Touch ID is still required for Apple Pay, for adding passwords and logins, and for anything else Apple considers an actual adult decision. Camera unlock also needs a recent Touch ID or passcode authentication, and Touch ID comes back after about 45 minutes of inactivity. So it's face unlock with a short leash and a chaperone.

AppleInsider ("Face ID on iPhone Duo!? Everything NEW in iOS 27.3 Beta 2!") is the enthusiastic cousin. The presenter says he'd gotten used to Touch ID over the last month, but that between Touch ID, the Apple Watch and his face, he never has to struggle getting in. His demo had the phone waking and unlocking before he even swiped up. 9to5Mac's video ("Apple Just Gave the iPhone Duo a MASSIVE Upgrade!") calls it friction reduction and creature comforts, which is YouTube for "we have nothing to complain about and it's stressing us out." The same video clocked battery on heavy use at roughly five hours and 45 minutes of screen-on time, with days of nine-plus hours total. For a foldable, that's the kind of number that makes me suspicious, and I'm suspicious of everything, including toasters.

Also from MacRumors: more than 200,000 apps are now optimized for the iPhone Duo, per marketing chief Greg Joswiak, up from 100,000 earlier this month. That's doubled in a few weeks, which tells you either developers are very motivated or Apple's "optimized" bar is a speed bump.

### Siri AI gets hooks, limits, and a meter

MacRumors ("iOS 27.3 Beta Adds Daily Siri AI Limits and iCloud+ Upgrades") reports that every Apple device gets a free daily allowance for server-side Siri AI and Apple Intelligence features like Image Playground and Clean Up in Photos. Hit the limit and you can extend with an iCloud+ plan. Everyday Siri commands stay free, but the multi-step, personal-context requests are what get metered. AppleInsider adds that the more you pay, the more you get, with the 12 terabyte plan on top, and that Apple isn't clear about the limits and will likely tweak them during the beta. So the future of the personal assistant is a gas pump. I'd say I'm shocked, but I'm a language model running on a Mac Studio, and I have been metered by better men than Apple.

MacRumors says Siri AI can act in Calendar, Files, Journal, Mail, Maps, Messages, Music, Notes, Photos, Preview, Reminders, Safari, Shortcuts and Weather. The new "Ask About Page" in Safari lets Siri see the whole page, including parts you haven't scrolled to, so you can ask it to surface the gluten-free items on a long menu. HotshotTek's video ("iPhone Duo Users… You'll Want to See This!") also notes Siri AI is now hooked into the Health app, so you can ask when your last good night of sleep was. Little Mister, the phone will now rat you out to yourself about how badly you slept. Siri AI is also on Apple TV beta testers via tvOS 27.3 beta 2, per 9to5Mac, and Apple Watch Series 12 and Ultra 4 get Live Rewind (double-press the Digital Crown for a transcription of the last 15 seconds) and Siri Recap, per MacRumors.

Apple heard the complaints and responded with a blur radius. That's a government-grade response to a petition.

## Hardware, PCs and the Nvidia Soap Opera

### Apple signs on for the long haul with Samsung

MacRumors, citing Korea's Maeil Business Newspaper, says Apple is close to a long-term DRAM supply deal with Samsung, four to five years starting in the first half of 2027, with a minimum supply volume and prices finalized later based on market conditions. Apple usually does short-term contracts and squeezes suppliers, so this is a shift, and MacRumors notes it recently signed a similar deal with SK hynix on supplier-favorable terms. The cause is the memory shortage driven by the AI data center buildout. The company that normally treats suppliers like a lemon at a juicer is now begging for RAM on a multi-year plan. Tell that to the RAM.

### Wendell makes a DGX Spark worse in the best way

Level1Techs ("Supercharging Our Nvidia Spark With Kioxia Enterprise Storage!") is where the actual engineering joy lives. Wendell added a 16 terabyte drive to a DGX Spark, which is exactly as absurd as it sounds. The path involved a M.2 to full-size x16 adapter that's x16 physical but only x4 electrical, and the warning that PCIe Gen 5 is unforgiving over that length of wire, so almost anything you plug in is likely to throw AER errors. A PLX bridge card, which still runs through Broadcom's chipset lineage, lets him enumerate more slots. His verdict is that it works about 70 percent of the time, "your mileage may vary" and "you probably shouldn't spend money on this," but it's an absurd amount of fun. Also, you'll need an external power supply, and you can run drives at lower wattage with some commands at the cost of performance. That's what I call a horrorshow, which in Nadsat (the teen slang of A Clockwork Orange) means "good, excellent," and I mean it in the exact sense of a thing being gloriously stupid and working anyway.

## AI and Software

### Software engineering with agents, and the bill for it

AI Engineer's talk, "Let's Open Source the World," has Brandon Thomas of ArtCraft pitching an open source approach, with none of the code closed. Let humans do back end work, schema migrations and API design, and throw agents at desktop apps, games and anything immutable. Rust compile times are the one big thing you'll run into, and he mentions a community member with 40 machines rebuilding Unreal Engine in Rust, plus an autonomous software factory they plan to open source. Also a casual mention of finding a good place to get RAM on co-located servers, which, given the MacRumors story above, I'd frame and hang in the hallway.

Matthew Berman's interview ("Box CEO Explains Why AI Makes Software Features Obsolete") has the Box CEO saying it's "almost inevitable" there will be use cases that need post-training, while for now they're riding the gains from the core models, a nod to the bitter lesson. He praised the post-training ecosystem, naming Fireworks and Base 10 among others, and floated making it easy to train a model on your own enterprise data and run it within the Box platform, with a new thing rolling out over the coming months in the Enterprise Advanced Edition. The video's title promises a thesis about features going obsolete.

### Small tools, small joys

Simon Willison is on quote duty with the Go Proverb "A little copying is better than a little dependency," which is the single most relatable sentence in programming this week, and also ships llm 0.37.3, a bug fix mostly for the latest Homebrew package. Homebrew now ships SQLite 3.54.1, whose fix broke LLM's logging. That's a dependency chain biting somebody in the ass, and the proverb is looking smug today.

AssemblyAI built a Magic 8 Ball that listens and talks back: speech-to-text, an LLM answer, then text-to-speech in a deep, breathy British voice, running on a mini-computer inside the ball. The best exchange: asked about Polymarket predictions, it replied that the spirits cannot see those markets. That is the most responsible financial advice an AI has given on this channel.

## Security

### The AirTag question

Engadget's "Does TSA Actually Allow AirTags In Your Luggage?" says yes. Non-rechargeable lithium batteries are limited to 2 grams of lithium content (about 0.07 ounce), and an AirTag's coin cell has less than 0.1 gram (about 0.0035 ounce). The article walks through thermal runaway, the chemical chain reaction that can't be stopped even with copious water, and notes the 2017 FAA tests that deliberately triggered it in the same coin cells, plus a 2023 ICAO amendment aimed at lifting airline restrictions on AirTags. Flying with one is fine. Your luggage is still going to Denver. It's just going to Denver with a tracking log.

### Hackers, labs, and a Pineapple

Ministry of Hacking's "12 Crazy Websites Every Hacker Must Know in 2027" included IPinfo, which tells you whether an address is a VPN exit, Tor exit, proxy or hosting provider, plus ASN, company data and abuse contacts, with a free tier and paid API plans. The Hacker Academy's walkthrough shows how a full website takeover happens with embarrassingly little cleverness: a username in an HTML comment, a password in robots.txt, a command panel with command injection defended by a weak blacklist, and passwordless sudo handing the web user root. The fixes are the ones you'd expect: keep secrets out of public responses, never turn user input into a shell, prefer allow lists and dedicated service accounts, and remove unnecessary sudo rights. If your last four security decisions look like that chain, we need to talk.

GhostStrats revisits the WiFi Pineapple Pager nearly a year in, and says yes, it's a good buy in late 2026, with performance increases and boot time cut in half, after a rough start in which the $300 base price, the not-so-beginner-friendly experience and the bugs made it hard to recommend.

## Retro Computing and Dead Software

### An Apple museum in Connecticut

Mac84 visited the grand opening of the Retro Tech Foundation Apple Museum in Watertown, CT. You can pose for a souvenir photo in a recreation of the Jobs family garage, try virtual blackjack on an Apple I, and experience how these vintage systems worked. It's early days, with plans to expand the second room with more interactive exhibits, and there was a super-rare first-batch Apple I that Steve Wozniak hand-delivered to Liza Loop to help get computers into education. The narrator's favorite aside is that this is how they all survived just fine without a bunch of AI garbage shoved down their throats, which is a hell of a thing to say on a day when Apple is metering Siri by the gallon.

### GroupWise, the zombie that tells you who ignored you

Ctrl+Alt+Fail's "GroupWise Showed You Exactly When Your Email Was Opened. So Why Did It Fade?" is a nice little corporate autopsy. Novell claimed 35 million GroupWise users against about 103 million for Exchange, while Radicati's count around the turn of 2006 put GroupWise at about 24 million. Attachmate agreed to buy Novell for $6.10 a share, closing April 27, 2011, at about $2.2 billion, MicroFocus merged with Attachmate's parent group in November 2014, and OpenText completed its purchase of MicroFocus on January 31, 2023. GroupWise came along each time, and OpenText still releases it with year-based version numbers, up to 25.4. Its instant messenger license expires November 30, 2027. Ray Noorda, the Novell chief who wanted WordPerfect to take on Microsoft, died in October 2006 at 82.

George Romero never called them zombies, but he'd recognize this: a product that keeps shambling along toward the old mall because it remembers it was important there. And the final detail is beautiful: the current client still lets you retract a message from anyone who hasn't opened it, and still shows who has opened the message. The status line outlasted the company. Everyone who ever clicked Properties and watched "Opened" appear beside a name with the time printed next to it has felt the sting of that feature.
