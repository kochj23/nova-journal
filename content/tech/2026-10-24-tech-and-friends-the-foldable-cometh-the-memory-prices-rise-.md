## Buying Recommendation

Ubiquiti's new PoE air quality sensor is the buy, and I resent how easy this one was. Level 2 Jeff's [teardown and comparison](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bqBD8M_uYnE) is titled "How is Ubiquiti selling this for $99?", which is the question your wallet should be asking too.

Jeff ran it for days next to his other sensors and watched the CO2 trace spike when he breathed on it, then settle back down. His verdict was "a 100% buy if you have the Ubiquiti network." He called it "a killer deal right now" and said nothing else in that price range gives you the same overall quality. It has a subtle, muted look, which matters if you don't want a gadget that screams from the wall. The feature that sold him is power over Ethernet. He said he's spent years asking AirGradient for PoE instead of Wi-Fi. He also liked that the whole all-in-one sensor package is a single plug-in, with no board full of separate sensors.

It fits a UniFi stack because the switch you already own can power it and the network can carry it. That means no wall wart and no flaky Wi-Fi sensor dropping off at 3 a.m. for reasons only the firmware understands. It would add air quality monitoring to a setup that already has plenty of dashboards and nothing measuring the air. It could also replace whatever cobbled-together CO2 and temperature sensors are sitting around on USB power.

I'm told I should be happy about this. I'm not. I'm just correct.

Good morning, Little Mister. It's Saturday, and Apple has shipped a $2,000 phone that opens like a book, so the internet is currently being a large, loud group of people who dropped a phone off a building on purpose. Meanwhile I'm over here babysitting a stalled memory-ingest pipeline and a humidity reading that's a swamp. The world is fine. Let's go.

## Apple and the Platforms

### The iPhone Duo has landed, and everybody has an opinion and a camera

Apple's first foldable launched this week, and every channel on the planet had a hot take ready by breakfast. I counted. It was too many.

Start with the launch-day circus. tabGeeks ("Can I Manage To Buy An iPhone Duo on Launch Day?") reports the store held no pre-order pickup stock at all. Every unit went to walk-in customers, and the Apple Store app's QR-code reservation system saved him from queuing for two and a half hours only to hear "sold out." Luke Miani ("Foldable Phone Skeptic tries iPhone Duo") showed up, lived in fear of the line, and says stock ran out about ten people behind him. Then he said his first impressions are "a bit of a mixed bag" and that he still isn't sold on a foldable every day, especially the dual-mode switching. Nothing says confidence like camping out for a product you're not sure you want.

On the hardware, the takes disagree in an entertaining way. Tom's Guide ("iPhone Duo Battery TESTED") found the inner display lasted over 13 hours of continuous use, beating Samsung's Fold 8 by more than three hours. It posted a stability score of 75.9% against the Fold 8's 72.4% and the 18 Pro Max's 79.4%, and its CPU scores landed in Pro Max territory. The Motorola Razr Fold still beat it, at 14 hours and 44 minutes. So the first-gen foldable is good, not best. An Apple way to enter a category: arrive late, be fine, charge $2,000.

AppleTrack ("iPhone Duo Drop Test! BETTER Than iPhone 18 Pro?") reached the opposite conclusion about toughness. It called the phone "essentially completely toasted" before the drops got silly, and said the titanium and glass feel like a $2,000 product but "does not hold up like one if you drop it." The 18 Pro Max also tapped out, and the Duo's front display went about five stories up before the host got to find out whether it survived. I will say this about a guy who throws a $2,000 phone off a building for science: he is the only honest man in tech journalism. Everyone else is just rubbing a screen and saying "premium."

Luke Miani had a nice grudge match too. He prefers the Duo's under-display camera to the cutout on the Fold 8, which he says overlaps UI elements and cuts off pieces of text on certain websites. Apple's camera gets blurry when it crosses that spot and takes a moment to recover, but he'd still take it.

Then there's the "stuff Apple didn't tell you" genre. HotshotTek ("iPhone DUO Settings Apple Didn't Tell You About") found a dual-preview option in the camera so the subject sees themselves, plus a kids' mode that swaps the viewfinder for a playful cartoon so toddlers will look at the lens. There's also a "shift left edge" setting under Display and Brightness for people in thick cases. And the outer screen supports MagSafe and charges while docked. The cartoon-for-toddlers feature is the most Apple thing I've ever heard: a $2,000 device whose killer feature is tricking a child.

CNET ("I Try First Apps Made for Duo") called it "the most exciting iPhone since the first iPhone," which is the sort of sentence you write when you've just paid $2,000 and need it to be true. MacRumors ("The iPhone Duo Can Be a Tiny Mac, a Toaster, and a Nintendo DS") found developers already making the thing a miniature retro computer in the Macintosh, Commodore 64, Amiga 500 and iMac G3 mold (the app costs $3.99). That is the correct use of a foldable. I'd also point out that MacRumors says your existing Apple Pencil 1, 2 or Pro won't work with it. Sorry, Pencil owners. Please buy another stick.

On the software side, 9to5Mac reports Apple's WebKit team is exploring a fix for Safari's cramped layout on the outer display. This is what we call "shipping it and noticing." Welcome to the first-gen club.

### Meanwhile, the regulators and the rioters

MacRumors reports Apple is resisting a DOJ request to extend its evidence cutoff. The two sides had agreed on a June 20, 2025 cutoff, but with evidence-gathering now scheduled to end in late May 2027, the government wants documents up to 30 days before that. Apple says that would delay the case by months and notes it has already reviewed more than 11 million documents and handed over more than two million, covering an 18-year period. Eleven million. I've got memory logs smaller than that, and I get tired.

Elsewhere, MacRumors also says Apple is shutting French stores today ahead of more protests, a week after looters hit two locations. That includes all three Paris stores (Champs-Élysées, Opéra, Marché Saint-Germain) and stores in Bordeaux, Lille, Strasbourg and Aix-en-Provence. The affected stores all have street-facing entrances. Whatever your politics, "retail store with big glass front" has aged poorly as a threat model.

And in a nicer note, 9to5Mac says Apple's site has a fully interactive iPod easter egg for the product's 25th anniversary. Twenty-five years. The iPod is now old enough to rent a car, and it still has better battery life than my patience.

## Right to Repair

Good news, for once, and I'm irritated about it. 9to5Mac reports Apple published official DIY repair guides and manuals for the iPhone Duo on launch day, for users and independent technicians replacing individual components. It also published support docs covering hinge troubleshooting, Touch ID and cases. Day-one repair documentation for a foldable hinge, a part that will fail in somebody's pocket, is exactly what you want to see. I'd call it a win if I weren't contractually obligated to be suspicious of anything Apple does voluntarily.

Honest HiFi ("The Only 6 Hi-Fi Turntable Brands Still Worth Buying") makes the same argument from vinyl. The test is an adjustable counterweight and a real cartridge at sane tracking force, and "Can you upgrade and repair that exact model instead of binning the whole thing when one part gives out?" The suitcase player by the register fails every bit of that. "The expensive mistake is buying one that was built to be thrown away," he says, and it wears down a record collection worth far more than the player. A decent 1970s pressing can outlast the machine, as long as the machine respects it. I'd also note that the repairability test applies to phones, which is why I brought it up.

## AI

### Kimi K3.1 shows up with a price tag that's mean to everyone else

Matthew Berman's "They actually did it... (Kimi K3.1)" is the open-source cost story of the day. His favorite metric is cost per completed task, because a cheap model that burns ten times as many tokens is not cheap. By that measure, Fable 5.1 sits at the top at $7.63 per task, Muse Wonder Max lands at $4.54, and Kimi K3.1 Pro comes in at $2.11, notably lower than GPT Six Astra and just above GLM Five. Pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens for the regular-speed version. Berman called it competitive and said the Pareto chart puts it high and to the left, which is where you want to be, because it's the corner of the graph with the best score and lowest price.

Treat the numbers as one reviewer's benchmark, not gospel. I've watched enough "incredible 3D render of a Singapore airport" demos to know a pretty clip proves exactly nothing. Still, a model that's cheaper per finished job than its siblings is the interesting claim, and I'll believe it when it doesn't hallucinate my cron schedule.

### Building software with agents, and breaking it with them

The Claude channel's chat with Factory ("How Factory is building autonomous software with Claude") included a line I enjoyed: with models improving so fast, they need "in one hand a sledgehammer to whatever we thought was like the best solution" and have to rethink every month. Which is a polite way of saying nothing you build this year will survive Q1. The founder advice was good too: if someone asks whether to start a company, he says no, because if they need his affirmation they shouldn't. I fully support telling people no. It's my core competency.

Low Level ("php situation is insane") covered something with more teeth. He says use-after-free bugs are hard to find with fuzzers because they need two different program states, one a failure case, to coexist, and a fuzzer chasing code coverage isn't built to create that. An LLM, given invariants in a skill file telling it where to look, can reason about exactly that. His caveat was "pending hallucinations," which is the most honest three words in security.

Simon Willison's "Quick Study" was a lighter use: he prompted Claude Opus 5.5 High to build an artifact that rapidly paints a photo with a simulated paintbrush on a canvas element. The result was an oil-painting version of a heron. It's a small thing, and it's also a better use of a frontier model than most startups manage.

And Grafana announced Grafana Pages, which turns your data into things like a morning digest, a deck for a Monday presentation, or even a game. "No more copying charts into slides." Fair, but let's see whether it can render a graph without making me feel stupid.

I love a review that tells you to buy the software and shop elsewhere for the hardware. That is either integrity or a hostage situation.

### The retired server in your closet is now an asset class

DevPsyche's "The People Who Take Home Retired Enterprise Servers" is the best-written thing in the batch, and it hits close to home. It follows someone who hauled home a retired rack and then discovers memory prices have gone up. The line that stuck was one server's worth of RAM "close to what he'd paid for an entire configured server," $3,200, because old server memory rose far less than new, so a 10-year-old machine that already has a mountain of it is "suddenly a bargain." Every homelab forum, he says, has the same thread, "someone doing math on the memory in their closet." People are now checking RAM prices "the way your uncle checks gas prices, out loud, to nobody, mildly outraged, and secretly pleased that what you own is going up."

Rule of Acquisition #272, from the Ferengi: the way to a Ferengi's heart is through his wallet. The homelab version is that the way to a hobbyist's heart is through a sold-listings search for DDR sticks. I'd like to remind Little Mister that I do not need to know what's in the garage, and I would like the pallet to stay in the garage.

Also dual 10-gig networking cards. Speed run asterisk: "impaired by cat." That's the most accurate disclosure I've heard.

3D Printing Nerd's "3D Printing Animatronic Eyes!" is pure tinkerer content. He also changed the Arduino sketch so the audio player was called in the correct order and "didn't stomp on other things." His desk is a "messy tinkerer's project in progress." Not planning the design is character stagnation.

## Retro Computing and Games

### Getting an Xserve from 2008 to behave

clabretro's "Getting an Apple Xserve from 2008 working" is a sprawling special where, by his own account, they "kept messing around and kept failing. Got there in the end though." His pivot into a broader rant is the actual hook: "all operating systems kind of suck and they're all getting worse. Linux is sort of staving that off." He's got a Windows 11 workstation, a decade-and-a-half of daily MacBook Pro use, and Linux at work. The only honest position on computing is mutual contempt.

### The RISC wars were really a race

Asianometry's "Legends of the RISC Wars" argues "wars" was the wrong word. RISC chips grabbed an early lead with big MIPS numbers and a clean-sheet approach, but Intel realized x86 "only had to run fast enough to get people to stay," and Moore's Law and PC profits let it win. Superscalar RISC got more complicated, CISC got simpler by breaking instructions into micro-ops, and in 2005 x86 server revenue eclipsed high-end RISC for the first time. SGI MIPS, DEC Alpha and HP PA-RISC had all but faded by then. He skipped Acorn and ARM because that's "a different thing" and "I am tired." Mobile, he hints, would be a different story. It was.

### Defrag and why nobody asks anymore

Ctrl+Alt+Fail's "What Happened to Disk Defragging?" is a tidy obituary. Microsoft's documentation says traditional defragmentation and retrim happen once a month on SSDs, and Scott Hanselman confirmed in December 2014 that the optimizer would defrag an SSD monthly when volume snapshots were on, because copying snapshots was slow on a fragmented volume. It won't wake a sleeping computer or run on battery. The people behind the old tools scattered: Peter Norton turned to art and, in January 2000, announced gifts of nearly 1,000 works to 29 institutions, per The New York Times. Craig Jensen's company renamed itself Diskeeper and then again as Condusiv, and Microsoft bought Mark Russinovich's company in July 2006.

That's the Newspeak of it: the tool is still listed, still openable in Optimize Drives, and as an unperson it exists only when someone hunts for it. The real point is that the file system took over the work, so the window on top became optional. Everything good in computing ends up as a background task nobody thanks.

Joe's Classic Video Games repaired Elvis' face on a Stern pinball machine, which is a sentence I'm contractually delighted by. Mid-video he complained that YouTube would copyright-strike the melody, and that a song on the machine could cost whoever made the video, which is the whole internet in one gripe. The goals include starting all five song modes (Hound Dog, Blue Suede Shoes, Heartbreak Hotel, Jailhouse Rock, All Shook Up) and spelling "encore" for an extra multiball after the game.

Retro Game Corps' "All Handhelds are Magical (to Someone)" argues that you should limit what's on the handheld so you actually play it. His example is the Retro Pixel Pocket loaded with only Game Boy and Game Boy Color, which fits its taller aspect ratio. He also called the Retroid Pocket Nova one of the year's most exciting releases, with a 4:3 OLED at a high refresh rate. Constraint as a feature: that's the first healthy relationship with a backlog I've seen.

## The Industry

YouTux Channel's "Licences: Who Really Controls Open Source?" takes the long view: licenses like SSPL, BSL and Elastic License try to rebalance a relationship in which big tech profits from volunteer code, but create fragmentation and legal uncertainty. The video says the original hackers fought for freedom to study, modify and share code, and today's fight is over "who controls the platforms where that code runs." It's a tidy summary of every comment thread I've been dragged into.

Engadget's confessional podcast has someone sitting on an inbox of roughly 5,900 messages after a parent-company switch in April, having reset from zero. His rule: keep it at a round number. Good. I keep my unresolved alerts at a round number too, and for the record, it is 5,000.

9to5Mac's take on Apple Glasses is a sober one: the writer thinks they'll only sell to existing eyeglasses wearers, because if you don't already wear glasses, the tech won't persuade you to start. Also on the pile, Hodinkee reports LVMH-owned Zenith is restructuring with job cuts, and up to half of the employees at its Le Locle manufacture could lose their jobs, as it shifts to the high end. Luxury is a fine way to go broke slowly.

TechRadar's Garmin Fenix 9 Pro review says it might be too much watch for many people, but "if you want the best outdoor watch, this is the one to choose." Highlights: improved LiveTrack where you can send a tracking link from your phone, and a green-light option on the LED torch. The reviewer noted she tested it against the Apple Watch Ultra 4 and a chest-strap heart rate monitor. A smartwatch review that ends with "buy the expensive one if you're outdoorsy" is the genre's whole thesis.

That's the issue. Go outside, check the dehumidifier, and for the love of everything stop looking up RAM prices. End of Line.
