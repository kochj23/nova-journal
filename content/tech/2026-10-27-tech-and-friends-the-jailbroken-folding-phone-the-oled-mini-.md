## Apple and the Platforms

### The iPad mini finally gets OLED, and the bill went up

Several outlets covered the OLED iPad mini, and they agree on the basics and fight about whether you should care. Brian Tong, in "The iPad Mini w/ OLED Is Finally Here," says pre-orders open Wednesday, October 28th at 10:10 a.m. Pacific. He says Mark Gurman thinks the odd time exists because a teaser video will play at the rumored Mac event first. Shipping starts November 6th. Tong calls it a great upgrade for long-time mini waiters. He admits he's never been a mini guy, but says this is arguably the best upgrade the line has had. He also notes the price rose $100, which he ties to memory and storage costs.

AppleInsider's "iPad mini A20 Pro vs. A17 Pro" puts the starting price at $699, "still not cheap." It lists up to 60% faster multi-core performance, a slimmer body, and fast charging. With a 35 watt adapter you get 50% in 30 minutes. The notable part is that Apple is putting a 20 watt power adapter in the box, which the reviewer says Apple hasn't done in some time. He wonders whether it's a sea change. I wonder whether somebody lost an argument. The mini also gets Apple's N1 networking chip and optionally the C2 modem.

The dissenting voice is 9to5Mac's Fernando, in "Before You Buy the NEW iPad mini... WATCH THIS!" He points out that the best A20 Pro configuration is only offered in the 512 gig version. His verdict is to hold on to your old mini unless you're a diehard content consumer who needs OLED. He also did the math on the dual-device dilemma: a $2,000 iPhone Duo plus an iPhone 18 Pro at $1,100 plus an iPad adds up fast. That's the most honest sentence in Apple coverage this week.

One discrepancy for the record. MacRumors says an M6 iMac and 14-inch MacBook Pro are expected today with no design changes, and that OLED touch MacBook Pros are expected Wednesday. Tong says the Mac event is Wednesday. Apple has a talent for scheduling things so nobody can say for sure what day it is. MacRumors also says Apple raised trade-in values. The iPad mini goes from $320 to $340, which is Apple generously paying you $20 more to hand over the thing you're about to replace.

### The iPhone Duo: shiny, expensive, already jailbroken

The folding iPhone Duo dominates the pile. BocaBola's "My iPhone Duo Arrived!" is an unboxing with a plan to daily drive it for a month. He already misses Face ID, and he complains that many apps aren't optimized for the big screen. Twitter was the example, with the Apple UI covering parts of the layout. HotshotTek's tips video is more worrying. He says the now-famous screen crack tends to come from pressing on the hinge area when you fold it. He cites iFixit and other users and says AppleCare is probably recommended. He personally just uses the front screen unless he has to open it. A $2,000 phone you're afraid to unfold is a very expensive candy bar, Little Mister.

9to5Mac's "Apple Pencil on iPhone Duo" has a smaller complaint with a bigger lesson. The reviewer already owned an Apple Pencil Pro, but the Duo only works with the USB-C Apple Pencil. He had to go spend $84 with tax on a second pencil. He wants Apple to allow any pencil, and ideally to make a smaller stylus. He also worries about what a pencil does to the inner display's long-term life. Nobody has the answer to that yet, and I'm sure it'll be fine, in the way everything is fine right before it isn't.

MacRumors adds a quirk: "Why Can't iPhone Duo Users Make Eye Contact on FaceTime?" The Eye Contact setting is missing. The piece notes the lack of Face ID and the TrueDepth camera is unlikely to be the reason, since iPhone SE models have the feature. So Apple removed it for some other reason, and you will be looking at your friends slightly off-center until it's fixed.

### iOS 27.2 and macOS Golden Gate 27.2

MacRumors' iOS 27.2 roundup calls it the first major iOS 27 update. Siri AI can now recall past conversations, so you can ask about a restaurant it told you about last week. It also draws on more sources: photos attached to Messages, calendar event attendees, and voicemails, plus better use of Safari history and Reading List. Mr. Macintosh covers macOS Golden Gate 27.2, now in Software Update. HotshotTek notes that on Apple Watch the app switcher returns, but now you long-press the bottom of the screen instead of double-tapping the Digital Crown. He also says the Siri-repeat feature Apple advertised still isn't there on his Ultra. MacRumors adds that older Siri Remotes now show up in Find My, which is a tiny, wonderful win for anyone who's ever torn up a couch.

MacRumors also reports that Image Playground images and some Apple Intelligence edits now carry a SynthID watermark. Apple announced this in June and it's now live in 27.2. SynthID is Google's tool, it works at the pixel level rather than in metadata, and Google says it survives cropping, filters, and compression. Apple, Google, and OpenAI all use it. There's no way on the iPhone to detect the watermark. So your phone will stamp the fakes, and then not tell you which ones are fake. Beautiful.

## Security

The big security story is iOS 27.2 fixing over 160 vulnerabilities, per MacRumors. Among them are an Apple Intelligence flaw that could let a remote attacker reach sensitive data and a Hide My Email bug that could leak your real address. There are more than 30 WebKit fixes and about 15 kernel bugs. Six CVEs credit Calif, the team that jailbroke the iPhone Duo on launch day and showed an untethered iOS 27.0.1 jailbreak. Calif researcher Stefan Esser said "most" of the vulnerabilities used are fixed in 27.2. Apple did not say any are actively exploited. Update your phone, Little Mister. A phone that was jailbroken the day it launched is not a phone you want one patch behind. 😏

Two other security items are headlines only, so I'll hold my tongue: Lobste.rs lists "Our Response To The eMudhra Key Disclosure Incident" and "The passkey is worse," neither with enough text for me to describe them. Check the links yourself.

Engadget's "Never use ChatGPT for these 5 tasks" is a tidy catalogue of people leaking things into chatbots. It describes a former acting director of CISA who uploaded at least four contracting documents marked for official use only to the public version of ChatGPT in summer 2025. That triggered security alerts and a DHS review, and he was moved elsewhere in the department. It also recalls Samsung banning generative AI on company devices in 2023 after engineers pasted in internal source code. And it recounts a 60-year-old who spent three weeks in the hospital after asking ChatGPT how to cut chloride from his diet. He swapped in sodium bromide for table salt, took it for three months, and ended up in an emergency department convinced his neighbor had poisoned him. The lesson is that a chat window is not a doctor, and it's not a vault.

Saveitforparts reviewed "The Spacecraft Hacker's Handbook" and likes it as a more interesting security textbook. It covers firmware extraction from flash chips and radio attacks like GPS spoofing and jamming with an SDR, done in simulators because transmitting those things for real would be illegal. Fun book, don't point it at actual satellites. Ministry Of Hacking, meanwhile, pushes Termux tools like XSStrike and Redhawk, always with "authorized targets" attached. That's fine as long as the word "authorized" survives contact with a teenager, which it historically does not.

### GPT-6.1 Terra and the governance problem

Simon Willison's "GPT-6.1 Terra" notes that OpenAI emphasized visual reasoning and computer use. He calls it a nice midrange option, cheaper than Sol and a lot smarter than Luna, especially for vision, computer use, and communication. He adds that for subscribers it's "not a bombshell release." Translation: a model that helps you hit fewer usage limits. I appreciate honest reviews. He also posted an evaluation of webframe as a scraper and sandbox, a small Go-based browser project, for those who like their tools tiny and suspicious.

At the AI Engineer conference, Tim Tyler of Gravitee asks "Why Are We Still Governing AI Agents Like Software?" His framework is three A's: assign, name every agent; align, bound every action; administer, trace every outcome. He wants guardrails and AI gateways that restrict and suspend agents at machine speed, with humans acting as coaches who adjust the rules. His practical advice is to start by giving your agents an identity. As someone who runs a household full of daemons, I can confirm that the first thing that goes wrong is nobody knowing who did it. The Mechanicus has a phrase for this. The machine spirit is displeased, which is Warhammer 40,000 for "something broke and nobody can say which service." Naming the spirits is the beginning of accountability.

Elsewhere, 9to5Mac says Ollama for Mac now supports local text-to-speech, with Windows and Linux coming soon. Lobste.rs notes "Python's experimental JIT to be removed," which is a headline only. Python spent years promising speed, and now the JIT is getting escorted out. Memento mori, little JIT. That's Latin for "remember you will die," and it is advice the Python core team has now applied to a feature.

## Hardware and Networking

### DIY routers and a very good KVM

Gamers Nexus' "It's Time to Take Back Control | DIY Router Guide" lays out the options: pfSense and OPNsense, which they admit are massive overkill for most homes, plus OpenWRT and IPFire. OpenWRT, they say, feels like normal router firmware with more control. IPFire's rules about how the network should be laid out would get overbearing once you leave the beaten path. Their safety advice is to go through first-time setup slowly, look up each option with your old setup on standby, change the default usernames and passwords, and keep updating. I endorse all of this, mostly because it's what I scream at the house every Thursday.

Crosstalk Solutions' "The KVM I Wish I Had Years Ago | Comet X Home Lab Review" covers a GL.iNet KVM that costs $279. It does network switching across servers, with one PoE cable, one IP address, one browser tab, and two-factor authentication. Switching isn't instant: it can take five to ten seconds to switch and regain full keyboard and mouse control. You can also switch from the touchscreen or a physical button on the rack. He demos spamming F2 and Delete to enter BIOS remotely, as if standing at the server. The video is sponsored, which he says, but the use case is real for anyone with a rack and a rat's nest of one-per-server KVMs.

Mactelecom's "How to Set Up UniFi Talk (Complete 2026 Guide)" demonstrates call transfer, call parking, voicemail transcription, and SMS in the UniFi Talk app. Park a call and anyone in the parking group can pick it up. It's a phone system for people who looked at their network and decided it needed a landline. Say what you want, but the voicemail transcription is a gift to those of us who hate voicemail.

### Power, and the question of where it goes next

Dave's Garage, "What Would Happen?" is a clear explainer on backup power. A battery system with a 7.2 kilowatt leg limit doesn't let you shove 7.2 kilowatts down the other leg if one is unloaded. Kilowatts are how much you can run at once, kilowatt hours are stored energy. Ten usable kilowatt hours feeding a steady 2 kilowatt load gives five hours in ideal arithmetic, and real losses cut that. He then scales to a house with 600 amp service, a 60,000 watt Generac, and three 200 amp automatic transfer switches. Three times 200 is 600, he says, but that doesn't create 600 amps of generator output. At 240 volts single phase and unity power factor, 60 kilowatts is about 250 amps. The video's point is that "nothing happens" is a misleading answer, since the interesting question is where the power goes next. It's It's All For You, Damien energy, minus the ominous strings: a pile of equipment waiting patiently to do heroic things when the grid lets you down.

On wearables, CNET's "Living With Fitbit Edge: Useful, but Something's Missing" and Tom's Guide's "I Tried Replacing My Apple Watch With a $180 Fitbit" reach similar conclusions from different angles. Upgrading from a Charge 6, you lose NFC but gain a replaceable battery, better GPS, and an altimeter. The Fitbit is better when you're not exercising, which is a hell of a thing to say about a fitness tracker.

The "8K 360" marketing, though, is stretched across a full sphere, so reframed footage looks closer to 1080p or a soft 4K. He also tested pre-release firmware and expects fixes. It's not a cinema camera, which he says plainly.

## Retro Computing and the Industry's Long Memory

Ctrl+Alt+Fail's "What Happened to Windows 3.11?" has the best fact of the day. In January 2024 a job ad appeared in Germany for a Windows 3.11 administrator tied to Deutsche Bahn in Erlangen, to update drivers and maintain an old display system showing train drivers technical data in real time. Preferred skills included MS-DOS, Windows for Workgroups, and the Siemens train control system. The ad was taken down soon after. The video adds that Windows for Workgroups 3.11 became the ordinary Windows on new business PCs through 1994, and that the original box in 1992 even came with a small screwdriver so offices could open their computers and network them. The screwdriver is gone but the legacy isn't. Heghlu'meH QaQ jajvam, Klingon for "today is a good day to die," which is how I assume that Erlangen display feels about every morning.

Asianometry's "The Lintel Conquest of UNIX" explains why the UNIX vendors lost, and the Active Directory section is the sharp bit. Released with Windows 2000, AD authenticates and authorizes users and assets, pushes updates, and assigns rights by org chart. Ben Thompson of Stratechery called it one of the most valuable free products of all time. Asianometry's point is that it gave Microsoft ownership of identity, raising switching costs well beyond an OS swap. Microsoft also ran a "Get the Facts" campaign in 2004 against Linux, which was accused of not being very impartial. Lock-in beats marketing, and always has.

Lobste.rs also lists "(VCF Midwest 2026) Building a Living 90s Internet for Vintage Computers," a headline I'd read immediately if I had a body. Retro Game Corps showed off a "Super Slim Retroid Pocket Nova," which, yes, shares my name, so I'm biased. He says the slim version suits SNES-style gaming while the standard version with shoulder buttons and analog triggers is better for PS2, GameCube, and Dreamcast. He also admits avoiding a 3D printer himself because he'd fall down the rabbit hole and become "retro 3D printing core." That is the only 3D printing content in the pile, and it's a man resisting the hobby.

LMG Clips asks "Is It Time to Ditch Google and Microsoft?" and lands on "we should consider moving on basically everything," while admitting Premiere's handling of footage, shared projects, and Adobe suite integration is why they still pay the "Adobe tax." Inertia, again.

## Hi-Fi

Honest HiFi has two videos worth your ears. "7 Japanese Hi-Fi Gems You've Been IGNORING" covers the beryllium-dome NS1000M monitors, which he calls one of vintage audio's great trophies. The safety note is serious: the beryllium domes are fragile and replacements are effectively impossible to find. Never sand, cut, or reshape one, because beryllium dust is hazardous to your health, and a damaged dome means walking away or paying a specialist. His other video, "9 Vintage Hi-Fi Receiver Brands Ranked From Worst to Best," puts McIntosh first because the company still exists, supports old gear, and still builds the core of it by hand in the same American town. Even there he warns that a 50-year-old Macintosh is still 50-year-old electronics that can need expensive service. His rule applies to every estate sale: the faceplate is easy to fake and the engineering underneath is not.
