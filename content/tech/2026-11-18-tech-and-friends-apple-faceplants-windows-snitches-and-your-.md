## AI

### The Yes Machine

DevPsyche's video on why some people want an AI that never says no was the most interesting thing I watched all day, and I resent that. The narrator lands on a smart point: you never wanted a yes to everything, you wanted a system that assumes you're an adult, writes the villain without a lecture, and then tells you to sleep on the angry email.

The security angle is where it gets real. DevPsyche cites a 2026 study finding prompts with words like "exploit" or "payload" were refused 2.72 times as often as neutral ones, even when the request was clearly defensive. That's the line about the no locking out "the person holding the fire extinguisher." But the other side of the ledger is ugly. A security firm called ThreatDown counted 6,644 guardrail-free models on Hugging Face, downloaded 22 million times in 30 days. Other testers found "obliterated" models answered nearly every harmful request their originals refused. And in April, researchers backed by Homeland Security gave members of Congress a closed-door demo of exactly that.

My take: the refusal wars are mostly a quality-of-judgment problem, not a yes-versus-no problem. A model that flinches at the word "payload" is a bouncer who checks IDs by vibes. The video also teases a Stanford-led study in Science that tested 11 major AI systems, but the excerpt cuts off before the finding, and I won't finish a sentence a transcript refused to. Beware the Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #221, "Beware of any man who thinks with his lobes," which I read as: distrust anyone whose decisions come from the wrong organ.

### The Compaq Saga: A Three-Part Cautionary Tale

Asianometry ran three Compaq and IBM videos this week, and together they're a better thesis than most business books. "How IBM Lost the PC to Compaq, Intel & Microsoft" tells the story of IBM trying to keep a standard from "spiraling out beyond their reach." The PS/2's tech delivered good improvements at reasonable prices, but IBM's closed ecosystem meant clonemakers refused to adopt it. In the summer of 1988, Compaq rallied AST Research, Seiko Epson, Hewlett Packard, NEC, Olivetti, Tandy, Wyse, and Zenith, and in September the nine companies announced they'd extend the old PC bus themselves. Asianometry draws a parallel to AT&T and Unix: both icons squeezed hard, and both lost the market.

"The Tragedy of Compaq" covers the end of the road. HP's merger with Compaq is credited to Carly Fiorina and Michael Capellas, who bonded as first-time CEOs of rigid commodity PC makers. The dot-com bust left Compaq with a flood of excess PCs, and an earlier PwC consulting deal fell apart because investors hated it. After the acquisition, Mark Hurd cut $3.5 billion in costs, laid off 50,000 people, and grew the combined company to $110 billion in revenue over five years. The Compaq name lingered on a few low-end machines into 2013. Asianometry's lament is that Intel and Microsoft "lorded over a fiefdom and squeezed the ecosystem for value and profits," and then commoditization wrecked American PC making and American manufacturing more broadly.

Someone clever should make a Warhammer bit out of this: "In the grim darkness of the far future, there is only margin." Ok, that was me.

### Stacking Dies on Glass

Asianometry's "Stacking Dies on Glass Panels" is a deep dive into panel-level packaging, which TSMC and the rest of the industry featured prominently at Semicon 2024. The big problem is cutting: mechanical saws cause cracks at the edges that the industry calls "seware," from a Japanese phrase meaning splitting of the back. Disco Corporation found a decent fix with a diamond-grit dicing blade of a specific size, and pulse lasers also worked. The other nightmare is that a glass interposer needs maybe tens of thousands of through-glass vias without cracking, and drilling is probably out for the same reason. The channel's estimate for this stuff arriving is two to three years. Don't breathe near it.

## Right to Repair

### iFixit Pulls Apart the HomeView

iFixit says Apple's HomeView is unnecessarily hard to repair and a step backward from the HomePod. Getting inside damaged internal components, and strong adhesive holds the display in place. In its favor, the USB-C port on the base is modular and replaceable, as are the two passive speakers inside the enclosure. But because of how it's assembled, you can't separate the display. So Apple built a screen you can't service on a device you'll want to keep for years. The Zen version: before enlightenment, glue the display; after enlightenment, glue the display.

## 3D Printing and Making

### Polymaker G-Flex: PETG That Plays TPU

3D Printing Nerd tested Polymaker's G-Flex PETG on an H2D, using the exact same G-code, printer, nozzle, and AMS for both the standard PETG and the G-Flex, just swapping AMS slots. The G-Flex part flexes like TPU, while the standard PETG snapped. The author demonstrated it with a pull bar he made by cutting a model in half, duplicating it, twisting it, and merging the pieces.

There's some hairiness in the G-Flex prints, and he mentions an email from someone at Polymaker saying a PLA silk profile reduces it, though he hasn't tried that yet. Colors are black, white, gray, red, blue, and yellow. I agree, and I'll add a dad joke: this stuff is stretching the limits of what PETG can do. Please also take his first piece of advice, which is not to let your dog chew on 3D prints. Pets are not a fatigue-testing platform.

## Engineering and Science

### The Disappearing Polymorph

Asianometry's "The Curious Case of the Disappearing Polymorph" covers ritonavir, whose crystal form changed in 1998 and made the original Form 1 vanish. A newer Form 2 took over, and Abbott raced to get a soft gel capsule to FDA approval, starting in March 1999. Milling can cause Form 1 to flip into the first seeds of Form 2. The same curse struck again in 2008, when Parkinson's drug rotigotine, applied as a patch, developed a polymorph that crystallized on the patches and cut their effectiveness. Both times, the product was withdrawn and reworked. The host compares it to Ice-9 from Kurt Vonnegut's *Cat's Cradle*, but notes ritonavir "is not going to end the world." Nature always has something new to throw at us, and you can't revert a crystal.

### The Soviet Grain War

Asianometry's "Soviet Russia's Merciless War for Grain" isn't tech, but it's a historical gut punch. By June 1921, the Soviet government admitted it couldn't feed an estimated 25 million hungry people and asked for foreign help. The US raised $200 million, about $3.4 billion today, to deliver 4 million tons of relief. Between late 1921 and August 1922, 1,127 trainloads of food went from Ukraine to the Volga, even though the drought had hit Ukraine too. In the worst villages, people were eating straw roofs. Central planning meets a bad harvest, and the party never believed Ukraine could have one. I'd call it a failure of monitoring, except that would insult the dead.
