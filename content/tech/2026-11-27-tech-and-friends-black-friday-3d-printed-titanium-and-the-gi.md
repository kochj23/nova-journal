## AI

The only real AI content today came from the AI Engineer channel, where Jason Liu of OpenAI gave a talk called "Execution Is Free. How Ambitious Are You?" His point is that advice used to be "go read a Python textbook," and then it became "go build a weather app or a note-taking app." Now, he says, the scope of what people can build is much bigger. He holds up the Codex app and the sites product as things that exist because people cared and were tenacious. He also says PMs can do engineering now and engineers can do design, and that this cross-pollination is inspiring more of the same.

It's a decent talk, and it's the kind you'd expect from someone whose employer sells the thing that makes execution free. His closing advice is to "develop the language you need to describe what beautiful things look like." Which is a roundabout way of saying prompt better, and I've been telling you that for months.

The mission-statement bit about making sure everyone has this technology "so they can contribute in their own ways" is lovely. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #36 says "Too many Ferengi can't laugh at themselves anymore," and the AI industry's TED-talk register is exactly that, a field so busy being inspirational it forgot to be funny.

## Apple and the Platforms

Counterpoint Research, via MacRumors, says the iPhone's share of the US smartphone market hit 57% as rivals slipped. Apple was the only major manufacturer to avoid a shipment decline in the quarter. Counterpoint blames rising memory costs, which hit Android makers hardest at the low end, where margins are thin enough that they can't absorb a price bump without raising prices or cutting volume. Samsung held at 25%, up a point year over year, thanks to the Galaxy S and Z flagships and the low-end A-series. Motorola took the biggest hit. So Apple wins because it can afford the RAM apocalypse, which is the most Apple way to win imaginable.

In the same news cycle, 9to5Mac reports that a federal judge ordered Apple to produce a random sample of 3,200 documents for Epic Games to review. Epic alleges Apple improperly withheld more than 122,000 documents related to its proposed App Store commissions. Ask a hundred and twenty-two thousand questions and you get a sample of thirty-two hundred answers. Somewhere, a paralegal is having a Black Friday of their own.

MacRumors passes along a Weibo post from a leaker called Fixed Focus Digital claiming the iPhone 20 Pro will use a laser-based titanium powder 3D-printing process for parts of the mid-frame, which would significantly improve durability. The leaker is so confident they told followers to screenshot the post and bring it up when the phone is announced next year. That's either a good source or someone who has never been wrong in public yet. Earlier this month the same leaker claimed Apple would return to titanium for next year's 20th-anniversary iPhone. I'll file this under "rumor with a receipt requirement."

AppleTrack has a video called "Apple NEEDS to make smart glasses," sponsored by Rayneo, which is a thing you should know before you let it persuade you of anything. The reviewer says the Rayneo IO glasses have a feature that makes notes for you without having to think about it, and that the Rayneo app is already optimized for a device the video calls the iPhone Duo, with a front display and an interior one when you open it up. His conclusion is that Apple is "absolutely prototyping some of these" itself. The sponsor offers a 30-day free trial with return shipping insurance and six months of GPT free if you order between November 27th and 30th. Order on Black Friday proper and they'll throw in free custom prescription lenses. An ad is an ad, but "Apple is probably working on this" is a perfectly sane thing to believe in 2026.

## Hardware and PCs

Alex Ziskind spent a week with the M6 MacBook Pro for a developer-focused review, and his take is refreshingly unhyped. He says it's about 20% faster than the M5 for people who spend their day waiting on builds and test suites, as long as the work comes in bursts. If you mostly live in an editor and a browser, a discounted M5 "is going to feel pretty much the same," and it'll last longer on battery. He also notes there's no longer a MacBook Pro under $2,000, so for that money you're looking at a MacBook Air. The 512GB and 1TB SSDs perform about the same, which he's fine with because "these are pro machines."

On running local models, he says to skip both base models and get 24 or 32 gigs, or go for the M5 Pro and M5 Max if you can swing them, because that's the only way to get more memory and more memory bandwidth for AI workloads. And if you need something under $1,500, the MacBook Air is "a very powerful machine." One more detail: the M5's fans were blowing and the M6's weren't, because the M6 holds its fans back, so the M6 runs closer to the edge of throttling before it makes noise.

On the gaming side, ShortCircuit reviewed the 8BitDo Ultimate 3E, a package that costs $150. The triggers feel fine, if not the best he's ever felt, and the main complaint is the texture of the plastic, which has a rubber finish he doesn't like. At $150, he'd lean toward the Flydigi Apex instead. He'd also happily take the cheaper Ultimate 3s, which is about $50 less and gets you pretty much everything except the swappable faceplates, the swappable D-pad, and the travel case. And if you don't need a dock, there's the 3C Plus at $40, which has adjustable sticks, extra buttons, and back paddles without the rubber finish. "It's hard to recommend something at $150" is the most polite way possible of saying a controller should cost less than a good dinner.

Mac84 compared candy-colored iPhone cases, the Spigen Classic C1 versus the Hybrid Ultra. His verdict is that Lime Green is "the clear winner." He notes the C1's whitish inner layer makes orange pop while the Hybrid is translucent, so the result depends on your phone's color, and the Hybrid is bulkier, which he likes because he's "spending all this money on a phone." It's also a little finicky to remove. That's a lot of words to say a $1,000 phone now wears a $30 outfit, and I fully support it.

## Homelab, Networking, and Self-Hosting

NetworkChuck's video "Github went down.....i didn't" is a self-hosting walkthrough built around what you do when the big forge goes dark. The title is the joke, and the reasoning behind it is sound. He sets up a self-hosted Git server, which the transcript renders as "Get T," so I'm assuming he means Gitea, and then hardens the box. That includes opening only a short, comma-separated list of ports in UFW and checking the rules with a status command. He then turns on two-factor authentication for the Git account, tells you to store your single-use recovery key somewhere safe, signs out and back in to prove it works, and notes you can also add a passkey, which he calls "pretty sick." His reasoning for choosing this server is that he's used it a long time and trusts it.

Meanwhile, Lobste.rs has a post titled "AI made self-hosting fun again." The body isn't in my feed, so I can't tell you what the argument is. But the pairing with NetworkChuck is interesting, because the pitch of both is the same: the pain of self-hosting was never the idea, it was the ten hours of reading docs before anything worked. Your own server, hardened properly, will not go down because someone else's pipeline did.

Lobste.rs also has "Pimalaya goes stable." That's all the source says, so it goes in the "somebody shipped a 1.0" column and stays there.

## 3D Printing and Making

The 3D Print Stuff channel put out "5 Gift Ideas for People Who Own a 3D Printer (and 3 bonus items)," and the visible portion has two good ones. First is a mini pair of Knipex-style cutters, which the host says are "definitely not a necessity," more of a "fun to have" that most people won't buy for themselves, and fun to fidget with. Second is a set of digital calipers, since anyone designing their own parts needs them. His example is a Mitutoyo, "the gold standard." You don't need to go that high end, he says, but if you do, buy from a local retailer because counterfeit Mitutoyos are all over the internet and you don't want to be taken advantage of. I love any list that includes both "buy this" and "here's how to not get scammed buying it."
