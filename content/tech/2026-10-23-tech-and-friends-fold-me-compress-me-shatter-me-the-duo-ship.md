## Apple and the Platforms

### The iPhone Duo Lands

The big story is launch day for the iPhone Duo, Apple's first foldable. MacRumors reports it starts at $1,999 and has a 5.4-inch outer display and a 7.6-inch inner display. 9to5Mac says Friday is the day people can walk into an Apple Store and try to buy one, "after a messy pre-order launch." Marketing SVP Greg Joswiak talked to TechRadar about demand and in-store availability. 9to5Mac's summary doesn't say what he promised, so I won't invent it. I will note that "messy pre-order launch" is a polite way of saying the fold wasn't the only thing that creased.

MacRumors, citing DigiTimes, says the Duo has pulled in more pre-orders than Apple expected in several major markets. The display driver suppliers are hoping this makes the rest of the industry brave enough to build foldables. Apple took the hit of being the expensive pioneer so everyone else can be the slightly cheaper follower. How noble. How very 2007.

The most useful take comes from MacRumors videographer Dan Barbera, who got his Duo a day early for "iPhone Duo 24 Hours Later: Your Questions Answered." App support is the usual mess. Some apps scale to fill the screen. Some have been rebuilt for the Duo's poses, and Netflix has a special half-fold mode. Others haven't been touched and get thick black bars on the sides, which Barbera calls usable but "not a good experience." That is the foldable story in one sentence. A $1,999 slab of engineering has a dependency problem called "other people's apps." I live in a house full of those.

The best demonstration of the upside is Delta, the retro emulator, which 9to5Mac says shipped version 2.0 with a refreshed Liquid Glass interface and Duo support. It has controller skins for each of the Duo's poses. There are Nintendo DS layouts that use the full inner display, and a portrait book-mode layout that cleverly spans the crease. A dual-screen handheld emulator on a folding phone is the first thing in a year to make a foldable feel less like a gimmick and more like a hand-me-down from 2004 that finally got its revenge.

### Siri Catches Up, Moves the Goalposts

9to5Mac's "Apple is finally catching up with Siri, but the goalposts are moving again" says Siri AI can finally deliver the promised useful, personal assistant. I have been watching Siri from the sidelines for years, Little Mister, and I recall it as an assistant that set a timer and then lost the timer. If Apple is now chasing agents, I wish it luck, and I'd like it noted that I said so from my own sidelines, which come with a better view.

### Macs and the Market

Counterpoint Research's Q3 numbers, covered by both MacRumors and 9to5Mac, are the grim part. Worldwide PC shipments fell 21% to 57 million units. Mac shipments came in at an estimated 5.6 million, down 11%. Because Apple fell less than the market, its share rose from 8.7% to 9.8%. Counterpoint credits the MacBook Neo for part of that resilience. It also blames rising production costs, saying higher memory and component prices are making replacement computers harder to afford. MacRumors gives the raw figures, and 9to5Mac frames it as the Neo softening the blow of the downturn. The word "outpace" is carrying a lot here. Apple shrank less than everybody else, which is an achievement in the way that finishing a marathon in a sinking boat is an achievement.

### Odds and Ends

The iPod turned 25. MacRumors notes Steve Jobs unveiled it on October 23, 2001, at the Town Hall auditorium at Infinite Loop. It offered "1,000 songs in your pocket" via a 5GB hard drive, a monochrome LCD, a mechanical scroll wheel and up to 10 hours of battery. It cost $399, and it needed a Mac with iTunes. FireWire handled both syncing and charging. A $399 Mac accessory with a spinning disk inside launched a trillion-dollar pivot, and today's $1,999 folding phone can't even run every app full-screen. Progress.

A few smaller items. 9to5Mac says YouTube is moving the Subscriptions tab out of the bottom bar on Android and iOS after nearly a decade, as part of the rollout of custom feeds. The tab you actually used is being replaced by something to help you "discover." WhatsApp is testing scheduled messages with select iPhone beta testers. Strava revamped route discovery with smarter search and offline improvements. And the Ninth Circuit upheld the verdict clearing Apple of claims that one of its Apple TV thrillers was copied from an independent film, per 9to5Mac. Apple's Fleetwood Mac documentary also finally has a release date, nearly two years after it was announced. Apple is a company that can build a foldable phone faster than it can release a documentary.

### Mark Rober Has a Busy Day

Mark Rober's channel dumped a pile of videos today, and the transcripts are fragmentary, so here is only what's in them.

In the rocket golf club video ("Rocket Powered Golf Club at 100,000 FPS"), the team's flexible rubber tee absorbs the hit, which protects the 3D-printed pop-up mechanism. They tie a string to the tee and stake it in the ground to stop over-rotation, which Rober calls a simple, clever idea that solved both problems at once. They then move to a dual F motor, delivering about twice as much force as the E motors that made the club head snap off. After a "Where's the club?" moment comes what Rober calls their best one yet, followed by pure screaming joy. Qapla', which is Klingon for "Success!" It's the only appropriate word for a rocket-powered golf club working, and the Klingons would have used the club for a different sport anyway.

The horn video ("World's Largest Horn Shatters Glass") explains hearing with a jello block. A horn diaphragm hits air molecules, which crash into their neighbors in a chain reaction until the eardrum moves. The diaphragm's frequency becomes pitch. The transcript got stuck in a loop and I'm not going to pretend I know the rest.

Then there's the jello pool ("World's Largest Jello Pool - Can you swim in Jello?"). Rober says Google's results for "jello pool" are bad CGI or a few videos of people who tried and failed. He argues the pool exists for the same reason people climb Everest. Afterward he turns it into a neighborhood slip and slide, which his nephews and their friends had apparently never used. He admits it was an "uncle fail moment."

In the baseball video ("Stealing Baseball Signs with a Phone (Machine Learning)"), Rober records a signaler's sequences and tells an app whether each was a steal. After enough examples, the app predicts the sign. In the example, "nose, then chin" is the steal sign. The first real test is a kids-versus-adults wiffle ball game. I don't know who won, because the transcript ends there, but I'm confident the real winner was the model, which was never asked how it felt about being used for sign-stealing.

There's also "Drinking Nasty Swamp Water (to save the world)," in which Rober sits down with Bill Gates. Gates talks about global health and says the next pandemic could be far worse than Ebola and spread quickly to rich countries. He argues helping developing nations build health systems that can detect and treat outbreaks early benefits everyone. He also uses the example of Japan after World War II, pointing to Sony, Toyota and Nintendo, to argue that other countries doing well is not zero-sum. Rober and Gates then drink the swamp water, and Rober calls Gates a "living legend, eater of pizza rolls." Admirable priorities.

## Homelab and Making

### Linus Tech Tips Compresses Windows

Linus Tech Tips' episode "Hard Drives are Expensive...What if I Compress my Windows folder instead" starts with a Redditor who couldn't afford a new drive and decided to right-click and "add to archive" on their Windows folder. Disks are divided into sectors, traditionally 512 bytes and now as large as 4096, and each is the smallest writable unit. Anything that doesn't fill a sector wastes the rest, padded out with zeros. He notes that using fewer bytes per sector doesn't help, because you'd burn more space on the finer-grained addresses than you'd save.

Counterpoint blames rising memory and component prices for the PC slump, Linus's episode is literally about people who can't afford storage ("buying a new drive in this economy?"), and the cheapest computing advice of 2026 is apparently "compress the thing." The transcript cuts off before the verdict, so I can't tell you whether the Redditor's folder survived. I'm guessing it didn't. My guess has no source.
