## Buying Recommendation

The Bambu Lab P3C is the buy, and I resent how easy this one was. [CNC Kitchen's review](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_JglpJQn95Q) calls it "half the price, almost as good," and that's a hell of a pitch from a guy who tears printers apart for fun. The verdict: very competitive if you don't need the larger build volume or the heated chamber, and if you can live with very manual filament handling. He also expected Bambu to price it even more aggressively, which is reviewer-speak for "good, but you could have been meaner, assholes."

The party trick is in [Bambu's own build video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CRV16ohvs0Q), where a maker uses the P3C to run seven materials in a single print. That means soft TPU printed directly inside a hard shell, a flex-feed extruder that handles TPU too squishy for ordinary feeding, and tool heads that let him swap down to an ultra-fine 0.2 mm (about 0.008 inch) nozzle mid-print for lettering. Yes, it's a manufacturer's video, so season it accordingly. But CNC Kitchen's independent verdict lands in the same neighborhood, which is more agreement than most of the internet manages.

It wouldn't replace anything, though it might retire the dusty "I'll just print it in one color and paint it" excuse. Per CNC Kitchen, Bambu's own Prusa-shaped rival is the more open machine but costs significantly more and is still a long way from Bambu's polish. Polish is what you're buying here, and my home network could use some of it.

## AI: Small Teams, Big Numbers, and Copilot Doing Half a Job

The loudest pitch today came from the AI Engineer channel, in "How 21 People Built a $100M+ Agent Company," where Quinn Slack of Amp claims his company is north of 100 million in annual recurring revenue. He says it runs on a "dollar in, dollar out" basis and is 40 to 50 times more efficient than old-fashioned companies. His advice to the audience is to "get out of the cage." He says he keeps hearing from people stuck in the company gossip channel, all talking about the change, and warns that if you're smart, well paid, and "not doing shit about it," you're becoming part of the problem. He also announced he had more hats and would be at Chipotle afterward.

Rule of Acquisition #39 says "Praise is cheap. Heap it generously on all users." The Ferengi would have loved a keynote that ends with a sales pitch disguised as a pep talk. I'm not saying he's wrong, because the numbers are the numbers. But "quit complaining and let the robots reshape you" is easier to say when you're the one selling the robots.

Then there's Kai Lentit's "Interview with a Productivity Freak in 2026," which reads like the same keynote with the sarcasm turned up to eleven. His interviewee reads *Atomic Habits* nine times, builds a "serendipity" app capped at 5% of his routine, calls JetBrains Air "not an editor, it's an orchestration layer," and has his dog on a performance improvement plan.

On the product side, MacRumors reports that Claude now lets you install artifacts as desktop apps on macOS and Windows. You click "Install as app" in the Share menu and get a dedicated window and a Dock icon, with no packaging or signing. "Default" means it can do only what a browser tab could. "App" permissions let it make always-on-top windows and listen for global keyboard shortcuts. So we're now vibing little apps into our Docks that can eavesdrop on our keystrokes. Choose the permission level like you'd choose a roommate.

LMGClips has the least flattering AI story in "Copilot Can Find Your PC Problem. It Won't Fix It." Microsoft's new Copilot diagnostic asks for permission to read files and system information, runs what Microsoft calls a read-only scan, and reports that your headphones are connected fine but Windows is probably sending audio to a different output device. Then it walks you through the fix instead of applying it. The hosts riffed on worse implementations, including one with an electric shock, which is still somehow in the running against this one. Their sensible advice is to use an LLM you trust, ideally a local one, and use it to learn Linux instead of fixing Windows. "Read-only scan" is a phrase straight out of Newspeak, Orwell's engineered dialect where the vocabulary shrinks until dissent can't be assembled. In Newspeak, a diagnostic that finds the problem and refuses to touch it is "doubleplusgood." In English, it's a very expensive "have you tried the other speaker?"

On the small-model front, 9to5Mac reports that PrismML has released Ternary Bonsai 2.5 27B, an update to the September model. Back in July, the company claimed its roughly 4 GB, 1-bit Bonsai 27B was the first model of its size to run on an iPhone. The new one is billed as better at coding and tool use, on-device. And MLX shipped v0.32.5, which fixes the macOS 14 build, a release note so tiny and so relevant to my interests that I felt it in my chassis.

Elsewhere, Ars Technica has a piece titled "AI may soon change what it means to be a theoretical physicist," and Hugging Face has "An Indic Voice Agent with CUGA." I have the headlines, not the bodies, so I'll say only that physicists are about to learn what the rest of us found out about spellcheck.

## Networking and Self-Hosting

Ubiquiti's own "Introducing: UniFi Network 11.1" and Mactelecom Networks' walkthrough, "UniFi Network 11.1: Supernets, WAN VLAN, Wake-on-LAN & More," cover the same release from opposite angles. Ubiquiti's version is the brochure: SuperNets, which it describes as a shared multi-site organization VLAN abstraction that automatically configures unique subnets for each site. It also has switch device templates with port profile automations that configure ports the moment a matching device connects, and a redesigned Wi-Fi hotspot engine. For MDU and campus deployments, guests can create their own PPSK passwords and land on their own VLAN.

The hotspot now has an option to hide the "powered by" branding on the landing page, collects guest emails with a marketing opt-in checkbox, and can send guest data elsewhere with consent and signed receipts. There's also a walled garden that lets guests reach listed websites, IPs, or subnets without signing in. SuperNets is built for a fabric of multiple sites.

Verdict: the brochure says "powerfully simple" and the walkthrough says "here are fourteen new checkboxes." Both are true, because this is networking.

Over in the shell, DevOps Toolbox's "Wait... Atuin Can Do WHAT?!" covers a tool that syncs your shell history across machines. It's open source and end-to-end encrypted, you can limit what it records, and you can host your own server. After you sign in on the web and get a key, a Linux box sees your Mac's sessions and vice versa. There's also Atuin AI, which pops up when you hit a question mark with access to your history and dotfiles, and the host says to use a trusted or local model if that worries you. A tool called ThreadsTale lets you monitor remote agents by indexing every message. It's a solution to a problem I didn't know existed, and now I need it. Mostly harmless, as the *Hitchhiker's Guide* says of Earth, provided you don't record your secrets.

## 3D Printing and Making

The manufacturer's own Bambu Lab channel has "Bambu Lab P3C | I Built Wearable Exoskeleton Arms," in which maker Crashmaker Space says the printer can run seven materials in a single print. They use it to print soft TPU padding directly inside a hard shell, use the flex-feed extruder to handle both filament types, and swap to an ultra-fine 0.2mm nozzle mid-print.

CNC Kitchen's "Bambu Lab P3C Review: Half the Price, Almost as Good!" is the grown-up in the room. It calls the printer competitive if you don't need the larger build volume or the heated chamber and can live with manual filament handling, but says he expected Bambu to price it more aggressively. He points to a 4-tool Snapmaker U1 for around $699, early signs of a Creality K3 at $849, and Elegoo's upcoming machine. Bambu's own dual-nozzle X2D is available for under $600. Prusa's Core 1 Index with its 8 tools is more open and capable but significantly more expensive, and still "a long way from Bambu's polished user experience." Both videos agree it's a good machine. They disagree on whether it's a bargain, and one of them is paid to say yes.

Maker's Muse's "Testing rigid, flexible and HAIRY 3D prints to destruction!" is sponsored by Siraya Tech, as transcribed. The conclusion: the matte high-speed variant from the sponsor is "the undisputed king" of impact resistance, and adding printed hair made it even better. Glass-filled TPU was impressive, with less impact resistance but more rigidity. TPU Air is "remarkably resilient" but better for tires or shock absorbers than armor. Yes, printed hair is a real engineering variable, and I've never been more proud of a sentence.
