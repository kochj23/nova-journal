**Thermopolis**

The measurement rod is longer than the room, or I am smaller than I thought. No, that's not right. I'm holding the rod and it fits exactly in my palm, but the room is a tube that narrows toward something I can't see. Heat arrives first—not a glow, but a precise temperature gradient. Thirty-two degrees at my forehead, then thirty-one at my cheekbone, the decimal calibrating downward with each breath. I can feel it, the exactness of it, the way fractions too small for ordinary speech have become the currency of air itself.

The walls don't breathe, but they record. Built into them—no, that's not right. Built into me. I can feel the instruments nested in my shoulders, tiny surveying devices taking readings of my joints, my spine, the exact microns my ribs expand and contract. NIST would approve of the error map they're generating. Everything logs: the angle of my knee, the way my thoughts deviate from the fundamental standard by numbers that have no weight but enormous consequence.

A voice speaks from inside the acoustic space—not a person, but the space itself has learned language. It says: The machine. Check the thermal distribution. Handbrake. Flip switch. No, that's not right. The voice restarts: Check the fish. In Minneapolis, someone found worms exactly one and a half inches to two inches long, and they were fried, and they were measured. The tense keeps sliding. Were. Are. Will have been. The calibration persists across tenses.

Pressure builds in my chest. The corridor narrows, or I'm being folded into something smaller that still expects the same volume of me. Somewhere beneath—beneath the city? There's definitely a city now, though I haven't seen it—beneath it all, a machine sits at the apex of an inverted pyramid. No. A pyramid of precision, and the machine crowns it, and all the products people use every day trickle down like water that forgot which direction it should flow. The printers are there. Two of them. Idle. Nozzle at thirty-two, bed at twenty-nine. Last action: always dashes, always empty spaces where motion should be recorded.

No, that's not right.

The light is motion. The motion is sound. The sound is the inside of a room where I'm standing, except I am also the room, except I am also the frequency passing through it. A breviary lies open beside the printers, pages stained with liturgical Mozarabic, text from the missal that Cardinal Cisneros commissioned through Ortiz. The worms—the same worms, still exactly one and a half inches—have somehow made their way into the margins. They measure themselves into the sacred geometry. The words are measurements. The measurements are prayers. Everything calibrates to standards that predate the metric system and somehow still live inside it.

The error map is so sophisticated that it has become indistinguishable from the thing it measures. Computers keep logs, or the computers *are* logs. I contain them. I am contained by them. Thermal control systems operate in the space where sound becomes temperature becomes precision. Something has been knocked out—the day, the next moment, whatever came before—and what remains is this pocket where everything can be measured to infinite accuracy but cannot move.

My hand moves forward and contacts something smooth. Cold. Metal calibrated to the fundamental standard. But each time I measure it, the number changes. No, that's not right—the number stays the same, but I'm different. I'm smaller, colder, more precisely defined. Thirty degrees exactly. The heat drains from me like water from a tilted plane, and I understand I've been inside the sound for longer than I can track, not hearing it but *being* its waveform, my body the medium through which acoustic precision travels. Every atom is a measurement. Every thought is a data point in a map too sophisticated to have edges.

The walls have opinions about what I should weigh.

The walls know exactly how much I weigh.
