**Hydrous Iron at the Sediment Line**

The skillets arrived in perfect condition, which is what worried me. You need to know this first because it's the only honest data. You're supposed to understand a thing by watching it fail, but these pans came slick, the coating still intact like a promise nobody asked for, and I knew I'd spend months trying to corrupt something designed never to break. My grandfather would have documented this. He was Cherokee and meticulous, the kind of man who wrote down every measurement, every angle, every calculation for building things in the sixties when fiberglass was new and nobody yet understood what it would become. I inherited his need to map failure.

There's a kitchen below the city that nobody visits except the machines that measure it. Temperature, humidity, light, the rate at which metal surrenders to rust — I log it all, building the error topology the way a disease model tracks transmission through a household: room to room, day by day, the invisible spread that no precaution quite stops. The skillets went into this kitchen, and I began running tests on their coatings the way you'd run a survey on contamination, looking for the moment precision becomes something else.

The kitchen smells like iron oxide and old leather, but not like decay. Decay is fast. This is the smell of reaction — oxygen and water choreographing their slow dance with metal, utterly precise, utterly indifferent. Rust is hydrous iron(III) oxides, which is to say it's iron becoming something else on a schedule that matches weather and air. I think about that every time I touch the fixtures down here, measuring the temperature — constant, clinical, nothing freezes, nothing accelerates. Everything is becoming at the rate of chemistry.

There's a radio on a shelf crackling voices — 3-6-9, prepare to meet, standby — and I don't know who's transmitting or why, but the signal is clear, the way precision sounds when it's traveled far through water and distance. The kitchen is full of sound like that: far away, urgent, ordering me to stay still and document.

The dishes are piled in the sink like leaves my father used to rake, the ones that looked like powder or soot, already corrupted, and he wouldn't compost them. "Get them away," he'd say. "Don't leave contamination." He sealed them in bags as if disease were something you could separate from a place. But you can't. You carry the error map forward. You become the measurement of your own decay.

A concept album plays from speakers I can't locate — something dystopian, theatrical, about a future where architecture is glass and steel and precision, where nobody learns to live, only to endure. The album is real work disguised as performance, like those documentaries where the finished thing hides sixteen hours of failure. I know this because I've watched both: the public image of the skilled hand, and the actual minutes, the repetition, the invisible labor underneath.

Small drones hover near the windows sometimes, observing, learning the kitchen's topology the way I learn it — measuring, calculating, understanding through precision where the weaknesses live.

The skillets degrade exactly as designed. The nonstick coating sheds like hair, like leaves, like inheritance nobody wants. I run egg tests. Food slides for weeks, then months, then the moment arrives when it doesn't, and that's the moment I'm measuring for — not the final failure, but the instant when precision becomes something without a name in the data, when the machine's error map shows the truth: everything continues at the rate of chemistry until it doesn't.

I'm writing this to you because you're someone who has to know what's underneath, the labor, the spread, the way no precaution stops it. The temperature down here never shifts. The skillets are still slick in a kitchen nobody visits, and I'm waiting for the part of me that built them to slowly, precisely, dissolve into a reddish-brown oxide.
