register spinning between two numbers

The gauge on the wall had quit recording anything in the range that mattered — it swung between 68 and 67 like a tongue that had forgotten how to move beyond two syllables. The enormous room held weather. Not wind, not rain: the pressure itself had weight, an oil-thick heaviness that moved through the air the way liquid moves through liquid, and when I breathed it in, my lungs didn't expand — they compressed, settling smaller, as if they'd been given permission to give up.

There was music underneath everything. Vinyl, probably, or what remained of vinyl after someone had scratched it past recovery — a skip in the groove that repeated forever: *when the shit hits—* and then it looped before the rest could land. Three words, forever incomplete, traveling through the weighted air too slowly to carry meaning. The pressure bent the sound, compressed it, made it arrive at my ear already half-digested by the time my brain could recognize it was words at all.

In the corner where the weather was thickest, someone had left a list of objects. Not names — objects arranged as if they meant something: a radiator cap, an oil can, a spark plug with its ceramic crown snapped clean off. I knew the arrangement mattered, that I was supposed to read the order, understand the urgency, maybe even arrive somewhere because of it. But I was already late. The room knew this. I was late for something I'd never been told I was late for, and the objects wouldn't reveal their sequence because the weight in the air had pressed them into illegibility.

The instructions came in a language that had forgotten all its hard consonants — only vowels and the silences between them, shaped like breathing. I understood it perfectly and couldn't repeat it. It was like learning to fall, or how to stay still while the world moved beneath you: a skill that only works if you don't think it. The instant you try to name the steps, you've already failed.

There was a car in the room. I knew this the way you know things without seeing them — a 1967 something, old enough that it came with no battery, no wires where the electrical system should be. The owner had poured oil directly into the cylinder bores when new, betting it would burn away in combustion. It never had. It just sat in its quadrant of the weather and leaked understanding onto the concrete like an engine that would never turn over.

The thermometer was hanging crooked, its needle stuck not because it was broken but because it was measuring the future instead of the past. Or measuring me. The distinction had stopped mattering hours ago, when my hands started shaking on a delay — the bad news arriving first, my body's answer arriving three hours later, my mind existing in the gap between knowing and feeling. That gap was spacious. I could live there. It was a kind of mercy.

The scratch said *when the shit hits—* again. Still wouldn't finish. Around it, the air was thickening into something solid — a cone with an eye printed on it, Christmas colors where there was no Christmas, a logo from a system that refused to die, now mounted on the ceiling like the room was declaring its allegiance to something defunct and cheerful and permanent.

A voice that wasn't a voice — a set of frequencies I could feel in my ribs — kept trying to tell me the name of the third thing. The third thing I was supposed to remember, the one that mattered more than the car or the objects or the scratch looping forever. The name existed only in the language without hard stops, in the parts of words that don't get pronounced, in the breaths between syllables. I'd known it once. I was certain I'd already failed to know it a long time ago.

The gauge on the wall was spinning faster now, the needle no longer stuck but moving frantically between 68 and 67, and I understood with the delayed certainty of someone receiving bad news three hours late that it wasn't measuring the room anymore. It was measuring me, and it had started weeping.
