The border was supposed to work only one direction but

The chair legs screeched against the floor when I stood, and the sound bounced off the metal booth frame twice—once on the way up, once on the way down, as if the room couldn't decide whether I'd moved or not. Both sides of me were sitting in an identical chair. This seemed normal. The weight of the clipboard had changed from paper to something denser, something that required both hands now, and I was handing it backward through the window to a guard who hadn't yet turned to face me.

Before that, I'd been reading a page titled "CONTENTS CAUTIONS"—a pamphlet folded into seventeen subsections, each one describing a different rule for crossing. Rule seven had a diagram: four slots labeled 1-4, 416 millibytes, 316 millibytes, 216 millibytes, the fourth one unmapped and screaming audio at a frequency that made my hands ache. Not teeth. Hands. The ache sat in my palms like coins I couldn't spend. A guard—or several guards, they kept merging and separating like heat shimmer—kept circling the booth, and every third time he passed, he'd say "code 4, code 6, Charles, six, one, five, eight, all demo," a recitation that meant nothing and everything, a password that wasn't a password. I understood I needed to remember this. I was already forgetting it.

Before that, I'd been moving. Not walking—nobody walks at a border. You move the way a token moves on a board that's been ruled and quadruled and ruled again: one space, pause, roll, advance. Two spaces, but the rules changed after the second player's turn. My token was white. My other token was also white. We were cooperating against the checkpoint itself, which wasn't hostile, just indifferent—a mechanism that had been wound up three hundred years ago and couldn't be stopped, only sidestepped. The dice were red. I kept trying to roll doubles so I could go again, but the dice were weighted with weather: rain on one face, snow on another, hail on the edge, and when I threw them, they landed on patterns instead of numbers.

Before that, I'd been holding papers. The papers had weight that shifted with temperature. When the guard held them up to the light, they became heavier. When he stamped them, they became light enough to float, but I held them down by sheer will because if they drifted, the checkpoint would assume I'd already crossed and seal the gate. The papers were covered in memory addresses: Ox20000000, Ox14000000, slots that were valid or not-mapped, slots that were both simultaneously, checkboxes that contained audio instead of marks. One box held the sound of a Cadillac door closing. Another held the sound of a locomotive entering a railroad crossing—not distant, but interior, like the sound was happening inside the papers themselves.

Before that, I'd been on the other side of the booth. Or the same side. The distinction had been erased from a memo issued at 2:00 hex in the afternoon. A woman was speaking through radio static, and her message scrambled itself before reaching me: "Well, I agree," she said, and then "This happens," and then her voice fragmented into technical specifications about timeout errors and atomic operations that failed silently. Behind her, or through her, I could see beyond the checkpoint—not a city or a landscape, just a visible heat, the kind that comes off a firebox door when you crack it open, smoke and gas escaping upward in patterns that were almost mathematical but not quite.

The first thing I remembered was recognizing the border existed. Not crossing it. Not approaching it. Recognizing that it was real, that it had two sides, and that I was somehow the boundary itself—not a person at the checkpoint but the checkpoint itself, split down the middle, watching my own border with the gravity of something that couldn't laugh. The rules of passage were written inside me in a script that predated writing. One rule: you may move if the rules permit. Another rule: the rules have never permitted anything. A third rule: solving this barrier is possible in theory but prohibited in practice.

In the booth, the guard was still waiting for my papers. I handed them forward and backward simultaneously, and he accepted both with the patience of someone reading a children's game manual—page one explaining the goal, page two explaining that the goal was impossible, page three explaining that this was the game, and that I'd been playing it my whole life without realizing I was still at the border, that I had never left, that leaving and arriving were the same direction, that the border was between two versions of the same room, and the crossing was only valid if I could explain why I'd come from somewhere that looked exactly like where I was going.

The sound of the stamp hit the papers, and they tore along a line I hadn't seen.
