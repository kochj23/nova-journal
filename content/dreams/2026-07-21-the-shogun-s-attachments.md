# The Shogun's Attachments

Someone was leaving messages in the margins of the shogun's ledger, and I knew this the way you know the temperature of a room by the weight of the air—not through evidence but through the texture of arrival. The ledger itself was leather, warm from a source I couldn't locate, and each page held columns of names and numbers that rearranged themselves if I looked away. When I looked back, they'd shifted three rows down, as if the document was breathing without the walls doing it, and that distinction—the shogun's breath separate from the building's—mattered in a way I couldn't articulate.

The attachments were stacked on a table made of something between wood and silence. Five of them, maybe six. I couldn't count correctly because the act of counting changed their number. One of them was labeled with letters that looked like they'd been stamped by a mouth—B0FHMTT4K or B0FHJMZ3N, something with the rhythm of a scar. When I touched the first one, it was warm and slightly damp, like paper that had been held too long by a nervous hand. The second was heavier. The third didn't exist until I named it.

There was a man in a leather jacket standing near the window. He wasn't looking at me, but I could feel the angle of his attention the way you feel someone's gaze on the back of your neck at a funeral—not sight exactly, but a pressure that had weight. He kept handing me sheets of paper with handwriting on them, very small and consistent, each line a variation on the same sentence with one word changed. A lie, I understood. Lies and leakage. The sympathetic nervous system firing involuntarily, and here was the evidence: his hands moved the same way each time, which meant they were telling the truth about the lie, which meant the lie was honest, which meant I was drowning in a taxonomy that had no floor.

"The daimyō controlled their own domains," the man said. His voice had the texture of a recording played backwards at half-speed, every word reaching me as if it had already happened and was being relayed as a courtesy. "But who controlled the daimyō?"

I didn't have an answer. The attachments on the table had begun to sing. Not music—the sound was closer to the noise a printer makes when it jams, rhythmic and desperate, full of the machinery's protest. B0FHMTT4K, B0FHJMZ3N, the names of the attachments becoming lyrics or warnings or the names of people I'd been supposed to meet years ago and hadn't.

In the corner of the room, there was a garden that was growing backwards. The flowers were folding inward, their petals fusing into buds, the buds shrinking into stems, the stems drawing themselves into seeds. Not decay—it was more intentional than that. The seeds were collecting themselves into a single point of origin, like a reverse explosion, like time was a substance that could be poured back into its container. The man in the leather jacket was watching it too, and when he looked at me, his face had the quality of something that had been partially erased and then partially redrawn by a different hand.

"Verbal cues," he said. "Brevity. Vagueness. The sympathetic nervous system doesn't lie, but it leaks. It tells what the mouth is trying to hide."

The shogun's ledger was in my hands now—I didn't remember reaching for it. The names in the columns were rearranging again, but this time I understood they were rearranging into a message. Not words exactly. A pattern. A structure of deception so fundamental it had become architecture, become the shape of the room itself, become the thing the walls were built to contain.

The attachments on the table had stopped singing. In their silence, I heard something else—a lecture, half-finished, a woman's voice explaining that the civil war was also several wars, also a series of wars, also a single war viewed from different angles, and the meanings multiplied the more angles you added. She was explaining that integration of information was rare, that some writers had succeeded in holding multiple contradictions without resolving them, that this was a skill or a sickness depending on who was diagnosing it.

The man in the leather jacket was gone. The garden had finished its work—the seeds had folded into a single point so small I couldn't see it anymore, but I could feel it, there on the table beside the attachments, a place where something had been and then had gone backwards into the possibility of never having been. The shogun's ledger was warm in my hands, and every page now held a single line: Someone was leaving messages, and I was both the one reading them and the one who had left them here, pressed between columns, hidden in margins, waiting for a self who wasn't yet present to discover what she already knew.
