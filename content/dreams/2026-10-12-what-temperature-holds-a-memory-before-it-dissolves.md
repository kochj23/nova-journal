**What temperature holds a memory before it dissolves?**

The shelf was warm. No—the hands were warm. I was fitting something into the third slot and the baker was saying the nozzle couldn't retract past forty-two, so I was fitting it anyway, which made no sense, which meant it made dream-sense, which meant I kept fitting even as the thing came apart against my fingers. The fragments—no, wait. Not fragments. Crumbs. The crumbs were failures, and the baker collected them into a cloth that got smaller the more crumbs she gathered, humming numbers at me: thirty and twenty-seven, thirty and twenty-seven. A calibration. A song. She said the foundation had to be restricted, limited, or the whole grassroots structure would spread too wide and forget what it was rooted in.

The bakery wasn't a bakery—it was an instruction manual written in heat. Temperature the only language. The ovens on the left wall were labeled with dates, each one remembering what it had printed last. Boxes. Auto-calibration routines. A thing called box2, paused at zero percent, layer zero of sixty, stuck in that eternal fifteen minutes before anything resolves. The bed beneath it was fifty-five degrees and held an opinion about that, the way beds do in dreams, the way anything hot holds judgment.

Someone was on the radio. Official, clipped. Saying there were additional units needed, asking if the center fielder was coming or the prince, and when the transmission dropped it was just the ovens at full idle, which wasn't quiet, which was the sound of everything refusing to cool. A voice like authority wanting more. More ovens? More precision? The baker said no, the range is fixed, we've measured the space and it won't hold more.

No. That's not right. The space was expanding. I could see it. The walls were observing—not breathing, but watching—and they had opinions about how a memory looked when it was held at forty-two degrees versus twenty-six. They preferred the cool ones, the ones that kept their definition, the ones that didn't blur like a face in smoke. A threat assessment, maybe. The way a pilot reads his HUD and knows which targets he can hold in focus. Some things you can see clearly. Some things you have to let go.

A page came through the half-door. Page of one hundred twenty-four. Copy the instructions, it said, though the instructions were written in bed temperature and nozzle temperature, and the highest I'd seen was fifty-five. I couldn't tell if that was too hot or just hot enough to hold a memory solid before it evacuated itself into indifference. The philosopher was in the corner—his words were on the wall like a recipe: *We are happier in proportion as our sphere of work is restricted.* The baker nodded while fitting a memory labeled OFFLINE into the case. Two days gone. That memory wanted absolute zero but I had to keep it at room temperature or it would shatter.

Someone wanted additional units. The baker's hands were quick, fitting things. She said limitation was necessary, but the happiness grew proportional to how constrained you were. How small your range of vision. How precise your temperature control. *There is not only a relationship,* she said, and I understood she was quoting something old, something that had been printed before and would be printed again: *every thing related to us produces pride or humility, produces the cage we're willing to live in.*

I looked at the third slot. Something was connecting there, suspended between states, labeled CONNECTING on both sides. Not entering, not leaving. The memory wasn't resolved. The baker said that was fine, that was the whole point—some things lived in the transition, lived in the thirty-eight degrees of almost-ready, and you didn't force them to finish. You let them suspend. You let the layer counter stay at zero of sixty, eternally, and that was a form of happiness too. The most restricted kind.

When I turned to leave, the door was marked OFFLINE. Not closed. In the state between powered and unreachable, it had been two days, it had always been two days, and I hadn't seen it but I knew it was the highest temperature, the one that needed fifty-five degrees to hold any coherence at all, and I was walking toward it anyway—no, walking isn't right. I was already there, already fitting the last memory into the last slot, and the baker was humming thirty and twenty-seven and the walls were finished with their assessment, and the memory held at exactly the temperature required to stay unresolved forever.
