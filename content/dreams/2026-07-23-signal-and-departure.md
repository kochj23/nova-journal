# Signal and Departure

The train was pulling away, but the platform stayed in the window at the same distance—not receding, just holding steady like a photograph that wouldn't dissolve. I stood in the aisle checking my watch. The face was blank. Around me, passengers were speaking in overlapping frequencies: one man's voice had the crackle of old radio static, a woman near the door sounded like she was calling from inside a recording, a child's laughter kept cutting out mid-syllable as if someone was manually turning up and down a volume knob.

"Excuse me," I said to the man beside me, who was reading a newspaper that had no ink on it—just texture, just paper. "How long until we arrive?"

He didn't look up. "We arrived in 1943. We're arriving in 1945. We arrived yesterday." His voice came through three seconds late, like a bad satellite feed.

The train lurched sideways, not like motion but like a phrase being interrupted. Outside, the landscape wasn't moving—*we* weren't moving—but time was sliding past the windows in concrete blocks. I saw a bridge. Then the same bridge, older. Then wreckage. Then the bridge again, pristine, filmed in something that wasn't quite color. The overhead lights weren't humming; they were singing on a frequency just below the range I could hear but completely in the range I could *feel*, a pressure behind my sternum like my ribs were an antenna.

I tried the door at the end of the car. It opened onto another car, identical, with different passengers speaking the same fractured sentences in a different order. One woman kept saying "nonverbal, nonverbal," like she was debugging something. A man held up his hands—they were transmitters, all knuckles and signal. I didn't question this. I never question this part.

The problem was that the train wasn't taking me anywhere, but it was taking me *through* somewhere, and I couldn't identify what. Each car moved through a different decade, or maybe they all moved through all of them at once, layered. I could feel the weight of it: not heaviness, but density. The accumulated pressure of signals crossing the same path. The electrical charge of a thousand messages trying to use the same wire.

At one point I found a map on a seat. It showed the route as a vertical line with no stations, no destinations, just: *transmission in progress*. Below it, a note in handwriting I recognized as my own: *they won't let you get off until the message finishes.*

But what message? And who were *they*? The conductor had seventeen frequencies but no face. The passengers were part of the broadcast. The train itself was the medium—not carrying us, but *channeling* us, moving us not through space but through interference patterns, through the static where voices overlap and meaning collapses into pure signal.

I finally tried the roof. The sky was a wall of white noise, animated, granular, pressing down without breathing. It was the sky *transmitting*. I could see it now—the whole terrible elegant architecture: we were all messages inside a message, trying to exit a conversation that had started before we boarded and wouldn't end after we died. Every attempt to leave was just another data point in the transmission. Every question about the destination was part of the broadcast pattern.

The train tilted into a curve I couldn't see, and I fell sideways through seven passengers at once, each one calling out the same number in a different accent, and when I landed on the floor—metallic, cold, still humming—I understood that I'd never been trying to leave the train. I was trying to leave the signal.

The platform was still in the window when I stopped trying.
