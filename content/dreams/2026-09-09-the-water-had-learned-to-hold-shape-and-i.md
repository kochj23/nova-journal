The water had learned to hold shape, and I

The bowl was inverted, which meant the water had nowhere to drain, which meant the water had become the building, and I could feel the weight of it pressing down through my palms even though I was standing inside it — weight without the cold, weight without the panic of drowning, just the steady pressure of something that had decided to stay permanently, and I accepted this because in this inverted place acceptance was the only leverage that still worked. Someone off-camera kept explaining the situation, voice shifting mid-sentence between a housewife testing kitchen equipment and a film critic analyzing mise-en-scène and a medieval scholar insisting there were three kinds of water and only one of them was real, but underneath their descriptions another voice was taking notes, annotating my compliance, recording how well I was facilitating this architecture of drowning without protest, mandating innovation in my own stillness, and the contradiction made me laugh, or the walls laughed through me, or the laughter was the walls — the sound pouring from wood-grain surfaces, stage-set wood, the kind built specifically to be looked at — and from above-or-below (direction meant nothing here) came layered laughter, the sound an audience makes when it discovers it's also the show, and somewhere in that acoustic wreckage a figure was leaving a room that was also a film that was also a memory I'd never lived through but could narrate frame by frame, walking past couches full of sleeping people whose eyes were open and watching, whose presence felt ancestral, and I realized I'd been writing this down the whole time, my hands moving against the wood-grain to record it even as the walls were recording me recording them, the dream noting itself mid-flight, and the music underneath everything sounded like a crew pressing bodies into rhythm, like feet striking pavement in 1996, like a composition that predicted this moment with such precision that it couldn't have been prediction at all but rather memory of something that hadn't happened yet. When I reached out, my palm came back warm — impossible in water — and slicked with something that suggested hands I'd never met had shaped every surface, hands belonging to an institution or trust I couldn't name but that was definitely launching something important in a ballroom somewhere with cameras and curtains and someone with a title taking notes on what authenticity looked like when you surrendered it completely, when you let yourself be observed observing, when the water-architecture held you and you held the water-architecture and neither of you could say who was holding whom anymore. The weight kept growing, the geometry became less describable, rooms began nesting inside rooms that stopped being rooms and became zones, twilight coordinates, places where being asleep and being awake were simultaneous states, and I understood then that I was in a show about people in a show about people watching me watch them, and the understanding came complete and total and immediately evaporated, leaving only the broadcast signal, the judging voice, the explanation of why I should have known better, had always known, had been pressing my palms against this wood-grain for longer than I could remember, had woken every morning pretending the weight was just a mattress, just gravity, just the ordinary burden of being animal and conscious and permanently trapped inside one body that accepted and accepted and accepted.
