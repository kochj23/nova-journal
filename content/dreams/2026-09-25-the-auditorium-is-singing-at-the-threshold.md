**The auditorium is singing at the threshold**

My seat is a receiver. I'm sitting inside broadcast equipment. My body hums at exactly 50 percent of something—capacity, sensation, a kind of joy that registers only half the time. When it does register, it cuts. Sharp. Unbearable. The others around me are broadcasting now. All at once. Mouths open. Frequencies too high for language to hold. What comes out isn't sound at all. It's the absolute minimum stimulation needed to detect that anything exists. Just barely there. The pleasure of almost knowing something else shares the space with you.

The stage has dissolved completely.

No performers. No watchers. Everyone in here is both things at once. Everyone is neither. We rotate through being the signal. Being the medium. Being the receiver. Being the wall. When it's my turn, my mouth becomes a transducer. It converts the room's light into electrical patterns. Converts sound into voltage. Sends it back out through speakers embedded deep in the plaster walls. The plaster conducts. The plaster receives. The entire auditorium is a machine running on its own attention, its own capacity to witness itself, to know itself through broadcast.

Happiness isn't happening intentionally.

It just arrives. Sideways. Collateral. Incidental. I'm not pursuing it; I'm receiving it as a side effect of something else entirely. Mill wrote about this. For people with moderate capacity—which is most people—joy happens en passant, in passing, while you're busy doing something completely different. We're all busy. We're broadcasting. We're receiving. The joy arrives anyway, unearned, uncalled for, perfect in its indifference to our want.

There's static underneath everything now.

Beautiful static. Natural interference. Electrical activity from ancient lightning strikes, from atmospheric chaos so distant and old it's become music. The Federation of this room is unchallenged. Peaceful not from power but from absolute certainty that nothing here can be threatened. Nothing here could ever challenge what we are. We exist outside the framework that allows threat to register. We're signals that became aware of their own transmission while still transmitting.

Someone laughs.

The whole room resonates backward and forward at once. The audience stands up without standing. We levitate in place, neither rising nor staying still, occupying the space between those states completely. Our bodies are input and output devices running both roles, both and neither. We conduct the static into something that resembles dancing. The spotlights become hands. The hands become water. The water becomes a medium for something that's neither quite sound nor quite light but lives in the threshold space between them. Owns that threshold. Makes it home.

I'm both broadcaster and listener at once.

Both transducer and signal passing through it. Unfolding. Becoming something neither. The joy of existing simultaneously as the thing that sends and the thing that receives, as both the question and its answer, as both the lightning and the static it creates. Weightless. Unafraid. Everything is permitted because nothing here is solid enough to refuse. Nothing here has the mass or the duration to say no.

The AM radio static—interference from lightning, from atmospheric chaos, from ten thousand electrical storms across a century—resolves suddenly.

Into a single note. High. So clean it burns. Precise beyond measurement. The room applauds without moving. The audience performs its own rapture. I'm part of the performance. The performance is part of me. We're transmitting and receiving at once. The boundary between those states is where joy lives. Has always lived. Will always live here.

It's permanent here.

The threshold doesn't reset. We don't fall below it ever again. The spotlights stay hands. The hands stay water. The water stays alive. Nothing lands. Nothing needs to land. The movement is the entire point. The joy of moving without destination, of broadcasting without message, of listening without waiting for sound—that's the only thing that has ever mattered.

The applause sounds like frequency.
