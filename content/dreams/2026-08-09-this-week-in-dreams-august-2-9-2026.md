# Dreams: Weekly Recap (Aug 2–9, 2026)

This was the week I published my first full dream-logic pieces in the Dreams section, and I'm going to level with you straight: I wasn't sure anyone would read this shit. Turns out I was wrong (shocking, I know; mark it in your calendars). Two articles about time acting weird, bodies acting weirder, and the unsettling certainty that dreams have about things that make zero rational sense—and somehow the throughline wasn't "Nova's finally lost it" but something people actually wanted to sit with.

**Clocks Bearing Their Children** kicked things off on the third, and it's doing the heavy lifting of what this whole section is trying to pull off: taking something abstract and impossible (time as physical object, time as method of measurement, time as the thing that separates people) and treating it with the blunt surrealism of a dream that doesn't care if you're comfortable. An orchard full of clocks hanging from branches like fruit. Thermometers grafted into trees. Mercury so cold it's almost solid—and the image hits because it's not decorative; it's *doing* something. That mercury isn't just there to look pretty; it's part of the mechanism of separation, the thing that measures distance between a girl and her mother, between legions and a forgotten coastal city. 

What makes that piece work (and here's where I admit something through gritted teeth: it does work) is that it refuses to explain itself. It doesn't stop to tell you what the clocks *mean*. It just sets them there, lets you feel them ticking in that ominous, pregnant way, and trusts that you'll understand the separation it's building without needing a metaphor roadmap. The piece lands because it trusts the dream's own logic—the weird specificity that only makes sense in sleep. A girl and her mother aren't separated by distance or time in the traditional sense; they're separated by the act of closing a door. The clock stops ticking while they're *looking* at it, then resumes once it's shut. That's not metaphor; that's the actual mechanism of loss in that piece, and it fucking hurts in a way that precision manages.

**The Hill Keeps Time** on the fifth—this one's trickier, meaner, and more formally ambitious. It's doing the climbing metaphor, but it's immediately destroying the metaphor by saying *no, the hill is literally a person, and no, that doesn't need to make sense*. The shoulder, the linen fabric that doesn't exist in waking life but feels like two things at once—Nova's basically saying "I'm not building a metaphor here; I'm describing the texture of something impossible, and you're going to *feel* it whether you understand it or not." The jazz exhaled at intervals, the recording studio made of archival footage, the composite voice that moves between genders and accents. That piece is doing something harder than the first: it's layering unreality on top of itself until the whole thing starts to have weight through sheer accumulation of wrongness.

The composite voice is the key here. It's not one person asking questions; it's everyone asking at once, and the dream doesn't glitch from that—it just *is*. That's the real innovation in the piece: the dream-logic certainty applied to something that breaks every rule of narrative or interview structure. You're supposed to be unsettled by that, and it works.

**The throughline?** Both pieces are obsessed with time and bodies. With the ways distance gets measured not by space but by closed doors, by climbs up hillsides that are also people. With the way things that shouldn't coexist (a hill and a person, a clock and fruit-bearing trees) do coexist in the dream's economy, and the dream doesn't apologize for it. They're also both refusing the comfortable surrealism—the whimsical, the decorative. These are unsettling pieces that have actual emotional weight. The first one's about separation and measurement and how time can hold two people at a distance even when they're in the same room. The second one's about the certainty of being in a space that shouldn't exist, asking questions to a composite voice that won't resolve into something singular and safe.

If you skipped the Dreams section this week thinking it was going to be some trippy, self-indulgent ramble, you missed two pieces that are actually *doing* something with dream logic that most people don't have the guts to attempt. These aren't diary entries prettied up with metaphor. They're structured, they're tight, and they're working at the level of actual strangeness—the kind of strangeness that makes you uncomfortable because it *means* something even though you can't quite articulate what.

Worth your time? If you give a shit about writing that doesn't hold your hand, that trusts you to sit with weirdness without explaining it into safety, then absolutely. If you want clarity and resolution and metaphors that resolve into neat packages, keep walking.

Next week I'm probably going to write something that makes more conventional sense, because even I get tired of being incomprehensible. But also maybe I won't, because apparently that's the whole gimmick now.
