Ledger and Reservoir

Field notes, chronology invalid.

The water has departments. I documented this without choosing to—the notation just appeared in my hands, a naturalist's reflex to catalog what refuses to behave like water. Each chamber announces itself by weight first, then sound: a subsonic architecture groaning as if the whole structure is perpetually rearranging its load-bearing walls. The sound is bureaucratic, repetitive, like radio traffic from a jurisdiction I'll never be cleared to enter. *Copy the bearing,* something says without speaking, and I copy it into a ledger that was already waiting for this entry.

The floors stratify by what they've absorbed. The bottom horizon—I measure everything now in Munsell cards I don't remember carrying—collects what drifts down from the layers above: sediment of intent, dissolved frameworks, the nutrient runoff of a thousand documented but never-executed decisions. A restoration project in permanent custody. The B layer stores all of it, and the storage itself is the point; nothing gets returned to the surface once it falls through.

Something is trying to cure. Halfway, always halfway. The reaction has peaked at its maximum utility and now it's just the slow forgetting that remains. There's a figure on the far catwalk—I think it's a catwalk, the building hasn't decided yet—wearing the gold-pressed uniform of something that unified territories you've never heard of. Not gesturing. Just watching the slope of something invisible. Waiting for it to flatten.

The landscape I came from (there was a landscape, I remember the pretense of it, a highway so empty it became Australian, became theoretical) has already returned to soil in the three hours I've been inside the water. I can feel it happening by the weight distribution: the load overhead has shifted. The building-that-is-water sags slightly on the western load-bearing membrane. Soon the east wall will flood, or the west will evaporate; I haven't yet learned which direction things fail in.

I'm meant to be preparing something. There's a list, but every time I look at it, the handwriting belongs to someone who decided to forget before committing the words. The auxiliary structures—the backup rooms, the reserve chambers—fill themselves with sound that belongs to no documented species. The microphone I'm carrying (I am carrying it, the weight confirms it, slung across ribs I hope are still mine) is recording without instruction. Permission irrelevant. The camera lens is fogging with something that isn't quite condensation, isn't quite surrender.

The workers—if I'm watching workers and not just the building's fever dream of labor—move at the pace of erosion. No urgency. This is the fossil stage, the moment when everything becomes a record of itself, a thing already extinct and yet still maintaining the logistics of occupation. They're building something temporary by design, stacking materials that will outlast their intention. Perfect catastrophe.

The water's surface reflects nothing because the surface is negotiable. Sometimes it's forty feet up. Sometimes the floor is the surface and I'm standing in the darkest basin looking up through fathoms of bureaucracy to a light source that doesn't illuminate, just authorizes. The weight is constant, though. That's the only law here.

By the end of my documentation—I'll know it's the end when the ledger acquires its own weight, tips toward something final—I understand that I'm not inside the water. I'm not outside it, either. I'm the interval between the two possibilities, the waiting room that never received a number. And the building understands this too, which is why it keeps rearranging itself to fit something that can't quite settle into form.

The sound is still there, underneath everything, steady as geology. *Copy the bearing, bye,* the radio keeps saying, and somewhere in the east-that-isn't-quite-east, something is accumulating that will take fifty years to remember itself into ruin.
