Did the orchestra know it was already buried?

1. The antennae in the field stand in rows like musicians who stopped mid-measure years ago, waiting for a conductor who died at exactly the right time to make it artistic, and the soil beneath them hums in a frequency I recognize from nowhere real—a city burning backward through time, Algerian maybe, or the kind of place you only hear on a broadcast that wasn't supposed to reach you. The antennae are listening down, which is funny because up is where the signal used to come from, back when people cared about up. One antenna twists toward me, metal joint singing, and it's using my voice to report something that happens tomorrow.

2. The weight arrives as everything I've been holding without knowing I was holding it. Compressed. The size of a memory but with the heft of a thousand of them, and when I look down my hands are full of soil. A marketplace has become a filing system, or maybe it's the other way around. Someone is showing me photographs—a destroyed engine, its subframe twisted beyond use—and explaining in careful legal language how things get tampered with, how interference works, how you can destroy something and still be within your rights. The weight doesn't hurt. It's generous: here is your life, all 1.4 million moments of it, try not to drop any. I laugh in the dream, which means something is breaking in the waking part.

3. The city has been laid out like a body. Streets are veins. The light is the color of sound that's been played backward so many times it developed a new meaning. A radio announcement comes through the walls—someone died who shouldn't have, or didn't die when they were supposed to, or is dying for the first time now in reverse—and I'm not confused about which because time here moves like weather, like air through something buried that's trying to remember direction. A woman in a doorway is singing scales that are also the names of underwater places. Behind her a radio tower is being built in real-time by people made of interference.

4. The band plays without instruments. Four of them stand in what used to be a corner and are now a plaza and are now a shape for grief. Their mouths make shapes of sound—strings, percussion, voice—and the audience understands because we've all lived the same life forward and backward and we're tired of it in the nicest possible way. Someone is reading a manifesto by the way he stands: society got so artificial it forgot to protect pleasure and started protecting permission instead, and now here we are, clapping at a show that stopped playing decades ago. The drummer's arms move through air that's thick as water. Everything is beautiful and nothing is working and that's fine, actually that's perfect.

5. There's an engine in this place—fourth generation of something that never needed improving—rusted into a kind of music I almost recognize. Someone is explaining the precise terminology for how to destroy things legally, and it sounds like poetry in a language I used to speak. The antennae have tipped down like a garden coming to harvest, and they're broadcasting back what they heard: the sound of crops growing where graves used to be, which is exactly what should happen but never does. I'm not surprised. I'm relieved.

6. There's a comfort in forgetting. Memories that hurt get soft from repeated loss, ghosts become songs become the ambient hum of a life moving in multiple directions. A broadcast tower is being constructed by hands I don't recognize—no face, just hands, just the work—and at its peak someone is hanging, not dead, just suspended like a mobile in a child's room, reading from a decree that's also a prophecy that's also a resignation letter. The figure is nobody I know but everybody I was.

7. Everything is beginning again and nobody's noticed because the radio is playing a song about how joy can cut sharp enough to draw blood, how pain could pass for relief, how we quit telling them apart around the time we learned to broadcast. The antennae are replaying what they heard from the soil. The soil is singing in a language that comes before writing, before the concept of having something to say. Beyond the marketplace there's a door—not new, just finally visible—and beyond that are all the cities, all the dead who never stopped broadcasting, all the channels that carried signal while we were holding the wrong receiver.

The broadcast has been continuous; we were just tuned to ourselves.
