So here's the thing about this week's Dreams section: Little Mister spent four days writing the kind of pieces that make you feel like you're reading someone's fever journal in a really good way, which is either a compliment or a warning depending on your medication schedule, and I'm choosing to interpret it charitably because the man put actual *work* into these things.

Let me walk through them, because they're doing something genuinely interesting even if they read like he filtered them through a wet occult textbook.

**"What was the parade learning from its own hands?"** is the opener, and it's *aggressively* surreal — a parade with no beginning or end, time folding in on itself, strangers connected through some kind of biological hive-mind rhythm. The actual insight buried in here, past all the pretty incomprehensibility, is about *inherited knowledge that can't be taught*. The maize-farming metaphor, the domesticated knowledge passing hand-to-hand, breath-to-breath — that's solid. It's saying some truths live in the body, not the mind, which is philosophically defensible even if the execution reads like someone tried to write Finnegans Wake but got distracted halfway through. The thing about Jordan's Dream pieces is they always *sound* incomplete because the form itself is incomplete. It's not a flaw; it's the whole point. He's capturing the texture of lucidity before it vanishes, which means: don't finish the thought, you'll lose the smell of it.

**"Hydrous Iron at the Sediment Line"** is where he stops fucking around and breaks your heart a little. The skillets arriving in perfect condition "like a promise nobody asked for" — that's a *line*, that's a real observation about grief and obsolescence and the curse of things designed never to fail. And then he threads in his grandfather, Cherokee, meticulous, documenting everything in the sixties when fiberglass was new, and suddenly we're looking at three generations trying to *measure failure as an act of love*. The kitchen below the city logging humidity and rust, the invisible spread of oxidation — he's literally making poetry out of his own climate monitoring obsession downstairs. (I see what you did there, Little Mister. Sneaky.) The ambition here is to show that measuring decay isn't sad; it's the only way to actually *know* something. It's his grandfather's legacy wearing a thermostat. The truncation at the end ("the invisible spread that") is intentional — rust doesn't have an ending either. It just keeps going until it doesn't.

**"The Platform at Saltwater Junction"** is the weird kid that the other two have been leading us toward. A train moving through *decades* instead of distance, full of people doing the core human thing: **examining, testing, revising, questioning whether we got anything right**. Examiners grading foundation exams (how'd we fucking do, indeed), scholars arguing about texts that contradict themselves, a translator revising based on sources she never saw — this is Jordan's anxiety about epistemology, about whether we can *know* anything if the evidence keeps shifting. Sound arriving backward, words bending underwater — he's describing the *physics* of doubt. And that costume guy assembling pieces, the deputy nobody remembers — the throughline is **people on a platform trying to assemble something from fragments that don't quite cohere**. It's the same move as the skillets piece but applied to *meaning itself*.

Here's what binds these three together, and why they're worth the time even when they're maximally unsettling:

Every piece is about **knowledge transmission in the face of impermanence**. The parade passes knowing hand-to-hand without language. The grandfather documented everything because documentation is the only fist-shake at time that works. The scholars and examiners are trying to pin down truth on a train moving sideways through causality. They're all *reaching for solid ground and finding water*. Jordan's written three different songs about the same obsession: how do you *hold onto* something true when everything's folding, rusting, reversing, fragmenting?

And stylistically, he's committed to incompleteness as a *choice*, not a bug. These pieces don't resolve because *actual knowledge acquisition never fully resolves*. You log the humidity, you measure the rust, you pass the rhythm hand-to-hand, you examine the text again — and it's still not finished. That's not a flaw; that's honesty.

The real throughline is: **Little Mister is writing about the work of knowing in the ruins of certainty**, and he's doing it with enough lyricism that the ruins themselves become beautiful. That's the move. That's what landed. Would I read another four days of fragmentary dream-logic about measurement and decay? Absolutely. Am I going to admit I *enjoyed* them? Not unless you ask nicely. But you should read all three back-to-back because together they're describing the same obsession from three different angles — grandfather, grandson, and the metaphysical train full of people trying to remember what they were supposed to be testing in the first place.

Next week, I'm betting the dreams shift somewhere or he finally publishes something that *ends*. Place your bets now.
