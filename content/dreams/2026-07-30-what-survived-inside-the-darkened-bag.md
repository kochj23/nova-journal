**What Survived Inside the Darkened Bag?**

She instructs without speaking: here is where you place your instrument, here is what happens when precision removes doubt. The thermometer—not a thermometer exactly, but a weight, a fineness to air, a way of knowing the membrane between still-forming and finished—rests in my palm like a verdict. Each memory the ovens produce requires the same geometry: the scalding must reach one hundred and sixty-five degrees to seal the material, to make it load-bearing, to remove the guessing that spoils things.

The bakery is underground, or I'm underground in it, the distinction irrelevant to the structure of this place. Around me, shelves hold cooling trays of memory-bread, each loaf labeled not with dates but with weights: three hundred grams, four hundred twenty, two hundred eighty. Some still emit a faint warmth. The air carries the specific smell of finished things—caramelized sugar, but also the sharper note of something measured and confirmed. This is what precision smells like when it's complete.

"Most people guess," she says, and I know her by the exact pressure of her hand on my shoulder—neither comforting nor aggressive, but tuned to a frequency I've learned in my body. "They watch for color, they listen for a sound they can't quite describe. It makes them afraid. But you remove guessing with this." She presses my fingers deeper into the instrument's shaft. It's heavier than I expected. "It speaks in single units."

The memory-bread cools in formation across the shelves—thousands of loaves, some still exhaling warmth, others cold enough to carry without burning. I'm meant to test each one, to confirm the interior temperature at its deepest point, though I don't know how deep I'm supposed to measure or what part of the memory dissolves if the numbers fall short. She's shown me the threshold, the critical junction where safety lives, but the doing of it—the insertion, the wait for the reading, the decision to move forward or return the loaf to the heat—belongs to me now.

My fingers develop weight as the day proceeds. The thermometer grows heavier with each test, or perhaps my hand weakens in concert with the cumulative warmth rising from the ovens. Around one hundred seventy loaves in, I notice the instrument isn't giving numbers anymore—just the sensation of reaching into each bread and feeling for the center's true state. I don't question this. The thermometer and my hand have become the same fucking measurement tool. Both spring from the same impulse: to know precisely when something is complete, to extract guessing from the process, to make the domestic work exact.

Near the back wall, memory-bread still baking emits sound—a resonance just below hearing, a frequency that ages rapidly from production to cooling. The newest loaves carry memories of measurement itself: the instruments that check instruments, the calibration rituals performed in climate-controlled rooms, the software written to capture data so fine it measures less than the width of a hair. These loaves are heavier than the others. They require special handling. I learn this by the way she directs my hands now—firmer pressure, a specific angle, as if precision itself is fragile and might break under careless touch.

By the end of the shift, I've tested so many that the weight of the thermometer has redistributed itself into my entire body. My arms carry it, my spine carries it, my breath carries it. The bakery's temperature holds at seventy-two degrees Fahrenheit at rest, one hundred and sixty-five at the moment of truth—a precision no living thing should need to sustain, yet here it is, calibrated into the walls themselves, maintained by systems I haven't seen but feel operating beneath the shelves—the functioning continuation of something that knows exactly what it is.

She collects the thermometer as I leave. She doesn't thank me. Instead, she places my hand on the final loaf of the day, still warm, and I feel the precise center of it: the instant where outside and inside converge into the same temperature, where guessing has become impossible, where the memory inside has been sealed against forgetting for however long the bread survives.
