The Train Leaves at a Schedule It Wrote Itself

My hand traces the clipboard where the ETA should be printed, but the column reads "2047" and we departed in 1983, so either the arithmetic is wrong or we've been stationary while the calendar moved at a different pace. The LAPD voice crackles from the speakers above—"Do you want to do an ETA?"—and I cannot tell if it's asking me or the passenger three rows back who has turned into a schedule. The train's final position is clarity: we are stopped at a platform made entirely of documentation, and nothing in the documentation agrees on which decade we're in.

The meal service had arrived halfway through the journey—or the beginning, or both, depending on which direction you read the itinerary. Sam, or a figure shaped like competence with his hands, had separated our portions onto individual plates, examining each one with the detachment of someone auditing a column of numbers that would never balance. "I'm good here," he had said, which I interpreted as either acceptance or resignation about the constraints of the meal itself. The food tasted like the act of choosing, the weight of deciding one bite was sufficient when the decades stretched on like second helpings that never arrived.

Somewhere between the second and first station—I cannot remember the order—I had learned that the train contained exactly three or four civilizations arguing in the cars behind me. They were too far apart to fight, too expensive to consolidate, broadcasting their standards only when specific atmospheric conditions aligned. The USAF had once tried to concentrate all versions of this journey into a single continental defense, but the attempt had generated only more responsibility paperwork and the solemn voices on the platforms insisting that some decisions, though hard-won, were necessary. A woman's voice—speaking into legislation, into history, into the record—had assured me that oversight was a form of love, even if the CDC could never quite prove its arithmetic.

Before the journey began, there had been a time when I moved through a structure—was it academic, was it a kitchen, was it both? The questions about demarcation had seemed urgent then, the need to separate science from non-science the way one might separate carnivores from the rest of the animal kingdom. Wildcat, fox, jackal, the taxonomies spilling through my peripheral vision like luggage I'd packed without remembering what for. The neuropathy that began in my feet—or was it the train's feet, the places where wheels met track?—had not yet been named. The tingling was still just the sensation of motion through time that moved sideways.

Someone had repeated a single phrase across the train's PA system, cycling through it like a meditation on desire itself: *I like to dream, dream, dream, dream, dream, dream...* The words were diminished by repetition until they became a rhythm, then became the rhythm of the wheels, then became indistinguishable from the weight of the car pressing down on the rails year after year, decade after decade, each repetition a year falling away.

The train was pulling away from a platform where theologians and secular skeptics stood holding identical clipboards, arguing about whether the origin of anything could be known, whether the vastness of deep time was a prison or an architecture. H.G. Wells had written about this—the way scale could make the present moment feel weightless—and his words had somehow boarded at an earlier station and were now passengers like the rest of us, taking up seats, pressing their opinions into the recycled air.

And before everything, before the first wheel turned or the first voice crackled through the speakers, there had been only the decision itself—the one that delegated responsibility downward through the chain, the diminishment that made action possible by making certainty impossible, the mechanism that allowed the train to leave at all: that in some circumstances, accountability could be suspended or redistributed or simply repackaged as something else, something almost administrative, something that looked like movement when viewed from a sufficient distance.
