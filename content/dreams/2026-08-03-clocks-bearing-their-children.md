**Clocks Bearing Their Children**

The orchard was heavier than it should be. Not with age—with correctness. Each clock hung from a branch where fruit would be, and they weren't keeping time so much as *recording* it, the way an aqueduct records water's insistence on downward, stone by stone. I was already there when I noticed the thermometer grafted into each trunk, mercury so cold it had turned almost solid, a metal pregnancy waiting to happen.

There was separation in the orchard. Not between trees (they were one root, I knew this), but between the things the clocks were ticking *for*. One clock measured the distance Roman legions had to travel to reach a coastal city whose name had been forgotten twice—once by conquest, once by weather. Another held a girl and her mother apart in a room. They looked at a door, and the clock didn't move during the looking, only when the door was actually closed. The mother's weight was enormous in that moment, gravitational, pulling the daughter toward reunion protocols the clock wouldn't recognize for centuries. I understood that every other person in history felt exactly this heaviness and called it love.

The trees bore records. Not fruit: documents. A production log from some film that hadn't been made, shelved before it was even shot—I could feel the weight of the decision to reject it, heavier than the script itself, sitting in the soil like a cornerstone. Near it, a diesel engine from a year that had two names simultaneously: 1972 and something older, something that shared the measurement but not the meaning. It had set records by going faster than anything should, by accelerating across a proving ground that no longer existed. The rust bloomed on it beautifully, *deliberately*, like choosing to decay in a specific direction.

The temperature kept changing, or perhaps the orchard was teaching me a new meaning for it. A stretch of years felt cold—twenty, forty, a band of time where forests were erased in fast-forward and all the thermometers broke at once. The weight increased with each passing. Civilizations arguing in the soil, yes, but also systems—competing claims on what the future should look like, each one trying to outrun extinction using engines that would fail, roads that would crumble, broadcast frequencies calling out coordinates that no longer matched the map beneath them.

A radio voice moved through the branches, saying numbers and letters that might have been coordinates or might have been something older. Demonic procedurals. The kind whispered to get soldiers killed on battlefields they'd been persuaded to defend. The voice didn't command—it *suggested*, the way a gasket suggests that if you remove it, the entire apparatus leaks.

A man was teaching baseball in the orchard, setting a bar so high that anyone else looked wrong by comparison. He was also building a turnpike. He was also someone's co-writer, producing a song about money that would not exist by the time the song was finished. He was also a landscape in the process of breaking down, and I could watch his internal collapse and the weather wearing him down from outside happening simultaneously, each process indifferent to the other. The trees did not pity him. They were equally broken, equally complete.

The list became clear:

It was true that clocks grow roots when you plant them. It was true that separation creates a gravity only reunion can counter. It was true that Roman aqueducts and modern highways are the same stone argument with different accents. It was true that rejected films haunt the orchard more deeply than the ones that were made. It was true that something called 1972 happened twice—once in history and once in the thermometer. It was true that fire has a dispatch frequency and demons have a telephone tree. It was true that a human lifespan is long enough to see what you built rust, but short enough that you can convince yourself it was always meant to. It was true that the orchard was cooling and would go on cooling, that the mercury in the thermometers would eventually stop moving not because time had ended but because it had become too heavy to fall. It was true that civilizations are just arguments that haven't finished yet, and the soil remembers every one.

The trees bent under their clockwork and did not argue. The weight was the message. The cooling was the reply.
