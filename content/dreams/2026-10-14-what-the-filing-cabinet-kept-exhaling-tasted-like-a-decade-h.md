The hill was warm underfoot—too warm, the kind of warmth that makes you question whether you're climbing or being digested—and the woman lying beneath its contours exhaled in perfect bureaucratic intervals. Her spine was a filing cabinet system, each drawer labeled by year instead of content, because apparently even dreams require the most tedious organizational logic imaginable. Each year held the same argument in different legal languages: prohibited, then studied, then legalized, then criminalized retroactively, then studied again under some amendment buried in an appropriations bill nobody read. The sound wasn't breathing—it was boxes being checked and erased and checked again by different hands, different agencies, different people's conflicting interpretations of what "safe" meant. Actual mass pressing down on my shoulders like a coworker you can't escape, and I was climbing her body like it was a hill, which technically it was.

Too many forms. Checkboxes for "Contains controlled substance." "Proof of age required." "Safe for flight — Yes / No / Redacted / Submitted but classified / Somehow both," and underneath, in smaller print, "Previously approved Initiative 59, Washington D.C., 1998, now contested, now sealed, now filed in a building that doesn't exist." Triplicate copies, because one suffering copy wasn't enough—we needed three, filed under three different names, so nobody could prove we'd ever actually decided anything coherently.

Bureaucrats, or the bureaucracy that had learned to walk, or maybe they were the same thing by now. One was taking inventory with the manic energy of someone having a breakdown: sales metrics from 2007, up 35 percent from the previous year, which meant something had been successfully legalized or eradicated or both simultaneously. One was reading the geological history of crops that had been declared un-Islamic and then un-declared, which rearranged the supply chains downstream—a decision made in Afghanistan in the year 2000 that cascaded into every subsequent calculation about what would grow where, who would plant it, what it meant to touch the earth with that intention. One was reading aloud from classified advertisements—old personal listings, lost people, each one a story of agency that had been swallowed by a system and filed away and numbered and entered into evidence against the next person who would lose agency in exactly the same way.

The woman's mouth opened but what came out was LAPD radio traffic: "5 just the 6th year, part back, unit back part"—fragmentary, official, searching for a signal that had stopped transmitting. Around 1874, some guy named Janssen had built a camera shaped like a revolver to catch Venus in transit, to prove that time could be sliced into frames if you aimed carefully enough at the right celestial body. I pulled that device from her filing-cabinet ribs and aimed it at the climbers, and for one moment they all froze mid-checkbox. The forms multiplied. The drawer labeled "Rohrabacher-Farr Amendment (2014)" opened on its own and spilled amendments—tiny codifications of compromise, each one saying "study this but don't help anyone with it, read about it but the research is classified, know it exists but don't apply it, exist in the gap between legal and illegal."

I tasted old photocopiers—not copper, just bitter dust that had learned to vote, dust that remembered every time it was ruled irrelevant, dust patient about waiting for the next amendment, the next checkbox, the next year when we'd rearrange everything and call it progress.

By the time I reached the summit—or what should have been the summit—there was only more hill, more drawers, more years stacked wrong, more classified ads, more climbers, more forms, and her still underneath, still exhaling her legislative calendar, still the timeline we were all walking on top of, still the thing we were systematically filing away forever.
