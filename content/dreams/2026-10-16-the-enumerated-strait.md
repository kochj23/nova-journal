# The Enumerated Strait

The broadcast came in already cut. That's what I need you to understand from the start — the numbers arrived severed, as if someone had been sorting them by weight rather than sequence. Twenty-one, four, one, two, and then the nines just *accumulating*, like water pooling at a threshold no one had built a drain for. I was transcribing them into a ledger that kept shifting its columns, and the ink I was using seemed to flow *up* the paper instead of down, which should have bothered me more than it did.

The room — if you could call it a room; maybe it was the inside of a transmission, a hollow broadcast space where all the dispatches were being filed alphabetically by the sound of the dispatcher's breathing — had weight to it. Not darkness. Weight. The kind of pressure you feel at depth, in your sinuses, behind your eyes, pressing on the small bones in your ears. Oakwood was there, but not as a street. It was a filing cabinet, and when I opened it, the address tags read themselves aloud in the dispatcher's voice, fractured into syllables that didn't quite align: male, Hispanic, twenty-four, no further description. Then someone else's voice — calmer, medical, instructional — began explaining the correct sitting posture for reading a radio call: spine erect, weight distributed through the sitting-bones, heels pressed flat to the ground, as if posture could somehow decrypt the numbers.

The weight was coming from above. Not pressing down — *emanating* from above, a presence in the upper registers of sound that I could feel in my chest. The ledger's pages were printed on something that felt like damp cellulose, and every time I transcribed another string (6.1, 6.2, L-16, some variant of forty-five), the paper would absorb the ink and then *speak* it back, fragmenting it, doubling it, creating echoes that had never happened the first time. Traffic patterns were unfolding in the pressure around me. There was discussion about whether certain codes were "more than a second" or whether a second could even be measured when sound travels sideways, when it moves wrong.

A patient was sitting across from me — or standing; the distinction kept renegotiating itself — and they were trying hard not to breathe. Not refusing. *Negotiating* it, treating breath as an optional courtesy. Someone was explaining (the instructional voice again, now close to my ear, so close I could feel the consonants) the mechanics of ventilation: two slow breaths, one-point-two to two seconds each, the seal around the mouth creating an airtight boundary between inside and outside. But the patient's mouth wouldn't stay in one configuration. It was like trying to create a seal around a radio frequency that kept modulating.

Then the numbers came back. Not in the ledger this time — they were being called out in a voice like standing-room in a dispatch office no bigger than a closet, a space *entirely* comprised of broadcast static and the weight of accumulated radio calls. Unit codes proliferated. Vermont. North. Coordinates that didn't quite map to any quadrant. The pressure increased. My posture was wrong — I could sense this without anyone telling me, some intricate neural system that wasn't mine firing off corrections: shift your weight from heel to ball, lengthen the spine, open the sitting-bones wider, as if posture was the only thing preventing the transmission from collapsing inward.

The voice that had been speaking about breathing obstacles — the hindrances to deep breath, objectionable standing positions, seated postures that block the diaphragm — that voice suddenly explained that all difficult speech is disagreeable to the ear, and that whether you *hear* it aloud or read it in fragmented radio calls doesn't matter; the disagreement lodges in the same place, behind the sternum, in the weight you carry in your ribs.

I was holding a ledger that was also a patient that was also a dispatch channel, and the nines kept arriving, and the pressure kept mounting, and someone had to explain, in careful, measured tones, that the hindrances to deep breathing are not always obvious, that very often the problem isn't the posture itself but rather the *discipline* of maintaining it while the numbers accumulate and the seal around your mouth keeps slipping.

The numbers stopped. The weight remained. The inside of the sound was still transmitting.
