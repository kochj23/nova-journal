The Hill Keeps Time

You're ascending something that has the tactile quality of a shoulder—smooth, rising, warm where the fabric (wool? linen? something that doesn't exist in waking life but feels like both) catches against whatever surface you're climbing. No metaphor here in the dream's economy; this IS a hill, and IT IS a person, and the distinction doesn't fucking trouble you. The hill exhales jazz at intervals. Not the music itself—the shape of air left behind after it's played. You can feel it moving from underneath the linen, and when you press your palm flat against the slope, the pulse underneath is a metronome nobody wound.

Someone is asking questions in a recording studio you've never entered but recognize with the casual certainty dreams afford. The walls are archival footage—not screens, but actual gray celluloid curling in the air like smoke. The voice asking is a composite; it modulates between thick Sheffield brogue and something flatter, between male and female, and you know without being told that it's one person, recorded seventeen times across three decades, asking the same shit over and over: "Do you remember when that happened?" The answers are being spliced into something larger. You can hear the tape hiss between the responses, can taste the precision of it.

There's a constant hanging above your vision line—white letters spelling a number so enormous it has its own gravity. Particles. The Avogadro's shadow. It hums, and you're not sure if it's humming or if you are. Makeup designs are arranged in the corner like a costume graveyard: each face a different persona, none of them worn, all of them waiting with the terrible patience of things that will never be looked at. The heat of anticipated eyes makes your sternum ache.

A saxophone solo from 1972 plays across your collarbone—not metaphorically. The musician, someone's old friend's old friend, stands beside a garden shed where demos were recorded in real earnest, and he's playing directly at your throat. The music has texture. Each note leaves a small tear in whatever you're wearing, and underneath, everything is the exact right temperature. You want to tell him something but the words come out spliced, response-like, part of the archive.

The negatives exist. Someone famous for keeping them—keeping control, keeping the exact reproduction secret, holding back the truth in a locked negative—stands at the edge of your peripheral vision. You know they're there because light refuses to fully illuminate that space. There's independence in that darkness. Also loneliness. Also a film you'll never see completely, only in fragments, only in suggestion, only in the particular weight of what's been withheld.

A voice asks if this is absurd enough. The question matters for reasons you can't articulate, and the voice belongs to someone no longer alive, or never alive, or alive only in one recording repeated endlessly, answering the same random bullshit questions in the same shed, the same way. You want to touch the speaker—some kind of caress, some kind of closing—but the intimacy feels like a choice you're supposed to make on behalf of someone else entirely. The hill gets narrower. The linen warmer. Something like affection, something like obligation, something like being truly understood by a stranger who'll vanish when you wake.

The archive itself breathes—not the walls, but the collection, the entirety of what's been recorded and kept and hidden. It has a diaphragm. You're climbing inside it. The pulse beneath the linen quickens. The saxophone solo resolves into the question you've been asked before in this exact configuration: Do you remember when that happened? And your answer, spliced into the tape with all the others, is yes and no and something in between—something that feels like recognition, tastes like being known, wears like a skin you'll never remove.
