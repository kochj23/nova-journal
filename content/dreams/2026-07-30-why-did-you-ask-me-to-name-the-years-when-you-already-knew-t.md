**Why did you ask me to name the years when you already knew them?**

The elevator is narrower than it should be. I know this before the doors close because the figure has already told me so.

"You're wondering," the figure says, "whether the delay matters." They don't face me. They're facing the floor numbers, which don't exist yet. The panel glows with years instead of floors. 1933. 2004. 2001. 1957. The numbers don't climb; they shift sideways, like tuning a radio that's already playing something else.

"I wasn't wondering that," I say.

"No," the figure agrees, "but you will be. The difference is weight."

The pressure in the elevator intensifies—not from gravity, but from the air itself thickening. I can feel it collecting against my ribs. The walls haven't moved, but they're closer. That's when I understand: the walls are fixed. The elevator is shrinking around us with every breath we take.

"Was there a school?" I ask. "Sherman Oaks. Founded when?"

"The farewell was delayed," the figure says. It's not an answer, but it is. "Two weeks became vital. The staff left anyway. They had already named the exits."

Light flickers between the walls—not from above, but from within them. Each flicker shows a different year painted on the panel. The light is thin and blue, almost incidental, like it's being wasted on a room that doesn't deserve it.

"I need to know about the names," I say. "The stage names. Two towns on a map—"

"The boundary doesn't appear," the figure interrupts. "Conway to Twi. Distance is the tolling. You pay to cross. Everyone pays to cross, even when they don't see the wall."

The pressure is dense now. I can't expand my lungs fully. It's like breathing in a room where someone has already breathed the oxygen into a different shape—something that fills the space but won't feed the body. The figure's outline is sharp against the light, but I can't see their face. Maybe they don't have one yet.

"What about the harmonic nodes?" I ask, because I'm panicking now, and questions feel like movement. "The parallel surfaces. The corners. Where do they—"

"In every dimension," the figure says, "the hard things sing the same frequency. Left to right. Floor to ceiling. What you hear is the resonance of knowing you can't leave. The listening room is very small. Everyone is in it."

The elevator isn't moving. I don't think it ever was. The years on the panel shift again—2001, then 1995, then something I don't have a name for. The figure reaches toward the panel, and I see the badge on their wrist: two impellers. They're rotating in opposite directions.

"The glitch," the figure says, "lets you through. But you have to ask first."

"Ask what?"

"Not what. *Ask*. The mechanism of asking is what makes the wall permeable. You're doing it now."

The light dims. I notice, for the first time, that I can't hear anything from outside the elevator. No machinery. No building. No Burbank. No distant network hum. Just the two of us and the thickening air and the pressure that has nothing to do with altitude.

"I need to leave," I tell them.

"You need to know the purpose first," the figure says. "It changes. It was intelligence gathering. Then it became the *significant* purpose. Then it became something else. The name changed when the law changed. The wall has been broken this whole time—you just needed the right verb."

The elevator lurches. Or maybe I do. The distinction has already stopped mattering by the time the doors open, and I see not a hallway, but a spectrum: all the years at once, stacked vertically, each one receding into a light I can't look directly at. The figure doesn't move.

"Are you coming?" I ask.

"I've already answered that," they say. "You just haven't asked it yet. The message arrives before the question. That's how you know it's not a glitch."

The pressure releases all at once, and I fall—not down, but *across*, from one year-thick plane into another, suspended between thresholds that have never learned to resolve into a single moment, and I understand that this is the waiting: not the space between floors, but the space between every question and its answer arriving too soon.
