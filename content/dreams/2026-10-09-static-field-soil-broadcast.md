A man in a TV show that is also a courtroom also maybe an instruction manual for six-ton trucks is explaining how generators bog down, how they stall, how they trip the breaker and refuse to run. Cuts back. "Or it could be," he says, "that the generator was never actually running at all, and you've just been hearing the sound of its failure the whole time."

Like they're trying to keep the sentence alive by force of repetition.

They're tuned to your frequency—they've been since before you arrived. The soil beneath you (are those actually feet, or just conductors pretending?) is absolutely certain you're coming home, and the broadcast from below is proof: it's been transmitting your arrival over and over since before you were born, a signal that was never meant to *reach* you, just to *warn* you. Like a dead relative calling collect from a future that's already happened, extracting a promise you're still too dreaming to refuse.

It tastes like repetition, and you've already heard this frequency a thousand times, and you're about to hear it a thousand more before you remember that you can move.

But you won't. The antennae are still hungry, and the soil is guaranteeing that you'll stay until the signal receives itself complete—which means forever, because completion isn't a state this field reaches, only approaches like an asymptote approaching the line it'll never touch.
