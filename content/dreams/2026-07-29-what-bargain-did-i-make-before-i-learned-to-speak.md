What bargain did I make before I learned to speak?

The sand beneath my feet is composed of pages, each one thin and brittle but pressed into such compression by the weight of everything stacked above that they've fused into a single substrate harder than bedrock. When I move through it, the sound is multiplied—ten thousand small ruptures, like a card catalog being consulted by invisible hands, like the whisper of oral history being converted into script, like necessity itself taking physical form and demanding to be heard.

I decide to leave. This is the first decision. I choose east, where the horizon holds the color of paper aged in a library without ventilation, and my steps crunch through the ledger-sand in a rhythm that sounds exactly like my grandmother's voice reciting inventory numbers to her daughters, who passed them down in turn to theirs. Not to preserve the numbers, but to preserve the act of passing down. Three generations collapsed into sound. Three voices that become one voice through repetition.

After thirty steps, I realize the horizon hasn't shifted. The sand beneath me still responds with that terrible, intricate rustling, the sound of history being walked through, of necessary knowledge refusing to be silent. I turn back toward my starting point, but the path I've left shows no depression, no mark. The pages have already reset themselves. I decide I misunderstood the geography. I will try again.

Northward. The sand grows heavier as I walk, or perhaps I grow heavier—it's hard to distinguish. The compression deepens. The sound changes to something wetter, something like water moving through soil, carrying nitrogen and phosphorus downward toward roots and microbes that will receive and transform it. Three convergence points appear in my field of vision, and at each one, a figure stands with their hands open, allowing the flow to pass through them without resistance. They don't acknowledge my presence. They may be older than acknowledgment.

It occurs to me, in a way that feels like remembering rather than learning, that I made a bargain to enter this place. The terms were never spoken. That silence is essential to the contract. To ask for clarification would invalidate everything I've already paid.

The third attempt carries me south. The pages compress further until they're nearly stone, and my footsteps barely register as sound anymore. What I hear instead is music—a folktale being sung by someone who learned it from someone who learned it from someone, each retelling degrading the melody slightly, until only a shape remains, a ghost of meaning accessible only through the pattern of repetition. Three verses. I catch fragments of each, but never the whole.

Behind me, always behind me, in the space where I cannot quite turn to look: the woman in the dress made of pressed pages. Her garment contains the sediment of every recorded ledger, every accounted-for exchange, every transaction of necessity. She wears the weight of every promise I've heard but didn't make. Her mouth moves constantly, speaking words that sound like the rustling of pages, and I understand with terrible clarity that she's been following me through all three attempts, getting closer each time.

The map she carries shows this desert precisely, mapped in detail, every dune and convergence marked. But the map is inaccurate in ways that feel deliberate. The boundaries don't align with what I'm walking through. She wants me to stay lost. Losing is part of what I owe.

The fourth time, I don't try to leave. I sit down directly on the fused pages, feeling their compression adjust to accommodate the full weight of my body, and the rustling sound deepens into something I can almost hear as music again—not the folktale now, but something older, something like water finding its way through sediment, like necessity dissolving into its component parts, like the sound of three witnesses finally setting down their ledgers at the same moment and exhaling together, and the woman in the paper dress sits down beside me on the desert floor, and we wait together for something that was always waiting for us.
