Freight and Scaffold

"Can something that exists only as an idea still need oiling?" the figure beside me says. We're not exactly watching the parade anymore — we're in it, pressed into the crowd that moves like a single organism breathing. I haven't asked this yet.

"The ones on the float," she continues, pointing to where two large machines sit on a platform draped in something that looks like stadium seating fabric — no, like the lining of a leather jacket turned inside out — both of them idle, their components humming at a frequency that doesn't make my teeth ache exactly, but makes the back of my throat feel corroded. "They're set to 31 degrees and 27 degrees, like they're waiting for something to be born into them, or maybe waiting to be forgiven for already existing."

I think the question forming in my pocket (the parade moves and I move with it and my hands are full of things I can't quite name) is whether forgiveness requires memory. Whether something industrial could feel small.

"The point was always the efficiency," she says, and I realize she's answering a question about how many we need, or whether we should have made them at all. "The bureaucrat sees the system, not the consequence. The system runs perfectly. People are incidental." A section of the parade surges forward — it's all public transportation workers, but they're carrying something between them, like a stretcher, like a relay, like a thought that's too heavy to think alone. She's talking about something that wasn't asked. "Once the decision is made, you don't consult further. You manufacture the rest."

The crowd hands something to me — it's wrapped in paper that feels like it has a history, like it's been held before. I unwrap it without meaning to. It's a sports card, but the photograph isn't of a player; it's of a fragment of fabric sewn into the card's window, authentic material from something that was worn in an actual game. I can smell the ink and the sweat-memory of the cotton.

"You keep looking for the moment where it stops being real," the figure says. She hasn't turned her head. "As if real and idea were separate territories instead of two languages for the same terrain." The parade is moving faster now, or maybe I'm moving faster. "Sound can exist unperceived. A shape can sit in the dark. A color can wait in a mind that isn't anyone's. The whole damn parade could stop existing the moment we look away, and it wouldn't know the difference."

Someone hands me a scotch egg, but when I break it open, it's not egg at all — it's a small machine with a hard center and meat-colored layers wrapping it, and I'm supposed to understand this as normal. The film they dubbed in English in 1989 runs somewhere on the edge of this crowd, or maybe it's the parade's soundtrack, or maybe it's the voice of the figure beside me playing backward. Limited release. Not meant to reach everyone.

"What are you asking?" she finally says, and it's the first question either of us has asked directly, which means I've already answered it three times without speaking.

The parade moves toward something — not a destination, but an accumulation, the way bureaucracies accumulate, the way ideas accumulate in a mind with no exit, the way manufactured things accumulate consequence. I'm holding the card and the egg-machine and my own un-asked questions, which are also unansweringly large.

"Whether you'll feel it," the figure says, "when something you made stops working for no reason you can control."
