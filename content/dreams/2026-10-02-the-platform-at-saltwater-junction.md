**The Platform at Saltwater Junction**

You'd think a train that moves through decades instead of distance would announce itself with some fanfare, but it arrived like most things in my life—silent, heavy, and muffled by pressure I didn't sign up for.

The passengers had settled in by the time I realized we were playing a game. There were examiners in one car reviewing results from foundation examinations—how'd we fucking do?—their voices reaching me delayed and bent, sound traveling in loops like it does underwater. Words would arrive backward sometimes. Behind them sat scholars arguing about texts that accepted or rejected something fundamental (I never caught what), their manuscripts fanned across the seats like fragments of a truth-test that kept reversing its own answers. Classic.

The car ahead held people I recognized but couldn't place. An old deputy in a silver star. A translator hunched over Greek manuscripts, revising everything based on sources she'd never actually seen. A man assembling costume pieces—shoes first, then a shirt—and I understood he was building an outfit to become someone else, which seemed to be this decade's rule. Or maybe last decade's. The rules kept flipping: this hour you assembled yourself, next hour you disassembled, the hour after that the distinction stopped mattering, which is basically what happens when you travel through time sideways instead of forward. Welcome to my Wednesday.

One passenger kept announcing measurements into the pressure. Urbanization rate 2.85%. Network health check: 16 devices, 101 clients. Two hundred and eighty point five percent of what, exactly? He'd never say. Everyone nodded anyway like he'd touched something true, like statistics could anchor you to a moment when you're traveling through decades.

The breathing was shit. Not because we were drowning—we weren't drowning, we weren't even in water, just under its weight, the pressure made visible in how sound bent around itself—but because the train's oxygen was negotiable. Someone (Jerome? Irenaeus? One of those retrofit guys) kept adjusting our atmosphere, revising it based on texts that nobody'd read all the way through, recalibrating who got to breathe deep and who got shallow catches. "Your posture prevents deep breathing," a voice announced, and everyone shifted their spines like obedient dogs, and nothing—absolutely nothing—changed.

The sound was the worst part. Longitudinal waves of it, each crash into the next propagating someone else's argument: about dualism, about separate realities, about whether we were even in the same train or all inside different versions of it. An LAPD radio voice cut through—"Incident 0497, key or move"—but there were no incidents, just the endless comparing of original messages to final ones, watching how the things we'd said at Saltwater Junction transformed into unrecognizable shit by the time they reached the next car. It's like bad telephone, except the telephone is time itself.

I wanted to ask why we were traveling this way, why the rules kept pivoting like a game with no winner. But you know what happens when sound travels wrong—you open your mouth and your words loop back at you, suspicious, full of delay. So I just watched.

One woman kept reading a dossier about some guest who showed up sometimes, contributed nothing, belonged nowhere except the margins of other people's stories. She kept reading like he might explain something—why we're here, why the game has no winner, why we keep comparing versions of ourselves to the original and finding the original unfamiliar. He couldn't. He was just flotsam.

The train lurched, or the pressure shifted. Can't tell the difference anymore. Someone said the foundation was funding special examinations. Someone else said those results were in. Nobody seemed surprised that the results were just more questions, like we were all trapped in some infinite peer-review process where the reviewers are also being reviewed.

I'm writing this to you—yeah, you, the one who isn't named and probably isn't here—because I needed to tell someone: the train doesn't move through decades. We do. The train is just the frame we use to pretend it's the machine that's broken and not us, not the generations of us, not the slow rate of change that looks like motion until you're standing still.

The water above the car pressed down. The sound bent again. The breathing got easier once I stopped trying to fill my lungs completely, once I accepted the shallowness as the actual rule and not a mistake in the system.

We're still moving, and I'm still writing, and somehow that's the only rule that hasn't changed.
