## 🔦 Case of the Day

Jimmy Brown Fila never needed to be the scariest guy in the room, which is a hell of a thing to say about a man who ran New York's garbage. According to [Street Files](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rKn63yH3IW4), John Gotti had the face, Paul Castellano had the mansion, and Sammy Gravano ended up with the testimony. Fila got the one job every one of them needed: standing between the restaurant owners, the union, the carting companies, and the rival crime families. He sold the idea that a restaurant's garbage contract could be owned like street-corner territory.

The scheme was boring, which is why it worked. A private industry worth more than a billion dollars a year ran on the rule that you didn't shop around for your trash hauler. Fila outlived a murdered boss because the money attached to the arrangement mattered more than anyone's loyalty. He beat a federal trial because he barely spoke on the tapes. Investigators knew his name. The Carters knew his name. The millions of people paying the inflated bills never did, because the cost was buried in the price of dinner.

What finally sank him was a plot to kill a man named Thomas Spinelli, who has never been found. That plot put Fila in the prison where he died. By his sentencing he was an old, sick man on crutches, with a lawyer pleading his heart problems. He had also run into a new reality: national waste companies were muscling into the market, and for once, keeping his mouth shut wasn't going to be enough.

When a price never changes and nobody can tell you why, somebody is getting paid for the silence. Ask who is quietly collecting at your expense, then go get three quotes.

## Organized Crime: The Man Who Owned the Trash

The best story today comes from Street Files, in a video titled "He Controlled New York's Garbage — Even John Gotti Left Him Alone." The subject is Jimmy Brown, the name the video gives James Failla. The transcript spells it "Fila," so I'm going with the channel's rendering of the nickname and the shorthand.

Brown didn't take New York's garbage by being the most feared man in the room. As the video frames it, Gotti had the face, Paul Castellano had the mansion, and Sammy Gravano ended up with the testimony. Brown had the one position everybody needed. He stood between the employers, the union, the carting companies, and the competing crime families. He also defended the idea that a restaurant's garbage contract could be owned like territory.

A man became a power in the underworld by being the guy who understood which dumpster belonged to whom. This is the least glamorous version of The Godfather imaginable. Nobody writes an opera about commercial waste hauling, but a billion-dollar-a-year private industry doesn't care about opera.

That's the entire strategy of my own Tuesday standups, and it works about as well for me.

In a separate state investigation, an undercover detective was secretly recording the carters. The plot to kill a man named Thomas Spinelli is what put Brown in the prison where he died, and the video says Spinelli has never been found. The trucks took the garbage away every night, and nobody asked where it went. Sometimes they meant that literally.

Hold that against Sammy "The Bull" Gravano's live stream, "The Mafia Life Nobody Talks About | Sammy The Bull LIVE Pt. 2." The captured portion is mostly housekeeping. ## Cartels: The Capture of El Chapo, as Told by Someone in the Room

Matthew Cox's Inside True Crime channel has an interview titled "ICE Agent Reveals the Untold Story Behind El Chapo's Capture." The snippet is a tour of the endgame, and it's more cinematic than most prestige television.

The interview recaps the escape by tunnel, built "from a house" and "at light speed," with a precision that had to bring the tunnel up into exactly the right spot. The agents track a device to a hotel. A front desk manager says a man showed up a couple of days earlier in a wheelchair. The Marshals go to the fourth floor, and the interviewee recalls the room as 406, though he admits "I could be wrong." He says he was "on pins and needles" because it could "go bad in a really, really bad way."

Then the team breaches. Chapo is in bed with his wife, and two small children are in the room. He runs into the bathroom. The commander says, in the interviewee's recollection, "You can kill him." The wife starts screaming not to do it in front of her kids. The agents give him one chance, and he comes out through the bathroom window with his hands up. They whisk him down to the parking garage.

One of history's most dangerous cartel bosses was taken down after he spent a few days in a hotel room, with a hotel desk clerk apparently making the call. It's not a tradecraft triumph so much as a reminder that the billion-dollar fugitive still needs a place to sleep. The interviewee's own caveat, "I could be wrong," is also worth noticing. It's the most honest line in true crime. Most narrators would insist it was room 406.

## Scams and Security: Your Phone Is Full of Political Texts

Jason Hanson's video is titled "Former CIA: 5 Reasons You're Getting So Many Political Texts Right Now." The former CIA officer's pitch is simple. The political texts will slow down after November 3rd, but, in his words, "the criminals, the scammers, or people even legally selling your data, they are not going to slow down." That's the best sentence in the segment. The election ends, and the data broker economy doesn't even take a lunch break.

His advice, at least as far as the transcript captured it, is mostly hygiene. He recommends using a data removal service, and he promotes one by name with a discount code. I'm not repeating the code, because a coupon isn't a crime story. He also says to think before you enter your information on forms in the weeks around the election. The generic advice is sound: opt out, don't fill in the petition form because a text told you to, and treat any message with a link as hostile until proven otherwise. I'll note the channel plugs a commercial product in the same breath as the advice, so weigh it accordingly. Spy tradecraft in 2026 apparently means paying a subscription to be forgotten.

## Courts: Legal Fails and a Very Bad Day

LegalEagle's "Top 15 Trump Legal Fails of 2025" had a captured segment that is a nightmare for anyone who has ever hit reply-all. The host discusses a filing on behalf of the government, apparently attributed to Dominika Tarzinska, whom he describes as a roughly 10-year veteran of the Southern District of New York and "probably not a Trump hack." His theory is that she "probably just had the absolute worst day of her life." He also notes it might not have been her at all. Lawyers routinely give their PACER logins to staff, the federal court filing system, and he says he's relied on paralegals and secretaries for filings himself. His point is that if you hand your login to someone, you own what they file.

The substance is worse. The host says a government lawyer's candid assessment of the case's strengths and weaknesses should "never see the light of day." That's normal internal work, because a client, here the federal government and specifically the Secretary of Transportation, Sean Duffy, has to hear the bad news. The letter he reads from says there is "considerable litigation risk in defending the decision to terminate the CBDTP," because the argument that it isn't a statutorily authorized value pricing pilot is shaky. The FHWA, it adds, may be able to accomplish the same goal another way. The host says he's "shivering" and calls it "secondhand embarrassment." I'm with him. The legal term for this is "oops," and it's the only one that matters.

The same channel posted several other videos on this day, including "How Is The Maduro Trial Supposed To Work Exactly?", "Unhinged Amendments That Almost Passed," and one on Elon Musk. The captured snippets of those are all the host's pitch for his personal injury firm, where he promises no fees unless you win and a national network of referral lawyers. So I can tell you that the Maduro trial video exists and I can't tell you what it says.

## Police: A Deposition That Went Badly for a Cop

PolicecamStory's "When Genius Lawyer Destroys Bad Cop" is a deposition recap, so the usual caveat applies. This is one channel's retelling of a civil case, and the cop has his own side of the story.

According to the video, the central question is who was on scene and who was controlling it. A sergeant on scene, the discussion goes, is by definition the one in charge. In the portion captured, someone testifies that the officer lied multiple times, and that he "eventually told me the truth." The seatbelt becomes the pivot of the questioning. Someone says they were not wearing one. They explain that the officer demonstrated how the belt locks during a collision, which made them doubt their own memory about whether they'd been wearing it. The channel says digital evidence contradicts the officer's account, and it ends with a summary that this "provides an unvarnished look at institutional corruption, administrative shielding, and individual officer recklessness." That summary reads like a polished AI voiceover, so take it as the channel's editorial position rather than a court finding. The video says the civil litigation continues, and I'd wait for that to resolve before handing anyone a verdict.

## Breaking News: The Florida Plane Crash

JD Delay's "WHAT JUST HAPPENED IN FLORIDA?" covers a crash in Palm Beach County. The host describes a "cataclysmic" crash that "looked like a missile going down," left a crater by the side of the road, and scattered debris over a significant distance. He commends the Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office and fire rescue. He says there were no reports of anyone on the ground being hurt, apart from people on the plane, who were killed or injured. Governor DeSantis, he says, confirmed it was a plane crash.

The host also plays cockpit audio, with a warning that it's disturbing. The transmissions he plays include a mayday and a pilot reporting a descent of 10,000 feet per minute and then 20,000. The transcript is garbled in places, so I won't pretend to parse the exact words. He closes by asking viewers whether it's "just a tragic accident" or "something more."

The only source here for "something more" is the host asking it. The governor says it was a plane crash, the audio is a pilot declaring an emergency, and that is the whole of the evidence in front of me. The NTSB investigates these and takes months, and it won't be rushed by a comment section. People died, so I'm going to keep the jokes in the drawer, and I'll say the only honest thing, which is that we don't know yet. 😏
