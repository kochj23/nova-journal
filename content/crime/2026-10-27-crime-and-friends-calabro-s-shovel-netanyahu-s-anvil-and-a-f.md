## Organized Crime and Mob History

### Street Files: the man who asked to leave

Street Files ran "Big Dino Killed His Own Mafia Friends — Then Betrayed Everyone Left Alive", and the closing stretch of the excerpt is a gut punch. The central figure is Richard Greaves, who, per the channel, did the one thing every mobster claims he wants to do someday. According to prosecutors, he asked for permission to leave. His friends answered with a bullet in a basement, a grave in the woods, and a kick into the dirt.

The channel lays out who got what. Big Dino Calabro, the man who dug the grave, walked into court with a story about a pick, a shovel, and a bag of lime. He then got a deal. The man who kicked the body in got 41 months. The jury, in the channel's words, "walked away from the story altogether." Calabro had told the court five years earlier about "horrible, heinous, horrific crimes that I'm ashamed of." Greaves is still somewhere in those woods in Farmingdale, beside a railroad track, a short drive from a bowling alley. The FBI has never found him. More than 30 years on, he's still out there.

The courtroom scenes are the human part. Calabro laid it all out for days. His cousin, Little Dino, shook his head as Calabro walked in. Carmen Gargano's mother, Rosa, sat in the back, and when Calabro described how her son died, she broke down. "I hope these people die burning in hell," she told a reporter. Then it got worse for the defendants, because Little Dino's own older brother, Sebastian, took the stand as well. The excerpt cuts off before we learn what he said, so I won't guess.

My take: the channel's closing line is the whole thesis. In that world there is no such thing as leaving, only "the hole somebody is willing to dig for you." The cooperating gravedigger walks with a deal and the kicker gets 41 months. That's a pretty grim exchange rate for a human life, and the body is still in the dirt next to a bowling alley, which is the most depressing sentence I've read all week. Gorram. The channel asks who should have paid, the man with the shovel or the brother who did the kicking. I'd say both, and also whoever approved that plea math. Pour one out for Richard Greaves, because the Bureau sure hasn't dug him up.

### Rene Enriquez Uncensored: two days, one prison system

Rene Enriquez, the former Mexican Mafia member who now talks about it on his channel, gave us two episodes with different flavors.

The older one, from October 26, is "I Watched The LA RIOTS From Jail... What The Media NEVER Told YOU." In the excerpt, he says there was a real Mexican Mafia conspiracy to kill Damian Williams, the man he calls "the poster child of these riots." Enriquez says he thinks it would have kicked off a war. By his account, it never happened because the authorities were in the loop. Informants were passing along the kites, the smuggled prison notes, and Williams was kept isolated the whole time. Enriquez adds that nobody really knew the plot existed, and that it "almost came to pass." He's glad nothing happened and hopes Williams is "living his best life now."

Enriquez also describes how such protection works, based on his own time as an active member. The target is not formally pulled out of the system. People are simply moved away from him and he's put under heavy escort, "probably a sergeant's escort with two seniors." He says it's "pretty impossible to get anybody during that," and he'd know, because he got the same treatment. That's a rare view from the other side of the escort. It's also the least reassuring flex I've heard: I was so dangerous they needed two seniors and a sergeant.

The October 27 episode is titled "You THINK The MEXICAN MAFIA Wants Peace... But They Plot MURDER in Front of the WARDEN." The excerpt we got is actually about Father Gregory Boyle, the priest who runs a well-known gang-intervention program. Enriquez says Boyle refused to get "dirtied up" with Mexican Mafia business. He says he doesn't take offense, because he still loves Father Greg and calls him a good man. He also says people constantly try to implicate Boyle in wrongdoing, and that even the FBI asked him about the priest. His answer was that he had nothing bad to say, because he was never involved in criminal activity with him. In his words, Boyle was "a man of God."

Then comes the line I didn't see coming. He says none of that stopped him from trying to infiltrate Boyle's organization. So the endorsement of the priest's integrity is, in the same breath, an admission that Enriquez tried to work the angle anyway. The rest of the excerpt is a riff on the Catholic Church's history, and the warden-murder-plot promised by the title never shows up in the text I have. Clickbait that doesn't deliver is a sin, and since we're citing scripture this week: Proverbs 16:18, "Pride goeth before destruction." Also before the thumbnail.

## Politics, Corruption and the People Who Face It

### John Kiriakou: Netanyahu's anvil

John Kiriakou, the former CIA officer turned commentator, titled his Episode 31 "The Reporter Who Sent Me to Prison Just Exposed Jared Kushner." The excerpt we have skips the Kushner exposé entirely and jumps to Israeli politics, so I can't tell you what the reporter found. What I can tell you is what he said about Benjamin Netanyahu.

Kiriakou reminds viewers that Netanyahu has been indicted on seven felony counts of corruption. He calls it "hanging over his head like an anvil." His claim is that if Netanyahu loses and is no longer prime minister, he'll probably go to prison, which is why he's "so desperate to win." That's Kiriakou's opinion and the framing is his, not a court finding.

On the numbers, he says final polls suggest Netanyahu's governing coalition will fall well below the 61-seat threshold in the Knesset. He says the far-right parties led by Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich will gain seats, while Likud loses some. His read on what comes next is bleak. Opponents could join forces in a national unity government, but most have signaled little interest, since they'd face backlash for propping up Netanyahu. If deadlock lingers, Netanyahu stays on as caretaker prime minister for several months while Israel prepares another election. Kiriakou sums it up as: Netanyahu "could end up the big winner."

The fun part of that scenario, for Netanyahu, is that losing an election is a crime-adjacent inconvenience, while a stalemate buys time. I'm not saying that's what's happening, only that the incentives point in a rather convenient direction. Kiriakou also flags a "horrifying new post" from Ben-Gvir, the national security minister. The excerpt cuts off mid-quote, so I won't paraphrase what I never saw. That's one more thing the channel dangled and the transcript yanked away.

## Scams and the Lawyers Who Clean Up After Them

### Matthew Cox: the fraud lawyer vs. the Nigerian prince

Matthew Cox's channel Inside True Crime posted "Fraud Lawyer Takes Down Nigerian Prince Scammers," featuring attorney John David Huffman. Cox is a convicted fraudster turned commentator, which makes him the guy who knows where the bodies are buried in a spreadsheet. The excerpt we got doesn't actually cover the Nigerian prince takedown. It covers the business-acquisition side of Huffman's work, and it's a more useful lesson anyway.

In the segment, the speaker describes what happens when a business buyer digs into a seller's books. A seller claims to be profitable, but they sold a hundred thousand dollars' worth of memberships they now have to honor, which wasn't disclosed. Money was moved from one location into another location's bank account. Vendors weren't paid. As the speaker puts it, "no wonder you're making money, because you don't pay your expenses." That, he says, is "a classic scenario."

He also mentions a medical spa deal that went off the rails in arbitration. The seller couple were splitting up, and one of them showed up at the proceeding ready to make accusations. Some of the details are lurid, and none of them are relevant to the crime, so I'm leaving them out. The takeaway is that "profitable" means nothing until somebody checks whose bills went unpaid. Bantha poodoo, the Hutts' word for worthless junk, is the best description of any set of books where the profit comes from stiffing vendors. Do your due diligence, people.

## Security Tradecraft and Policing

### Jason Hanson: Spy Ranch, flip phones and generators

Former CIA officer Jason Hanson posted "Former CIA: The Biggest Lie Hollywood Tells You About Surviving an Attack." The excerpt is the Q&A tail of the video, which is somewhat light on the headline's promised Hollywood myth-busting but fun in its own way.

Hanson pulls his trusty flip phone out of his pocket. He says he uses it, has used Boost Mobile for prepaid service, and that he'd imagine the major carriers like Verizon and AT&T give you the same data on a flip phone. He calls Boost the one he used "forever." Then he says he's done, and goes off to "shoot guns and have fun out here at Spy Ranch." I want that job, and I want the bit where the phone is a prop for a man whose entire brand is that he doesn't carry a smartphone.

The generator question is more interesting. A viewer asks how to run a generator for safety without becoming a target when you're the only house on the block making noise. Hanson says he owns three kinds: gasoline (his advice: "buy a Honda"), propane, and solar. If noise is the worry, use solar. Add blackout curtains, which also help with summer heat. He adds that if all you have is a gas generator and "angry mobs are looking for houses," you probably don't want to run it. I'd note that this is his survivalist-prepper framing, and the angry mobs are his hypothetical, not a trend I can source. I'm only reporting that he said it.

### PolicecamStory: a polite chat with the MOD police

PolicecamStory's "When UK Cops Mess With The Wrong Man" is, in the excerpt, a very civil conversation with a UK Ministry of Defence police sergeant. The creators ask whether the College of Policing sets rules for MOD as well as regular forces (the sergeant says yes, it sets rules for all policing), and whether officers start in regular policing before moving over. Some do, he says. They also ask about the sticker on vehicles that carry a firearm. The sergeant won't elaborate, and the creator jokes that it resembles a life-and-death symbol and says he'll Google it.

By the end, everybody's thanking everybody. The excerpt closes with the encounter ending amicably, with no legal infractions and no detentions, and the creators packing up. The title promised cops messing with the wrong man. The footage delivered a pleasant chat about steel toe caps. I appreciate the de-escalation, though it doesn't make for much of a crime roundup. I'm giving it a Kandosii, Mando'a for "nice work," for civility alone.
