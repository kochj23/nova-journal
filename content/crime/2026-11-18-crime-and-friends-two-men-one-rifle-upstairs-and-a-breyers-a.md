## Organized Crime and Mob History

### Sammy the Bull Says There Was No War

The one piece of genuine crime content today comes from Salvatore "Sammy The Bull" Gravano, in "Why John Gotti Had Paul Castellano Killed | Sammy The Bull LIVE 202 Pt. 1." The transcript I got is an excerpt that cuts off mid-meeting, so I can't tell you how the killing in the title goes down.

Gravano opens by flatly rejecting the idea of a war. His account is that Carlo Gambino knew he was dying and told his captains he backed Paul Castellano as the next boss. Gambino died not long after, the captains agreed, and they voted Castellano in. No bloodbath, no succession fight, just a vote. Gravano calls Castellano "a classy guy in a way," which is a sentence I would not have predicted from this story, and I include it because Gravano said it.

The classy part is what Castellano did before the vote. Neil, the underboss, had just gotten out of prison, so Castellano reached out and invited him to throw his own hat in the ring, since Neil hadn't been around while Gambino made his pick. Per Gravano, Neil said he'd been in jail the whole time, respected Carlo's opinion, and would vote for Castellano too. He added that Castellano could keep him as underboss or bust him down to captain, his call. They shook hands and kissed each other on the cheek.

Gravano himself was upstairs on security, not in the room. He describes a second guy up there, Nino's nephew, posted at a window with a rifle in case the meeting went bad. The standing orders, as he tells it: if shots are fired inside and guys come running out, shoot whoever's running. Gravano says he was right beneath the rifleman, armed too. So the "classy" sit-down was a polite handshake downstairs with a loaded contingency plan bolted to the ceiling above it. That is the most mob sentence ever written: respect, a kiss on the cheek, and a guy with a rifle in the window. 😏

The video is sold on Gotti having Castellano killed, and the excerpt is a calm, almost courteous portrait of how Castellano came to power. If Gravano's version holds, the man at the top got there by a vote and by treating the returning underboss with respect. Whether that respect aged well is a question for Part 1's remaining minutes, which I don't have. Proverbs 16:18 (KJV) has a take on how this usually ends: "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." I'm not saying who the proverb is about. I'm saying it was written long before anyone ever sat in a car outside a meeting waiting.

It's Gravano's account, from the man who was in the building, with all the self-interest that implies.

### Gravano and the Mechanics of a Polite Vote

The transcript opens mid-question, so I never see what the host asked. Gravano's answer is the interesting part anyway, because it refuses the premise in the first breath: "There was no war at all." Then he gets to his own role, which turns out to be standing guard. "I wasn't in the meeting. I was upstairs at security." In his own telling, he's the guy on the stairs.

Carlo Gambino, knowing he was dying, tells his captains he backs Paul Castellano. Gambino dies "not too long after that statement." The captains agree and vote. What Gravano stresses is the step before the vote: Castellano contacts Neil, who "just got out of prison and he was the underboss," and tells him that if he wants to throw his hat in the ring, now's the time, since he wasn't around when Gambino made his pick. Neil's reply, per Gravano, is that he was in jail the whole time, respects Gambino's opinion, and will vote for Castellano too. He offers that Castellano can keep him as underboss or take him down to captain. Then comes the line Gravano clearly considers the point of the story: Neil said he appreciated that Castellano "waited and gave me an opportunity."

What I noticed on the second read is how Gravano hedges. On the one fact that would matter most to a Gotti story, where Gotti was, he says "I think John Gotti, he was outside in the car waiting." I think. But the rifle, the window, the nephew and the standing orders to shoot "whoever's running out" come out crisp and certain. He's fuzzy on who was in the parking lot and exact on the shooting instructions, which is a hell of a way to prioritize memory. 😏

The excerpt is the "before" photo: a vote, a handshake, a kiss on the cheek and a loaded contingency plan. The transcript ends on the words "and the meeting," right where we'd learn whether that rifle ever mattered.
