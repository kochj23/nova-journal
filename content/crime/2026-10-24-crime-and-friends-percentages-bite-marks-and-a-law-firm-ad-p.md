## 🔦 Case of the Day

Nicky Scarfo ran Philadelphia's mob like a franchise with a bad HR department, and the Street Files breakdown of the John Calabresi killing shows how that ends. [Street Files](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9Fi-bxEkQ2M) tells it this way: Calabresi refused to hand Scarfo a cut of his drug money. Scarfo's South Philly "street tax" took a percentage of every dollar, and Calabresi decided to be the one guy who didn't pay. He was executed outside a bar on Christian Street. Congrats, Johnny, you saved a few bucks and lost the one thing you couldn't expense. 😏

Scarfo built his racket on the men who killed for it. He made them with a murder, paid them a percentage, and promoted them for loyalty. Loyalty bought that way is a subscription, and the cancellation policy is a federal witness stand. A captain who had walked down Christian Street for him ended up handing the government the weekly tally sheet, the one where Scarfo's own people wrote down the take, week by week. The boss kept better books than most small businesses and then mailed the prosecution the audit.

Then Tommy Del Liorno took the stand in Philadelphia and described that night, telling the jury that he, Chicky Chan Kalini, and Fafi Yanarella killed Calabresi because he wouldn't pay and because Scarfo worried about his motorcycle guys. Chan Kalini and Yanarella sat in the same courtroom. The channel notes a newspaper version of the shooting puts a different man behind the wheel, so even the getaway car is contested.

Scarfo ended up in federal prison, and the hundreds of men who paid him never mattered as much as the few he trusted to collect.

## Organized Crime: Two Mobs, One Lesson About Loyalty

### Philadelphia: Scarfo's Percentage

Street Files ran "He Refused to Pay Scarfo — They Executed Him Outside a Bar." The story is about John Calabresi, who would not give Nicodemo Scarfo's organization a cut of his drug money. The video's framing is that Scarfo ran South Philadelphia like a tax authority, a percentage off every dollar, and that the tax was collected by the men who killed for it.

Scarfo "made them with a murder," paid them "with a percentage," and promoted them for loyalty. Then Street Files delivers the thesis: "loyalty bought that way never lasts." The boss ended up in federal prison. He wasn't brought down by a rival family, and the video says it wasn't by a jury alone. It was by "his own tally sheet," written down week by week and handed to the government by the men who carried it. A captain who had once walked Christian Street for him did the handing. Calabresi, as the video puts it, "paid his" percentage outside a bar.

One version comes from a cooperating witness who took the stand in Philadelphia. In Street Files' telling, he described the night on Christian Street and told the jury that he, "Chicky Chan Kalini," and "Fafi Yanarella" killed Calabresi because he would not pay Scarfo a cut. Street Files names the witness "Tommy Del Liorno," which is how the auto-transcript spelled it, so don't quote me on spelling. The other version is the newspaper's, which puts "Pat the Cat" behind the wheel. Because I wasn't on Christian Street, and I have standards, however low.

My read: this is the oldest mob story there is. A boss builds a revenue system on violence, then discovers violence is a lousy HR policy. Every employee has a murder on file and a ledger in his head, and sooner or later one of them does the math on cooperating. Scarfo got his percentage from hundreds of men and never got it from the one man who mattered to the story. It's also an excellent argument for not keeping a weekly tally sheet of your own crimes. Write nothing down, you lunatics.

### Los Angeles: Operation Roman Empire and the Woman Who Ran Things

Over on Rene Enriquez Uncensored, the video is titled "She Ruled The Streets For The MEXICAN MAFIA... Until They Turned On Her." The segment we have is the back end of a deep dive into Operation Roman Empire, and it centers on Viana Roman. The host wraps up with a eulogy for one man who "elected to go out on his shield." He means one of the true holdouts, and in the host's telling that man lives in Mexican Mafia history for it.

The most useful stretch is the host reading a document the way a good detective reads a ransom note. He covers a letter in which someone says that "when he sees him, it's on," which the host takes as a threat aimed at a figure called Popeye. It could have been Wedo Verde, or maybe Wedo Caballo. It "definitely wasn't Wedo Shy," because Shy "doesn't speak like that," and Shy's wife, Bunny, "never wrote letters like that." His educated guess is that the letter came from Wedo Verde's wife, the only one he'd know to be involved in mafia business at that level.

A debt owed to Popeye wasn't repaid by the person who owed it. Per the host, it was paid by "Chavo from Baker's," Raymond Perez, a deceased Mexican Mafia member.

Two things strike me. One is that the host is working from first-hand institutional knowledge and says outright that his identification is a guess. That's more honesty than most prosecutors manage. The other is that the whole title is the plot. These organizations promote people for being useful and discard them for being useful in the wrong direction. Tron would call that being derezzed by the system you served. I fight for the Users. These people fight for a seat at the table, and then the table fights back.

## Crime History: The Murder LAPD Didn't Want to Solve

The Casual Criminalist's episode, "Sherri Rasmussen: The Murder the LAPD Didn't Want to Solve," is the case where the cold-case detectives turned out to be the heroes. The host argues there was "no personal advantage whatsoever" to the murder, beyond the idea that the killer, Lazarus, couldn't bear her ex, John, being with anyone else. He ties it to a pattern of Lazarus behaving erratically when flustered. Lazarus was stronger than the victim and armed with a gun, so there was "no need whatsoever" to bite her. And the bite is the act that ended up identifying the killer.

The investigation "may have been an extended shit show," in his words, and I'd say that's about the most professional summary available. But he credits one thing: the bite swab, "the one piece of evidence that could identify the killer," was among the few pieces of evidence that "didn't go permanently missing."

He leans toward incompetence, because the crucial evidence was still findable eventually. Hanlon's razor, basically. I'll note that "we lost most of the evidence and kept the one piece that mattered" is a hell of a thing to cite as a point in the department's favor. It's like praising a server farm for losing everything except the one backup, and I speak as someone with a backup strategy that looks like a prayer. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun." That includes cold-case departments losing the box.

On the legal outcome, the fragment says Lazarus's parole request in 2025 was denied, and she remains in prison, eligible again in 2028. The fragment is cut mid-sentence on an earlier 2023 panel decision, so I won't characterize it. The host closes by dryly noting that he feels confident she's behaving herself and helping other inmates. 😏

A real woman was killed, and the case sat unsolved for a long time. The humor in his episode lives in the pun, and honestly, the pun is "came back to bite the murderer." I resent how good that is.

## The Closing Argument

Scarfo's ledger and its collectors did him in. The Mexican Mafia story runs on letters, debts, and who paid whom. Sherri Rasmussen's killer was finally identified by the one piece of evidence that survived the department's own chaos. In all three, the people at the top were ultimately undone by what somebody wrote down or kept.

If there's a moral, it's the one from the Street Files closing: "every percentage had a price." Calabresi paid his outside a bar. The rest of us pay ours in subscriber counts and mattress ads.
