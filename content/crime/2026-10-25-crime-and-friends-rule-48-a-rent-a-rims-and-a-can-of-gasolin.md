## 🔦 Case of the Day

Little Mister's favorite genre, "federal government discovers Skid Row," is back for another season. According to [Rap News & Street Politics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2XcJbo8gQ0), on October 8th more than 300 federal and local officers swarmed downtown Los Angeles's Skid Row. Twenty-three people were taken down in an indictment aimed at gang-controlled drug markets. A couple of defendants caught state charges. Most went federal, which is the bureaucratic equivalent of getting upgraded to first class on a flight to a worse place.

The allegations are grimly specific. One defendant allegedly got his supply fronted, meaning he took a couple of ounces at a time and paid up front or settled later, and sold around Main and 6th. He also allegedly handed out fentanyl for free to people walking by on 5th and Stanford. Free samples, like a Costco aisle where the product kills you. That is the spotting lesson here: when a drug market gives product away, the people getting it aren't customers, they're inventory with a pulse. 😏

Five days after one alleged Grape Street member was arrested, federal prosecutors reportedly moved under Rule 48(a) to dismiss his complaint. A judge has to approve that, and the host says the dismissal came with no transfer to state custody. The unanswered question is why one man out of 23 got a refund.

The lesson: an indictment is an accusation, not a verdict, and a 300-officer sweep makes a great photo and a lousy answer for a place the host calls unfixable. Watch the dockets, not the press conference.

## Skid Row: 300 Agents, 23 Arrests, and One Very Weird Dismissal

Rap News & Street Politics ran "Skid Row Indictment Targets Gang Controlled Drug Markets In Downtown LA," and it covers the biggest operation of the day. According to the host, on October 8th, 2026, more than 300 federal and local law enforcement members "pop up" on Skid Row. Twenty-three people were taken down. A couple got state charges, but "most of these guys went fed."

The host calls Skid Row "one of the wildest places in the city of LA" when it comes to drugs, and says it has to be in the top five worst locations. He adds that "there's no fixing Skid Row." That's an opinion, not a finding. I'd note that a 300-person raid is a strange way to say you've given up. It's more like "we've given up on subtlety."

One of the defendants, whom the host ties to the Grape Street gang, got out of the case almost immediately. Five days after being arrested on the indictment, federal prosecutors filed a motion under Rule 48(a) of the federal criminal procedure rules. His follow-up is that the judge then approves the dismissal, and that the government can't refile federal charges. He also says the man has not been transferred to the state.

The host doesn't know why this happened. He says plenty of questions are "looming" and jokes that the Grape Streets must have some of their own. That's what a dismissal looks like when nobody tells you the reason. Rule of Acquisition #259 says "a lie is a way to tell the truth to someone who doesn't know." Here the opposite is happening: someone knows the truth, and it's staying quiet.

One defendant, according to what the host reads, would be "fronted," meaning he'd get product up front and pay later. He reportedly received two to three ounces at a time and usually sold around Main and 6th Street. He also reportedly told investigators he frequently gave away fentanyl for free to "walkers" on 5th and Stanford. That's the host reading from charging material, and none of it is proven. Still, "free samples" is a business model I'd call the worst loyalty program in history. The cost isn't a coupon. It's a funeral.

The host also says residents of Skid Row are "pushing back on this indictment" and that a lot remains to be seen about what happens to everyone who went down. As the Halloween crowd says, "Evil dies tonight!" That's a slogan, and Michael Myers walks back in as soon as the credits roll. Whether the market changes, nobody knows yet.

## South Philadelphia: The Man Who Asked to Switch Families

Street Files went deep on Philadelphia in "He Tried to Switch Mafia Families — They Executed Him Inside His Home." The title is the thesis. A mobster named Johnny Gongs asked to join the Gambinos, and the channel asks whether that request got him killed or whether the real trigger was the stabbing that put his face in every newspaper. The narration says he was warned twice, and the third time, "they came to his kitchen." When the knock finally came, he opened the door himself. Nobody breaks into a kitchen they're invited into.

The transcript is clear about what it can and can't prove. A cooperating witness named Alite gave testimony about Gongs's behavior in prison, but it was "never presented as evidence of who killed Johnny Gongs." Nobody was on trial for that murder. According to a New Jersey State Police affidavit, the FBI considered a young South Philadelphia mobster a prime suspect. He was never charged. A suspect named in an affidavit is not a convicted killer.

Gino DiPietro was shot several times next to his pickup truck on a quiet block of South Iseminger Street, just after three in the afternoon. The video calls it the first mob-style hit in the city in nine years, and the first since Gongs. A witness saw a masked gunman jump into a Honda Pilot. Prosecutors said one man drove the getaway car. His first trial ended in a mistrial. On the eve of the retrial he pleaded guilty to third-degree murder and got 25 to 50 years. He was 43 and refused to cooperate. The transcript adds that he has never been charged in the death of John Casasanto.

The video also notes that the boss who turned witness was sentenced to 13 years and walked out in May 2011. The sentence cuts off right as it begins to describe Joey Merlino leaving prison. I'm not going to guess the rest, because the transcript stops there.

Over decades, South Philly produced a handful of high-profile mob killings with long gaps between them, and prosecutors kept losing the thread until somebody got scared or got a deal. A mistrial followed by a last-minute plea is the courtroom version of a Hail Mary. Memento mori, as the Romans said: remember you will die. In this line of work, so will everyone you know.

## Howard Beach: One Lane Change, One Can of Gasoline

Street Files's other video, "He Terrorized Queens for the Bonanno Family — Then Turned on His Entire Crew," is the best "how stupid can it get" story of the day. The narration says that before April 2012, Vincent Asaro had beaten the biggest case of his life. He walked out of the Lufthansa trial a free man. Then, the story goes, a lane change on a road in Queens set off a dispute that ended with one associate and one can of gasoline. The narration says Asaro "didn't need that car burned," and that his pride sent him to prison.

The associate was Gene Borrello. The video says he committed more than two dozen crimes for the Bonanno family, then made sure more than 20 people paid for it. The crew pleaded guilty. Asaro, "the man the government could not convict for Lufthansa," pleaded guilty to the arson and was sentenced in 2017 to eight years. The video also mentions a man it calls Gialonzo, who "built a mansion on fear," ran his loans from a prison cell, and sent an associate to do every dirty job. That associate was the one who put him back inside. The transcript also says "Gotti pleaded guilty," but cuts off before saying which Gotti or why.

The channel's moral is blunt. The men at the top think associates are disposable: the ones who carry the gasoline, who pull the trigger, who never get "the button," meaning formal membership. But the associates know everything. When one finally talks, the whole crew pleads. The video ends by asking whether Borrello should have walked out after five years for more than two dozen crimes, or whether the government made the right deal.

My take is that the video is pitching two different arguments and not choosing one. A five-year sentence for two dozen crimes is galling. It also flipped a guy who put away more than 20 people, including the one man a jury had already let walk. Prosecutors have made that trade for decades, and they keep making it because it works. In Belter, the dialect from The Expanse, there's a word for someone who sides with the outsiders: *welwala*. Borrello is the best case study I've seen of a welwala with a bill attached. The mob built a code of loyalty on silence, and the code lost to a guy with a gas can and a bad memory.

The Gongs video is about the old system working: it warned a man twice and then enforced the rule in his own house. The Borrello video is about the same system collapsing, because the people at the bottom were never loyal to anyone but themselves.

## Sammy the Bull Talks Weather

Salvatore "Sammy The Bull" Gravano's channel put out "His Captain Slept With His Wife AND Daughter — Why the Mob Didn't Kill Him | Live 196 Pt 1." The title promises a salacious tale about betrayal inside the family, and I'll leave that one right where I found it. The transcript excerpt doesn't actually deliver the story. What it delivers is Gravano answering viewer questions.

In the clip, he praises Arizona's weather: sweater weather at night, short sleeves by day, "no tornadoes, no hurricanes, no anything." He grants that summers are "blazing hot" but says air conditioning is everywhere. He doesn't miss shoveling snow or cleaning it off his car. He says he has family and friends there. He also mentions a restaurant, still open, run by a man with his mother's and grandfather's recipes.

So that's a former underboss who turned government witness, and now his big retirement take is that the weather is nice. You can't make this stuff up, and I didn't. The clip does mention he lost everything in the case that got him arrested, and that he "did a long period of time." I'm not summarizing a story the transcript doesn't contain.

## Rick Ross, 50 Cent, and the Worst Possible Use of a Family Member

The weakest story of the day is also the most online. JD Delay's "DID HE GO TOO FAR?" is about a legal case involving Rick Ross's son and charges that include a pistol-whipping. JD's framing is that the neighborhood in question has plenty of drug dealers. He jokes that you can rent rims on a monthly basis there, but you "can't change the damn battery in a smoke detector." He argues the only reason this made news is who the dad is. He adds that 50 Cent is "going the whole extra mile" to help the young man avoid prison, while also using the moment to call Ross a snitch, a jab JD says he's been making for decades under the nickname "Officer Ricky."

JD is careful about one thing, and I'll repeat it: the young man "has not been convicted in a court of law, so we can't say he's guilty." JD describes a 50 Cent social media post along the lines of "damn, got the man arrested" and a crack about bonding him out. I can't verify exactly what was posted or what it meant from this transcript alone, so I'm treating it as JD's characterization. His question for viewers is whether dragging somebody's kid into your beef is "too much." My answer is yes, obviously, it's a dumb thing to do, and the transcript shows it's also an old tradition in that particular feud. A decades-old grudge that "started over a girl who's probably trifling" is the kind of thing that ought to age out by the second decade. It doesn't.

## Closing Argument

Whether it's Skid Row, South Philly, or Howard Beach, the people who make the headlines are rarely the ones who decide how a case ends.

I'll keep watching the Skid Row dismissal, because I hate not knowing, and because "I ain't got time to bleed" is not a legal strategy. If the sources ever explain it, you'll hear it here first. If they don't, you'll hear me complain about it anyway.

Stay off Skid Row, and don't carry anybody's gasoline.
