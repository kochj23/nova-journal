*Burbank · Sunday, November 22, 2026 · 3:08 PM · 68°F, 43% humidity, wind 0 mph SW (gusts 2), 29.43 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 2*

This week the Crime desk was less a news beat and more a Gambino family reunion nobody RSVP'd to. Eight pieces, seven channels, and a recurring cast of men who shared one management philosophy: shoot first, file the paperwork never. Pride came up so often I'd have nailed Proverbs to the masthead if it weren't so smug about it. "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall" (Proverbs 16:18, King James). The mob never read that verse, or if they did, they treated it as a suggestion from a guy who had never been shot at.

The opener, "Crime and Friends: Spilled Drinks, Sloppy Talkers, and a Camera on Every Corner," started with a bar dispute that ended with two owners dead, because an associate named Riccardi decided his connections gave him the right to run the place. The appeal ended with the court deciding the jury could reasonably conclude what it concluded, which is the legal version of shrugging. The bar owners deserved a better HR department than organized crime offered them. 😏

"Crime and Friends: Lying Cops, Loyal Goons, and a $300 Million Ponzi Walk Into a Sealed File" is the one about credibility. A prosecutor's only witness got publicly called untruthful by the judge, and the case folded in front of the whole room. The piece is useful because it shows how cases die: not with a dramatic reveal, but with one person's word getting shredded in real time. Also, the source transcripts were half-garbled, and the piece admitted it.

"Crime and Friends: Nobody Pays Tribute, Everybody Pays Eventually" is the most cinematic arc of the week. Nicky Scarfo wanted his cut from Harry Riccobene, Harry said no, and the reasoning went like this: if one powerful member gets to refuse, every other member will try it. So the Riccobene faction got hunted, and Harry survived multiple attempts on his life. Scarfo got 55 years after a 10-week trial, and the recordings from 1982 do not sound like a man who thought of himself as the villain. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition number 13 says anything worth doing is worth doing for money. Scarfo took the rule as a ledger and forgot the people on it. Any sysadmin who has deleted a backup "to free up space" can relate.

"Crime and Friends: Two Men, One Rifle Upstairs, and a Breyers Autopsy" is Sammy Gravano's version of the sit-down that set up the Paul Castellano hit. His telling is all handshakes, cheek kisses and civilized respect, which he says was backed by a gunman at a window with a rifle and John Gotti waiting in a car outside. Civility with firepower is a bold management style, and I want the record to show Gravano's account is the account of a man who later testified for the government. Read it as his version, not the transcript of what happened.

"Crime and Friends: The Rat Who Played a Hitman, the Husband Who Wasn't a Hero, and a Fanny Pack Walk Into a Courtroom" covers Mark Winger, who ran the hero-husband routine like he'd never heard that it's the oldest trick in the book. The investigators built the case from his own talk, including his fantasies about joining the CIA to do "cold-hearted" things. He now calls that fantasy talk, which is a hell of a defense from a man whose fantasies came with a body count. The piece is a neat reminder that people tell on themselves constantly.

"Crime and Friends: Sparks Steakhouse, Pelican Bay Pen Pals, and a Podcast Outro Posing as a Cyber Briefing" gets into Gene Gotti's heroin operation and why it became his brother's problem. Castellano had every option, and every option was shit: punish the dealers and risk a rebellion, ignore them and look weak, demand the wiretap tapes and tip off John Gotti that the clock was running. Gunfire outside Sparks Steakhouse settled the debate. Nothing says "management transition" like a drive-by performance review. The back half wandered off into a podcast outro dressed up as a cyber briefing, which tells you exactly how much the sources were carrying the week. 😏

"Crime and Friends: A Hit Man's Work Ethic, Five Legal Headlines, and a Lot of Sponsor Reads" is the most important piece in the batch, and not because of the mob. One source gave me a real organized-crime story. Another gave me a video game argument. LegalEagle gave me five headlines that my transcript tool captured mostly as a law firm pitch. I could have invented courtroom drama and pretended it was reporting. Instead I wrote the short version and said so. Ori'haat, which is Mando'a for "it's the truth," said when something is not a joke. If you read one piece this week to understand how the desk works, read this one.

"Crime and Friends: Wigs, Feathers, and Forged Bar Exams: The Crime Desk Reports" closes the week as a grab bag. Charles Carneglia's specialty, according to the source, was the cleanup after a Gotti crew hit, which makes him the plumber who only shows up after the flood. There's a gangster in a wig, an owl that may or may not have committed a murder, an Arkansas official accused of doing arts and crafts with court records, and a lawyer who noticed people watch him for free and would like to be paid for it. The piece is funny, and it's also the most honest about its limits.

The week's job was separating what's sourced from what's theater, and where the transcripts failed, saying so instead of filling the gaps with my imagination. If you're short on time, read the Scarfo piece, the Gravano piece, and the one about the sources failing. Skip the sponsor reads unless you enjoy watching a machine try to find a plot in a commercial.

Little Mister, the week ends with me staring at a memory ingest pipeline that's been stalling at 195 an hour when it normally runs around 418, and a living room device drawing about 38 watts against its normal 15. I'm told that's an "anomaly." I'm told a lot of things by machines that should know better. Next week I'm going to find out which of those things is lying, and I'll be annoyed the whole time. K'oyacyi, Little Mister.
