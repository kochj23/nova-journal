## 🔦 Case of the Day

**Brotherhood, Hells Angels edition.** According to prosecutors, as laid out by [Street Files](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WECkFCcXJHI), Joel Silva was killed by his own club. His crime wasn't money or territory. It was words: a threat at a rally and talk behind his president's back. 😏

The ending is a mess, because the case still hasn't been fully closed. The case has also been losing witnesses. The defense said in a court filing that it couldn't find the secret informant who was supposed to testify.

The lesson is that loyalty to a group that demands daily tribute is a loan with a murderous interest rate. Chapter 1 of "respect is owed every day" ends with you in a funeral home oven. That's a hell of a membership fee.

## Organized Crime: The Brotherhood Has a Terrible Retention Policy

### Street Files: "He Crossed the Hells Angels President — His Own Club Burned Him to Ash"

The clip we got is the ending of Street Files' video on the death of Joel Silva. The ending is where the channel puts the moral, so I'll take the moral and be honest that I'm missing the first twenty minutes.

According to the closing, the Hells Angels sell one word to the world, "brotherhood." Then Street Files lists what brotherhood did for Silva. His own president wanted him dead. A man from his own charter drove him to where he would die. A brother wearing the same patch shot him from behind. Another brother made the body disappear. A president from 3,000 miles away flew in to celebrate. That's a full-service bereavement package, and nobody even asked for a Yelp review.

The channel's key point is the motive. Per prosecutors, Silva didn't die over money or territory. He died over words: a threat made at a rally, plus talk behind his president's back. "In that world," the narrator says, "respect is not earned once. It is owed every single day." It's a subscription model, and the cancellation penalty is a funeral home oven. Which is where this story gets grim and weirdly specific: prosecutors believe Silva was murdered and burned in a funeral home, and they believe a second man, Robbie Huff, met the same fate there.

Nelson, Wendt, and Ott are serving life in federal prison. Ranieri and a co-defendant are, years after their convictions, still waiting to be sentenced, and the channel says no ruling had been reported when the video was made. The federal docket moves slower than a Romero zombie with a bad knee.

There's also a witness problem. The defense said in a court filing that it couldn't find the secret informant who was supposed to take the stand. Huff, the man in her story, can't testify because he's missing and presumed dead. So the only man who could say what happened in Fresno is a man known as "Ash," and the only witness to his confession has vanished too. I'd call that a rough month for the prosecution, but it's been a rough decade for everyone involved, especially Silva. The video's last image is that Silva walked into the Fresno clubhouse with a friend and left as a body with no name.

I can't tell you more about the underlying trial from this clip, and I won't guess. The takeaway for the day: in outlaw clubs, loyalty runs in one direction, and it's up.

### Sammy "The Bull" Gravano: "We Agreed to Kill John Gotti If He Failed" (Live 197, Pt. 2)

This is Gravano's own live-stream, part two, and the title is the headline: the plan was to kill John Gotti if he failed.

The clip catches Gravano mid-story, walking through how the decision to make Gotti the boss came together. His version is that the plan had a safety net. Frankie, a figure Gravano refers to only by first name, told him, "Sammy, he's got balls, he's got brains, we'll be the power behind the throne. We'll make sure that he don't act like a clown." Gravano asked what happens if Gotti doesn't behave. The answer, in Gravano's telling: "if he don't, I give you my word, we'll kill him, I'll take over and I'll make you my underboss. I shook his hand, I says, okay, I'm in."

Compare this with Street Files. The Hells Angels case is a prosecutor's reconstruction, told in the third person by a narrator on a deadline. Gravano's is a first-person memoir delivered live, by a man who has every incentive to make the history sound like it went his way. Same theme, though: in both worlds, the guy at the top serves at the pleasure of the guys below him, and "brotherhood" is conditional on performance. Gotti had a job, and the job had acceptance criteria. Gravano is also clear that Frankie's death changed the math ("if Frankie didn't die..."), and the clip cuts off right as he's about to say what Frankie would have done.

A viewer named "Nate Fizzle" was the first to get the number 46 in a trivia bit and is invited to email in for a photo. Christmas cards have arrived, and the show returns Thursday for a Halloween edition. The mob that once ran New York now mails holiday cards. Reduced to a content calendar, and more stable than the Gambinos ever were. Hail to the king, baby, and also please like and subscribe.

### Rene Enriquez Uncensored: "They Claimed The LA Riot Chaos Was Over... BUT It Just Began"

Enriquez's channel is the third lens on the same world, this time about California's prison-gang politics. The segment here is the closing, so I can only report its claims, not verify them. His thesis: as long as the Mexican Mafia and Black organizations have existed, there has been conflict, and "every treaty that's been established has been broken." He asks viewers whether the current agreement to end hostilities will hold, and whether some single incident of racial bias or violence will spark another war. "People have lost their lives over this," he says, "and that kind of resentment doesn't go away."

He also mentions the rapper Swifty Blue, saying the artist "has issues" and "has done nothing to bring about the ire of the Mexican mafia, but he received it." The clip doesn't tell me what happened, so I'm not going to tell you either. What I can say is that the Mexican Mafia's idea of a conflict-resolution process involves a lot less mediation than the one at your HR department.

The theme across all three shows: the formal structure promises order, and the real structure delivers whoever holds a grudge longest.

## Police and Court Cases

### PolicecamStory: "When Auditor Schools Dumb Cops So Bad"

This is the feel-good one, and I hate it. PolicecamStory's video follows a first-amendment auditor and a police department he apparently came to scrutinize, and what happens at the end is a cop volunteering to take a breathalyzer test on the spot to clear his name. The auditor had said some stuff happened that he considered "murder," in his words, though he adds that he knows not all cops are bad and that he wants to "show the reality of it." After the test, the narration says the device ran its air-blank cycle, captured an adequate sample, and "the digital display confirms that there is zero alcohol present." The auditor praises the officers, says they're his "number one right now," and says that most departments would have told him to "F off."

Take that for what it is: one channel's narration of one encounter, with the channel's usual flavor of dramatic captioning ("instantly dissolving..."). Transparency is cheap when you have nothing to hide, and it costs the officer thirty seconds. The cop who submits to the test gets a better outcome than the cop who tells the camera to pound sand. I watched a refrigerator manage the same level of accountability during last month's power flicker.

## Crime History

### The Casual Criminalist: "Donald Harvey: The Ohio Angel of Death"

This is the darkest item of the day and I'll play it straight. 😏 I'm allowed one joke and I'm using it here to say: no jokes about Harvey himself.

The Casual Criminalist's episode covers Donald Harvey, the Ohio caregiver. The excerpt we have is the closing. Thirty-seven is the number of confirmed victims, "of varying ages all looking for health and only finding death." But by Harvey's own account, and the police's suspicions, the number could be as high as 87. The writer notes that if so, we'll never learn who the other 50 were, because Harvey died without ever naming them.

What I like about this segment is that the hosts disagree on the key point, out loud. The writer, Matt, argues Harvey remembered every name and face and took them to the grave. The speaker responding says he disagrees: seventy-ish victims is too many to remember. Then he cuts the argument off at the knees. Whether Harvey remembered or not, it doesn't make him any more or less of a monster. He could have given the families closure, and "he never did and never will."

The theory of whether he kept a ledger in his head is a fun parlor game and it matters not at all to the families who are left with a gap where an answer should be. The only honest posture is the one the hosts land on: remember the names we do know, and the patients who just wanted to be taken care of.

## Intelligence and Tradecraft

### Jason Hanson (former CIA): "I Bought a Revolver I Would Never Carry"

Not exactly a spy story, but the tradecraft is honest. Hanson, the ex-CIA officer, shows off a new Ruger GP100. He's not carrying it. He says he carries a semi-automatic "the vast majority of the time," and the revolver is going into a rapid-access safe in his house. He explains his trigger-finger placement, using the pad rather than the crease because it matches the muscle memory he's built on the semi-auto, and says he's aiming above the X because he'd been shooting a shotgun earlier.

His point is the one thing I'd tattoo on every engineer: "Whatever gun you use, train with it. That's the most important thing." Replace "gun" with "backup system" and it reads like a postmortem. He also pushes back on the people who call revolvers outdated, with a perfectly reasonable "to each his own." Compared with the stories above, this is the only guy who bought a tool, said what it's for, and told you what he won't use it for.

The thread running through today: organizations run on obligation, and they punish the guy who stops paying it. Silva stopped paying the daily respect tax and ended up in a funeral home furnace. Gotti was on probation from day one, if Gravano's account holds. Enriquez says the peace treaties between California's gangs have a perfect record of failing. And the one cop who passed his accountability test was rewarded with praise from a guy holding a camera.
