## 🔦 Case of the Day

Turns out the best witness against a New York street gang was the guy the gang itself had framed. The Westies, the Irish crew that ran Hell's Kitchen, handed prosecutors their own undoing, and the gentrifiers finished the job. Per [Street Files](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hv6mQjpahxE), it started on April 25, 1985. Michael Holley, an ironworker who'd taken a job at the Javits site, was shot on 35th Street near 11th Avenue. The legend says the construction job got him killed. The gang actually blamed him for an old killing, and the job site had nothing to do with it. Nothing says "tight-knit organization" like murdering a guy over a grudge and then blaming the paperwork. 😏

Mickey Featherstone was convicted in March 1986, and this time he was innocent. The conviction was later thrown out. He concluded his own gang had framed him and went to the prosecutors. The Times Magazine wrote that perhaps nothing would have stopped the Westies had Holley not been murdered. In 1988, Jimmy Coonan got 75 years in federal prison, with a reported mandatory release date of June 1, 2030.

The lesson is that a crew built on loyalty and fear has no fallback plan once the fear stops working. Frame your own guy and he'll walk into the DA's office with a grudge and a good memory. One top Manhattan prosecutor even said gentrification may have done more to end the Westies than law enforcement did, which is a hell of a thing to put on a résumé. 😏

## Organized Crime and Mob History

### The Westies: Ending the Hell's Kitchen Myth

Street Files ran "He Refused to Surrender Hell's Kitchen — Fat Tony Butchered Him." I'm only working from the back end of the transcript, so I can't speak to what the title promises. It picks up with the ironworker Michael Holley, who took a job at the Javits site and was shot on 35th Street near 11th Avenue on April 25, 1985. The gang blamed him for an old killing. The narrator is firm that the job site had nothing to do with it, which pushes back on the "legends" version of the story.

A New York Times Magazine article, as the video tells it, said that perhaps nothing would have stopped the Westies had Holley not been murdered.

The video says the gang that took over from Mickey Spillane came apart over a murder a few steps from the building he's said to have died for. That is dark and tidy, and I hate how much I enjoy it. A 1987 article opened one section by saying there was no Hell's Kitchen anymore. The Irish bars were turning into French bistros and the tenements were going co-op. So the mob wasn't brought down by the FBI. It was brought down by a brunch menu. Pride goeth before destruction, as Proverbs 16:18 has it, and apparently so does the arrival of a croissant place.

The video also covers where the family ended up: a son who became a lawyer and Democratic district leader, and bars in the neighborhood, one named after his father. It reads like a family that went legit and stayed local.

### The Mexican Mafia and the Poway Takedown

Rene Enriquez Uncensored posted "They Opened Fire On Federal SWAT... Then Learned How The Feds Retaliate." The transcript snippet is the end of the episode and a press conference roll call. He walks through the "extensive reach of the Mexican mafia." He says he's shown "a number of instances" where the Mexican Mafia infiltrated tribes for "nefarious activities." The reasoning is that Native Americans are typically seen as independent peoples with no gang involvement, so the tribes make a convenient front.

Per Enriquez, the director of corrections can ship them out. It's "typically done for witness protection, but they also do it for problematic inmates," and it's "been done plenty of times."

He reads off a list that includes the FBI's San Diego Field Office, the San Diego County Under Sheriff, a California Highway Patrol Assistant Chief, the police departments of Oceanside and Carlsbad, an Assistant United States Attorney who'd deliver remarks in Spanish, and the Deputy SAC for Homeland Security Investigations. He pauses to explain that SAC means "special agent in charge," not "a brown paper bag," and calls the lineup "heavy hitters in law enforcement." A press conference this crowded means someone is having a very bad week. He calls it an "extensive, extensive press conference on the Poway Indian takedown." That's all I've got on the case itself, and I won't guess at charges or numbers the transcript doesn't give.

## Intelligence and Security Tradecraft

### Jason Hanson Wants Your Cash in a Flashlight

Former CIA officer Jason Hanson posted "8 Household Items That Hide Your Cash in Plain Sight." The advice is simple. Take a big flashlight, empty out the batteries, and stuff it with cash. His words: put it "with the other 8 million flashlights I own, and nobody's gonna know." He also pitches a fake can of WD-40 that, per the fine print, "is not the exact same as real WD-40," and says you could stash "thousands upon thousands of dollars" in it with your tools. He mentions a lint roller in passing, as the video cuts off mid-sentence.

Nobody has ever burgled a house and thought "let me check the flashlight." But let's address the 8 million flashlights. The man has a flashlight problem. Little Mister, I live in a house with 33 Hue lights and a Mac Studio, and even I think that's excessive.

Then the pivot, which is the best part. He closes with a plug for a gold and silver dealer, saying "there's way too many scammers out there" but this company is the one he uses. There's a scam-adjacent joke in a CIA veteran telling you to hide cash in a fake WD-40 can and then sending you to a sales page. I'm not accusing anyone of anything. I'm noting that "trust me, I'm a former spy, buy gold" is the exact sentence a scammer would also say. Verify, then trust. Stuff your mattress if you like, but the advice at the end of a video is rarely free.

### Kim Kardashian's Lawyer Show, Graded

The one LegalEagle video with real legal content in my packet is "Real Lawyer Reacts to Kim Kardashian's Lawyer Show (All's Fair)." It's the best bit in today's lawyer pile. He grades the show a "D plus" and says "it's clear there's a reason why she hasn't been able to pass the bar." The real crime analysis is a scene where characters threaten to release photos of "a very private nature" unless someone signs a settlement agreement. He calls it "Every single element of extortion under California law" and delivers a sarcastic "Great job. Way to go, Kim." His suggested fix for the victim is to send a litigation hold letter telling them not to delete the photos, then report to the State Bar and the attorney general's office. Fiction teaches us that the extortion happens in a conference room with a slide deck. Real life makes it a text message. The show, as described, made it a slide deck.

### A Driver Lectures a Police Station

PolicecamStory's "When The Driver Is Smarter Than The Police" is a long argument at a station in Bloomington. The driver insists that "I pay for your department" and that police are "tax recipients who work for the public." He objects that when "you pull somebody over and you steal their money because they've done a traffic violation," that isn't how tax revenue works. The channel's summary says he's "hoping to force a change in the officer's conduct." I can't tell you the underlying stop, so I can't rule on who's right. I can say that "I pay your salary" has never once in recorded history ended a police encounter faster.
