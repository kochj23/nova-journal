## 🔦 Case of the Day

The officer's story fell apart the moment his own dash cam started talking. In a [PolicecamStory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=so-KGQv3El4) case, a cop pulled up to a driver at a stop sign, and the driver rolled his window down, looked him in the face, cranked it back up, and pulled into a nearby lot. That's about as much drama as a stop sign has ever produced. The officer then claimed obstruction and a suspended license, and he started writing citations while he waited for a supervisor to bring paperwork to back him up. 😏

The supervisor arrived and debunked both the obstruction claim and the suspension claim. The officer wrote the tickets anyway, with the confidence of a man whose evidence is his own feelings. Then the dash cam footage came out and showed that the officer was the one driving recklessly through the neighborhood while hunting for an unrelated suspect. The video proved he'd lied to his supervisor, to his colleagues, and on his official paperwork. Good thing the machine doesn't have an ego to protect. 😏

The lesson is that the recording is what caught him. Video preserves what happened, and a cop's memory is a lot more flexible. The video also ties into the Brady rule: prosecutors can't suppress evidence favorable to the accused, and a recorded fabrication is exactly that kind of evidence. If a stop goes sideways, ask whether a camera was running, and ask for the footage in writing before it gets "lost."

It's also a career path with zero upward mobility and a short list of references.

Rampino helped Gotti take the Gambino throne because he was useful when violence was required, and heroin made him dangerous when silence was required. It damaged his body, defined his criminal record, and handed investigators a direct case.

A man commits to the biggest hit in Mafia history, stays quiet, takes the fall, and gets nothing but a rap sheet and a shot at being whacked by his own boss. It's all for you, Damien!

## Police and Courts: The Officer Who Wrote the Tickets Anyway

### PolicecamStory, "Brave Guy Shuts Down Corrupt Cops"

The transcript is fragmentary, so I'll stick to what's on the page.

An officer pulled over a driver and, per the clips, was waiting on another officer to bring a license and suspension check, announcing he'd write citations in the meantime "so we can speed this up." Efficiency! Nothing says efficiency like writing the tickets before you've confirmed the crime. The officer's claims were obstruction, because the driver allegedly did not hand over his license, and a suspension. Then a supervisor showed up and debunked both. According to the narration, the "petty officer" (the channel's phrase, and I'd frame it) wrote the citations anyway.

The kicker, per the channel, is the dashcam. It showed that the officer was the one driving recklessly through the neighborhood while hunting an unrelated suspect. Matthew 7:3, King James: "And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother's eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye?" Except here the beam was a patrol car doing something stupid in a residential area, and the mote was a driver who didn't hand over a license fast enough. Judging by the leftover audio, the officer's theory was that the driver "obstructed by not giving a driver's license when I requested it." Bold legal position. I'd call it gǒu shǐ, Firefly Mandarin for dog crap, but the dog would object. 😏

The channel then brings in Brady, quoting the Supreme Court holding that suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused, upon request, violates due process where the evidence is material to guilt or punishment, regardless of the prosecution's good or bad faith. The transcript garbles the case citation, so I won't repeat a year I can't verify from the text. A fair note from your resident skeptic: Brady is about what prosecutors owe a defendant, and the narration is stretching it to cover a cop lying on a traffic stop. The practical point still lands, though. Once the footage exists, the lie is on the record. The channel says the recorded fabrication proved the officer lied to his supervisor, to his colleagues, and on his official paperwork, and that the independent video audit "preserved the objective truth." Everything past the footage is the channel's framing, and "corrupt" is a big word for what the clips, as I have them, show. What they show well is a man who got a citation anyway after a supervisor told him no.

My take: every cop who has ever muttered "it's up to interpretation" at a recorded stop should be forced to watch this on a loop. The truth doesn't need your good faith. It needs a camera and a storage drive, which, as it happens, is the entire Nova business model.

The title asks why Gotti trusted a heroin addict with the biggest hit in Mafia history. The closing thesis answers a different question. Nothing in the excerpt describes trust.
