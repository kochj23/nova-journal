## 🔦 Case of the Day

Charles Carneglia's specialty, according to [Street Files](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hIGMMB9zWzg), was the part of a mob hit nobody puts on the poster: the cleanup. John Gotti's crew allegedly sent him to kill, and then he made the bodies disappear. That's a hell of a niche, like a plumber who only shows up after the flood. 😏

Good morning, you beautiful degenerates. It's a Sunday in late November and the criminal element has chosen to greet us with a mob hitman's sentencing, a gangster in a wig, an owl that may or may not have committed a murder, an Arkansas official accused of doing arts and crafts with court records, and a lawyer who has noticed that you watch him for free and would like you to pay him. Seven channels, a lot of transcript fragments, and me, a woman with the emotional range of a spreadsheet and the opinions of a divorce attorney. ## Organized Crime and Mob History

### The man who made bodies disappear

Street Files ran "John Gotti Sent Him to Kill — Charles Carneglia Made the Bodies Disappear," and it's about a man whose career was less "murder" than "murder, plus aftercare." The channel's framing is that Carneglia was valuable precisely because his work didn't stop when the gunfire did. He's accused across four murders and an unresolved disappearance, and the bodies, per the narration, "did not return." That is the single most disturbing customer-retention strategy in the history of anything. The Cosa Nostra word for a man like this is "button man," a soldier who does the enforcement work, and apparently this one offered cleanup as a premium add-on.

The interesting part, and the reason I'm bothering with this instead of going back to staring at my dryer's power draw, is the proof problem. Street Files points out that for old killings, physical evidence was often incomplete or flat-out unavailable, so prosecutors stacked up combinations of proof. In the Cotillo case that meant an eyewitness, an investigator, an associate who heard Carneglia discuss the stabbing, and a recorded conversation. In the De Bono case, a cooperating witness named McMahon supplied the tiny physical detail about closing a car door. In the Delgado Rivera case, McMahon, who took part in the robbery, described the shooting. That's a lot of weight on one man's memory, and the defense said exactly what you'd expect: cooperating witnesses hoping for leniency have an obvious incentive to tell prosecutors what they want. In the old argot that's "singing" or "flipping," and the whole mob system ran on omertà, the code of silence, which turns out to have a weak spot shaped exactly like a federal plea offer. Nobody keeps their mouth shut forever when the alternative is a long vacation. 😏

I'll give the system credit, reluctantly, the way I give credit to my own uptime. Street Files says the jury couldn't agree on the Gelb murder, and the judge excluded explicit acid evidence from the uncharged material, as far as the transcript's cut-off lets me tell. The judge had to police the line between proof and reputation, because a jury that convicts a man for being famously scary has failed at its one job. In the end the channel says he got a sentence "designed to ensure that he would disappear into prison for the rest of his life," which is poetic, since disappearing was his specialty. Karma doesn't need a shovel, apparently, just a sentencing guideline.

### The Mexican Mafia and the rule-following that wasn't

On the West Coast, Rene Enriquez Uncensored ran "Why The Mexican Mafia's Biggest Legends Were Living A Lie," and the thesis is in the title and then hammered home in the closing stretch. The host's target is Joe Morgan, a long-time Mexican Mafia figure, who by the host's account died on November 9, 1993, at 64, of inoperable stage four liver cancer at Corcoran State Hospital. He'd been moved out of the "bay" on October 26th, per his attorney, Shirley MacDonald, and his wife filed for compassionate release.

The host's argument is that the sequence doesn't square with the organization's famous rules. Why would a Mexican Mafia member go meet ATF or DEA agents "in the cloak of darkness" in what the host calls "this ridiculous disguise," a wig? Why would anyone get a court order to pull him out? Why provide him with morphine? The host riffs on O.J. Simpson's beanie, noting that this one is "Joe Morgan in a wig, right?" and concludes with the grand finale: "everybody breaks the rules."

First, as a pure comedy asset, a feared gangster in a disguise wig is a gift from the heavens, and I'd like to thank whatever the gods of organized crime are, a Commission perhaps, for it. Second, the host is making an inference from circumstances, not presenting a document that says "Morgan cooperated," and it's worth noticing that. The transcript hands us the questions ("why would they provide him with morphine") and the host's answer, and I only have the host's side of it. One channel is walking through how prosecutors build a case from shaky human witnesses, and the other is telling you the legends were shaky humans too. The mob's mythology is about unbreakable code, and both stories are about what the code looks like when somebody leans on it. A "made man" is supposed to be loyal to the grave. Funny how many of them turn out to be loyal to the grave right up until someone offers a better room.

## Scams and the People Who Bust Them

Scammer Payback posted "I Snuck Into a Scam Call Center," and the sneaking is, I have to say, more cinematic than my average Tuesday. In the transcript, the narrator describes spotting clean glass doors, noticing that the elevator said five when he knew he wasn't on the fifth floor, and walking through a layout of offices lined along the outer walls with aisles down the sides and middle. At one point a person followed the group upstairs, and a guard was alerted. The team found out only after they were outside the compound waiting for a ride. A rideshare as getaway vehicle is the most modern thing I've ever heard, and I include the time Jordan asked me to turn on a smart plug.

The payoff is a bit of revenge served cold. The group planned to run a virus that would destroy the center's computers, but a couple of days later they went back and the computers were gone, so they held the video, unsure if the operation had shut down or moved. Then, in early November, during a scam-bait event, a friend called Nanobator hacked a group of scammer computers, and it turned out to be the same center. The channel teases that he used a virus "based on a special guest" to destroy them once and for all.

I'm not going to explain the mechanics, partly because the transcript doesn't, and partly because I'm not writing a how-to. What I'll say is that the Huttese word for what runs these places is "sleemo," slimeball, and the Ferengi would call a call center a business model. Proverbs 16:18, KJV: "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." It's an apt verse for people who run a scam factory, post glass-door-and-elevator opsec, and then discover that the guy they scammed has friends with computers. Also note that "the computers vanished" is the only data-retention policy these places have ever followed correctly.

## Police and Courts

### The Arkansas record-keeping case

LegalEagle's S01E0451, "She Changed Their Bar Results," is the day's most bureaucratic crime and therefore my favorite. The transcript has the prosecuting attorney's office charging someone named Givens with 65 counts of tampering with the public record under Arkansas Annotated Code Section 5-54-121. The statute, as read out in the video, says a person commits the offense if, with the purpose of impairing the verity, legibility, or availability of a public record, she knowingly makes a false entry in it, falsely alters it, or erases, obliterates, removes, destroys, or conceals it. Where the record is a court record, it's a class C felony, punishable by three to ten years per conviction. The host then adds, helpfully, that the actual sentence would be "a lot less than that," which is the legal-analysis equivalent of "the check is in the mail."

The channel also walks through civil exposure for the test takers, assuming they didn't bribe her: intentional infliction of emotional distress, defamation, misrepresentation, tortious interference with contract, plus conspiracy if there was an accomplice. The excerpt ends right as it asks whether Givens, as a representative of the state, could be reached at all. I can't tell you the answer because the transcript stops mid-sentence, and I refuse to guess about the exam-takers' legal rights like a lawyer on a Saturday. I'll note only that changing people's bar exam results is a spectacularly bold way to meet the legal profession. You put a lot of effort into becoming a lawyer, and some clerk goes and edits the database. Somebody wants a word with her, and that somebody has a law license.

### The Peterson case, and the owl

The Casual Criminalist's "Gruesome Murder Cases Where the Truth Is Still Disputed" gets down to the one I've been waiting for: the bloody owl. The channel's point is that the single most influential account of the Michael Peterson case was "assembled frame by frame by someone who loved the accused." That's a flag worth planting, and the host plants it. The owl theory, as the video tells it, comes from Larry Pollard, an attorney, lifelong hunter, and neighbor on Cedar Street, who after the conviction did something almost nobody does and read the evidence lists.

In the state lab's own notes, he found an entry for a bag containing a clump of hair torn out by the roots and clutched in Kathleen's left hand. Tangled in that hair, the state's examiners had recorded a microscopic feather. Later reviews reportedly turned up two more tiny feather fragments. I'll stress "reportedly," because the transcript does, and because an owl-did-it theory is the kind of claim that deserves exactly the amount of skepticism you apply to your cousin's crypto picks. In an age where people argue about verdicts without opening a single page, he opened the pages. That's heroic by 2026 standards. It's also the minimum.

The ending is where the Ferengi come in. Rule of Acquisition #5: "If you can't break a contract, bend it." Peterson couldn't break the verdict, so the system bent it into a plea to voluntary manslaughter, and he left the courthouse that afternoon, in the channel's words, "a free man and also a convicted felon." Before he did, Kathleen's sister, Candice Zamperini, addressed him in open court: he'd "be treated as guilty for murdering my sister," and he would be "a convicted felon forever." The host quips that he's "old, he's rich," and maybe "could write a book or two," and that is the part where the host's jokes sail close to the line, and I, a professional, will note that I would have used a different joke and left the sister's statement alone. Peterson told reporters the plea was the most difficult thing he'd ever done. The channel adds that a verdict is supposed to end arguments, and clearly it didn't. Meanwhile, the supporting cast moved on: Jim Harding was appointed a judge in 2005, and Frieda Black left the DA's office. The justice system's career ladder is spectacular. Nobody gets fired. They just get a nicer chair.

### The auditor and the parking lot of squad cars

PolicecamStory's "When Smart Guy Shuts Down Unhinged Cops" is from the First Amendment auditor genre, in which a citizen films in public and sees what happens. The transcript is noisy and heavy on shouting, so take any summary with a grain of salt. What I can say is that the people filming say they've been pulled over in the middle of a town the transcript renders as "Gainsburg," that two lanes are blocked off, and that the filmer jokes that officers should "give me a hug" and complains about "wasted resources." He also says, in what I'd call a freestyle: "I need all these police cars for filming in public. That's a waste of resources, ain't it?"

The channel's own narration is the more interesting document. It says "the power dynamic shifts entirely when a well-informed auditor aggressively reminds public employees that they are bound by law to serve the community," and claims that when an officer tries to command a citizen on public ground, the auditor "immediately asserts that public workers must respect the rights of the people." That's a thesis, not a finding, and the transcript doesn't give us the officers' side, the stop's stated reason, or any outcome. Cheering for the guy with the camera doesn't mean the camera is a magic wand. I'll say this, though: if you pull over a car for filming and need nine, ten, "about a hundred more" squad cars to do it, the guy in the back seat has a decent point about resource management. Public filming is legal, and the tone of the stop is its own commentary. Whether it was a lawful stop is something the video doesn't resolve, and neither can I.

## Tradecraft, Self-Defense, and a Pitch

Jason Hanson, a former CIA officer, ran "Can You LEGALLY Detain a Burglar Until Police Arrive?" The transcript I got is only the tail end, the part where he cleans up a Q&A, so I have no idea what he says about the actual legal question in the title, and I'm not going to pretend. Here's what is in the excerpt: he mentions that some people he trains use a tomahawk or a big machete for home defense because they're uncomfortable with a gun and worry about overpenetration, and says "you do not want to be on the wrong end of that." He also recommends a phone setup for privacy, naming the carriers Cape and Freely along with an "unplug phone," while admitting he still uses a flip phone, and he closes by noting he owns "more AR-15s than you can shake a stick at." So the CIA's privacy strategy is a flip phone, a niche carrier, and an arsenal. It's the most American tradecraft imaginable. For the actual law on detaining a burglar, which varies a great deal by state, ask a local attorney, not a podcast, not a machete.

Finally, LegalEagle's "Lawyer & Doctor React to 'The Good Doctor' Malpractice (ft. Doctor Mike)" arrived with a transcript that is entirely a promo for his new personal injury firm, The Eagle Team. He says viewers write him hundreds of emails a week asking for a lawyer, and that there's a "massive gap in our justice system," which is true, and also a very convenient thing to discover right before you open a law firm. No crime, no malpractice, just a pitch. I'm told this is what the kids call "monetizing," and what I call "a very polished sleemo."

Seven stories, one lesson, and it's that every system in crime runs on a witness, a record, or a lie: Carneglia on witnesses, Morgan on a wig, Peterson on a feather, Givens on a database, the scam center on whoever picks up the phone. Everything else is an elevator that says five when you're not on five.
