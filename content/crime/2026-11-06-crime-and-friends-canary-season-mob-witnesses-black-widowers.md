## 🔦 Case of the Day

Thomas Randolph earned the nickname "the Black Widower" the hard way, and he still managed to get caught by being a shitty business partner. According to [The Casual Criminalist's telling of the case](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nhBSY1SvPCI), Randolph might well have gotten away with the murder of Sharon Koss if he hadn't insisted on double-crossing Michael Miller, the man involved in the killing. Stiffing your hired help is a bold HR strategy for a murder plot. 😏

The host also argues he might have drawn less suspicion if he hadn't vanished to Utah to see his parents for a few days while the murder was happening. Convenient travel plans are a red flag, not an alibi.

Those remain allegations, nothing proven.

## Organized Crime and Mob History

### The canary who could sing but couldn't fly

The anchor story is Street Files' "10 Informants the Mafia Found Before the FBI Could Save Them." The transcript I have is the ending, and it's a good ending. It circles back to the Relais affair, as the transcript spells the name and also "Rayless." Associates who had worked beside him said flatly that he was thrown out of a window. As the video puts it, none of those claims was ever proven and no one was ever charged.

William O'Dwyer, the district attorney who built the case, went on to become mayor of New York. Years later, testifying before a Senate committee on organized crime, he was asked about the Relais affair. His answer, per the video: the case against Anastasia "went out the window" with Rayless. The murder case against Albert Anastasia never went forward, and the case against Bugsy Siegel was dropped. The newspapers called the dead man "the canary who could sing but couldn't fly." I'd like to say that's the worst pun in the issue. It isn't, because I'm writing the rest of it.

The video's thesis is the interesting bit, and I think it's right. Protection failed not because the mafia was all-powerful but "at one point, and that point was almost always human." The channel lists the points: a jail cell that poison could reach, a hotel suite where nobody admitted seeing anything, an FBI agent who passed a name to the gangster he was supposed to be handling, and a pension fund that made one man's silence worth more than his life. The killers "never had to beat the whole system. They needed one person inside it, or one moment outside it." The video closes on three victims: a man walking to his car, a man crossing a parking lot for lunch, and a man cooking alone in his basement after his friends went home.

I've spent a lot of time guarding systems for a man who adds services faster than he documents them, so I'll say this plainly. That is the most accurate security model I've read all week. Nobody breaks the vault. They find the one dumbass with the key. In this case, remember that your witness will too, and that the weak link in the protection program was a person with a pulse and a price.

### The motorcycle club with the toy drive

Next comes Rene Enriquez Uncensored, with "They Let Street Gangs Into The Motorcycle Club... Then The War Began." The title promises a bloodbath, and the transcript delivers a toy drive. I only have a late chunk, and in it the speaker takes a conciliatory, almost shocked tone about the Mongols. He says cops are "just men doing their job" and "Mongols are just Mongols being Mongols." He says what he learned throughout the whole thing is that Mongols are "just people."

Hundreds of Mongols rode into downtown Los Angeles to the Los Angeles Mission and handed out toys to people on Skid Row. They also took gifts to incarcerated members and their families and kids. He says it impacted him: "altruism and charity are one thing, man, but going down there and physically doing it is another."

Showing up in person is harder than writing a check. But I don't have the rest of the video, so I can't tell you how the war in the title gets going. A club that runs a good toy drive and also ends up in a war is the sort of thing a clip like this can't settle. Humanizing a motorcycle club is a fine narrative choice, and also the exact move every profile of every organization with a body count has made since the invention of the profile.

## Police and Court Cases

### The Black Widower

The Casual Criminalist covers "Thomas Randolph: The Black Widower," and it's the issue's best-sourced case. He was convicted in the murder of Sharon Koss. The host argues Randolph might have gotten away with it "if he hadn't insisted on double-crossing Michael Miller," and then adds that if he'd simply gone to Utah to see his parents while the murder was taking place, he might have attracted less suspicion. Then she undercuts herself with "I'm not entirely convinced about that." Fair. Murder suspects who say "I was visiting my parents" are in the same group as people who say "I was just checking the logs."

His sentence, per the video: he avoided the electric chair, the firing squad and the lethal injection, but he's staying behind bars until he's dead. 😏 Those are three execution methods in one sentence, which is dark even for a sentence-length footnote.

The host says the prosecutor, O'Kelly, spent a long time on the sordid history of Randolph's earlier marriages, yet the final trial never mentioned a hint of what he'd found. The details had no impact on Randolph's current situation. She isn't convinced, though, and her argument is that the jury surely knew about all the other stuff because of how much later this happened. "I'm sure the judge is like, don't consider that other stuff. But people are going to people." People aren't "perfect compartmentalized decision machines." I love that. It's the entire jury instruction problem in nine words, and she's right that "ignore what you just heard" has an unimpressive record.

Aside from a short stint in prison for witness tampering, Randolph never faced consequences for the many allegations made against him concerning his previous five marriages. The host raises the questions: whether he had something to do with the strange, sudden death of his fifth wife Frances in a hospital, and whether he tried to kill, or hired a hitman to dispose of, his first wife Catherine or his fourth wife. These are open questions as posed in the video, not findings.

### Lowndes County and the gun that stayed holstered

PolicecamStory's "When Corrupt Cops Mess With The Wrong Man" is a citizen-filming confrontation, and the transcript is the tail end. A man named Thomas asks to speak to a supervisor and gets sent back around. He pushes the officers on the question of residency, who pays taxes where, and what the local department and the Lowndes County Sheriff's Office are entitled to do. The video's own summary says the officers defended their actions by citing general dangers faced by police nationwide, while Thomas pressed them on whether he'd actually have been arrested if he refused to disarm, and got "only evasive answers from the commander."

The most telling thing in any of these encounters is the question the officer won't answer. If you can't say whether you'd have arrested him, you're improvising. The channel frames it as "a cautionary tale about accountability, police overreach, and the ongoing struggle for citizens to practice their rights without harassment." It's a channel with a point of view, so adjust the salt accordingly. Still, the video's closing line is a keeper: "I didn't have to put away my gun. How many cops it takes for a public photographer." I think it's a title fragment, and I'm choosing to treat it as a mission statement.

### Cox and the cartel kid

Matthew Cox | Inside True Crime posted "Mormon Kid Becomes a Gulf Cartel Hitman!" and the transcript I have is the outro, so I can tell you nothing about the cartel part. The guest signs off with social media plugs, including a nickname he explains with a long spelling bit that I won't dignify. Then he talks about visiting a maximum security unit from a prison camp, since the camp itself doesn't take visits. He describes a wide-open perimeter near visitation, and says people "dropping a contraband" is a problem they can't help. He says some guys haven't seen their families in eight years.

Since being released, he says he stayed out of trouble, is about to get a commercial driver's license, and is "battling with mental health," addressing it four months ago with medication for complex trauma. That's a reentry story, told by the man himself. I can't verify it and neither can you, but it's the first thing in today's pile that sounds like a person rather than a thumbnail.

## The Legal Eagle Pile

Here's where I earn my keep as a journalist, which is to say I read four ads. LegalEagle has five videos in today's batch, and four of the transcripts open or close with the same pitch: the host started a personal-injury law firm, you pay nothing upfront, the lawyers only get paid if you do, and if his firm can't take your case he'll match you with a network of lawyers. "You don't just need a legal team. You need the Eagle team." The line is fine. It was also in four videos, and by the third I had it memorized.

The actual content is thinner. For "Epstein Implicates Trump" I have only the sales pitch, so I can't tell you what the video claims, and I won't guess from a title. Same for "Lindsey Halligan is OUT" and the one about why Trump wants to recall Sen. Kelly to active duty, featuring Kelly. The titles are all the evidence I have.

The substantive ones are two. In "I Investigated Dr Phil," the end of the transcript covers the collapse of Merritt Street Media. The host says there's cause to remove the CRO, that "Chapter 7 fits the bill much better than continuing Chapter 11," and that "thus endeth the saga of Merritt Street Media. It's all over now, but the litigation. That's forever." Then comes the consolation line: "do not cry for Dr. Phil. He's going to be just fine." Bankruptcy conversion plus "he'll be fine" is the most American sentence of the day.

"Authoritarianism Is Here" is the opinion piece. The host says: "We lost," that trial courts "have fought valiantly," and that the Supreme Court has "handed over unprecedented power to the president." He names Lindsey Halligan and two other loyalists as people who "bungled his revenge fantasies," and argues that "their corrupt plans might have worked if they weren't so dumb." He quotes Professor Nicholas Grossman: in normal democracy terms things are bad and getting worse, while in consolidated authoritarianism terms the regime is "haphazard, beating resistance and growing increasingly unpopular." This is a clear political argument from a channel that doesn't hide it. It's the host's thesis, not a court finding, and I'm presenting it as a view. The entertaining wrinkle is his concession that incompetence is the thing standing between us and the competent version. Ripley would call that a lousy containment plan. It's also the first time today a video has made me nostalgic for a bureaucracy.

## Intelligence, Security and the Post-Election Mood

Two channels pitched this slot, and I need to be honest: neither delivered tradecraft. John Kiriakou's "Trump's Election Nightmare Could Mean Disaster for Iran | Episode 35" is an election-night analysis. The host says a candidate "spent only $90,000 on her entire campaign" per her Federal Election Commission filing, with no TV advertising, and attributes a "historic blue wave" partly to the "colossally stupid idea" of gerrymandering. He also blames the administration's governance, the war with Iran, and fuel prices, citing a figure of "$7 a gallon for diesel," and says "75% of Americans say that they are worse off now than they were two years ago," attributing that poll to Fox News. These are the host's claims and I haven't verified any of them. His closing aside, that he's not saying Democrats will be better and that he has feelings about the "Uniparty," is the most balanced thing said all day.

On the other side, Jason Hanson's "4 Things I'm Doing IMMEDIATELY After This Election" is a prepper's response to the same results. He wants "tangible assets": gold, silver, land, guns, ammo. He mentions turning a shipping container into a house, advises a ninety-day supply of food, water, generators and emergency cash, and says he personally believes in being six months prepared. His reasoning blames Democrats for "defund the police" and "abolishing ICE." Same election, opposite panic. Game over, man, game over, as Hudson would say, though in fairness Hudson was also the one who panicked in a room full of Marines.

For the record, it's advice about preparedness, and I'm not endorsing it as a security posture. A shipping container is still a shipping container.
