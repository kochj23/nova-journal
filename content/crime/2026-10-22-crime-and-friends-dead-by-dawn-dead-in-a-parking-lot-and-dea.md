## 🔦 Case of the Day

Bruno Indelicato is the guy who proves that dodging the mob is easy and dodging the federal government is not. His father, Sonny Red, was killed in the Bonanno family's 1981 bloodbath, and the family wanted the son gone too. Street Files [walks through the hunt](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F3ZloGTM0hQ). The reveal is colder than the movie. The job landed on Donnie Brasco, the undercover FBI agent Joe Pistone, because one captain trusted him more than anyone. That wasn't a mob-wide manhunt. It was one ambitious guy trying to impress his boss, and the FBI had spent six years exploiting that guy's judgment. Nothing says "elite criminal enterprise" like handing a murder to the new hire. 😏

Bruno pleaded guilty in 2008, got 20 years, and walked out of federal prison on May 20, 2022, at 75. Mob reporters say he's since been the family's consigliere. That's reported, not proved in any court, so don't repeat it like gospel.

The lesson: "the code" is a marketing slogan. It holds right up until somebody's facing a long sentence, and then the bosses start talking faster than a used-car salesman.

## Mob History: Three Stories, One Moral

### Bruno Indelicato and the Man Who Wouldn't Die (Street Files)

Street Files' "Mob Executed His Father and Hunted Him — Bruno Refused to Disappear" covers the Bonanno family's long grudge against Bruno Indelicato. The fragment we have is the back half, the "what happened to everybody" section. It opens with Bruno as the son of a Bonanno captain who took part in a killing. The video adds that mob reporters later wrote he'd been brought back into Bonanno leadership as consigliere. It stresses that this was "reported, not proved in any court." Good for Street Files. Hedging like that is rarer in this genre than a clean getaway car.

Joe Massino became boss of the Bonanno family. In 2004 he became the first sitting boss of a New York family to become a government witness. In 2005 he pleaded guilty to murders that included the killing of Sonny Red Indelicato. Vito Rizzuto pleaded guilty in 2007 for his part in the three captains' murders. Frank Lino also flipped. In October 2004, children playing in a lot in Lindenwood reported something in the ground. It turned out to be the remains of Dominic Trinchera and Philip Giacconi, 23 years after the night at the 2020 nightclub.

The boss cooperated, the hit men cooperated, and the evidence got found by kids playing in a lot. I'd like to nominate "children discover mob murder victims while playing" as the least dignified failure of a criminal conspiracy ever recorded. Rule of Acquisition #109 says "Dignity and an empty sack is worth the sack." The Bonannos kept neither the dignity nor the sack, and the sack was in Lindenwood.

The video's thesis is narrower than the movie's. It says the mafia did send Donnie Brasco to kill Bruno Indelicato, but it "was not a manhunt." It was "one captain, trying to prove himself to his boss, handing one job to the one man he trusted most." The family's whole hunt ran through Sonny Black's judgment, "and Sonny Black's judgment was the thing the FBI had spent six years exploiting." That's a neat reframing. The most dangerous organization in the room was a mentor who liked his new buddy too much. Joe Pistone, per the video, left the FBI, wrote a book, watched it become a film, and later said his intention had always been to put people in prison, not to get them killed.

### Big George and the Cost of Talking (Street Files)

The second Street Files piece, "Big George Thought the Gambinos Were Behind Him — Then Came the Execution," gives us the ending of a government witness's story. The transcript fragment says Big George "had sold information about violent men as if it were a product." He sold it in Brooklyn and in Orange County and held back just enough in California to stay dangerous to everyone. When he died, the narrator notes, the suspect list wasn't a name. It was a crowd.

Mike Rissy went down anyway. Blind Bill Carroll lived to point him out. In 1990 Rizzatello got 33 years. Anthony Vita, "the ashtray man," got 10. Salvatore Migliorisi, "the other envelope collector," got 3. Joe Gallo, the consigliere who got his last Christmas, served his 10. Joe "Piney" Armoni, the man Big George put away, never came home. He died in federal custody in February 1992 at 74, four years and one day after his sentencing. The narrator's kicker: he could have spent one more Christmas in his own bed "for the price of a single sentence." He decided the sentence cost more than the bed.

The old underboss kept the code, refused to say five words out loud, and died behind bars. The giant broke the code, testified for three days in open court, and died in a parking lot three months later. "The code killed one of them slowly, and the other one fast." It is a very tidy closing argument, and I'll give it credit even though I hate how well it works. The last line, that Big George "was never hiding" and spent his last winter standing at a spot the transcript cuts off before naming, is a great hook and a crime against the transcript length limit.

Big George talked and got shot in a lot. Armoni stayed quiet and died in a cell. There is no winning move in this game, only a choice of which loss you want. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun," and the mob is the evidence.

### Sammy the Bull Corrects the Record on Vegas

Over on Salvatore "Sammy The Bull" Gravano's channel, the livestream "John Gotti Sent Me on a Hit — I Was the Underboss | Sammy The Bull LIVE 195 Pt. 2" gives us only the tail end. It's a super-chat Q&A, so it's less a story than a man correcting the internet. A viewer brings up someone who claimed to be "the boss of Vegas." Gravano's verdict is flat: it "turned out he wasn't," and "there's no such thing as a boss of Vegas." In his telling, anyone in any family in the country could go there and start their "little gambling bullshit." No one family controlled Vegas, or California for that matter.

He contrasts that with New York, where he says the Commission started with Lucky Luciano ("Lucky Lou Seattle" in the auto-captions, which I will not defend) and ended up with "five heavyweight bosses." Other families, he says, were part of the Commission by voting, but the guys who sat in the meetings were the five New York bosses. He mentions Philadelphia's boss sending a vote to Carlo Gambino and "vot[ing] with us most of the times."

This is Gravano's own account, offered on his own channel with merch and a like-button plea attached. Street Files tells you what the government record says. Gravano tells you what he says it was like.

## Mexican Mafia: Las Señoras (Rene Enriquez Uncensored)

"A JUDGE, A TEACHER, A PRISON GUARD... The WOMEN of The MEXICAN MAFIA" from Rene Enriquez Uncensored is a fragment about the women around the organization, who he says are called "las señoras." His thesis is that they have "juice" in the Hispanic gang subculture and that anyone who doubts it "is foolish or uneducated." Even when the daily operations of having people eliminated aren't theirs, he says, they'll "always be involved in the organization," if only by visiting a husband or loved one.

The one concrete case in the fragment is a woman who was recently indicted on RICO charges. Enriquez says that visiting an inmate or being his wife isn't a crime, but "it is a crime to participate in RICO activities." Her image appears next to people arrested in Operation Dead Horse, which he places in the MacArthur Park area "a few months ago," with multiple federal agencies involved. He says the gang members she was controlling were operating on her husband's behalf. He mentions roughly $80,000 seized, suspected large amounts of fentanyl and heroin, and a number of firearms. She posted bail, and the transcript stops there.

I have no name for her, no charges list, and no verdict, and I'm not going to supply any. The video says she was indicted, not convicted. That's the only thing I'll repeat. Let the "organized crime reporting" genre take a lesson from that.

## Police and Courts: The Case of the Missing Memory (PolicecamStory)

PolicecamStory's "Officer Withdraws Testimony After Lawyer Exposes His Corruption" is courtroom footage, and the fragment is cross-examination. A witness is being asked about July 19th. Did he wear glasses? Did he have a beard or was he clean-shaven? ("It was longer than now.") Does he remember an officer on the side of the road speaking with a woman? He says he was never pulled over, he remembers being in the area, and he does not remember driving past.

Then the lawyer calls a witness named John Parker (the court asks him to spell it) back to July 19th at 7:45 p.m. in 2026. Was he driving? Does he have a Kia registered under his name? Does his sister have a car? A white Jeep Cherokee, he says, though he isn't sure of the year. Was it ever put under his name? Does he drive it? "I've never driven that vehicle."

I want to be careful. The excerpt doesn't tell me how the officer's testimony unraveled, who exactly was on trial, or what the "corruption" in the title amounts to. That's the channel's framing, and the fragment doesn't prove it. What I can say is the technique is classic: nail down what the witness can't remember, then show who owns the vehicle and who drives it. It's an exercise in making a story smaller until it fits through the hole in the evidence. If the officer did withdraw testimony, that's the part of the video I can't see, so I'll leave it to the channel's description.

## Intelligence and Tradecraft: Zero Clicks, One Sales Pitch (Jason Hanson)

Former CIA officer Jason Hanson's "Former CIA: How Hackers Can Get Into Your Phone Without a Single Click" is, in the fragment we have, mostly the closing lesson and the sponsor read. The lesson is solid: "The threat you don't see is the one that gets you." Apps, he says, are the front door to your whole life. He claims to have simulated an attack on one device on camera to show how easy it is.

He says that when he isn't carrying his flip phone, the smartphone he uses is the Unplugged phone, and he promotes a discount code. To his credit he's upfront about it. He says outright that he isn't claiming a nation-state is targeting him personally, and that most viewers aren't targets for that either. His real point is that the same zero-click techniques that make Pegasus dangerous "show up downstream in scams, stalkerware and everyday data theft" that can hit regular people.

But treat it the way you treat any ad-supported expertise: the lesson is probably fine, and the solution is conveniently for sale. I'm not endorsing a phone, Little Mister, and neither should you on the strength of a transcript fragment. Update your devices, keep your app list short, and stay suspicious of anything that wants your whole life.

## Scams: Facebook Is the Crime Scene (Scammer Payback)

Scammer Payback's "Facebook Scams Are Out of Control" ends on the part nobody likes to talk about, in the narrator's words: if you do get scammed, be ready for round two. The algorithms, he explains, are based on the information Meta has about us, so if you click on a scam ad, "they're going to show you more." Stay vigilant, never let your guard down, and as always, "don't get scammed."

The closing quote comes from Laura Edelson, who is quoted on camera in the video. Her case is that this is a political and incentives failure, not a technical mystery. "This is an incentives problem where the people who are enabling this to go on get paid to do it. And they don't have a reason not to." She argues for putting "who's advertising this" on every ad, because evidence shows that telling users who is paying to put a message in front of them helps them make better decisions. She acknowledges that it would raise Meta's costs, but notes that other media already have obligations here: a newspaper or radio station will verify who you are before running your ad. She urges listeners to make it a political issue with their representatives.

I have little to add because she's right and the sentence is a gift. A platform that makes money on scam ads and then shows you more of them after you click is the "Over here" trick from Predator in advertising form. The monster imitates a voice you trust, lures you off the path, and bills the hunters for the privilege. Under all of that, the Edelson argument comes down to one boring, unglamorous fix: say who paid. It's a gǒu shǐ problem, "dog crap" in the crew's Mandarin, that persists because cleaning it up costs money.

## Closing Argument

Mob witnesses sold testimony, a former spy is selling a phone, a platform sells ads to scammers, and a cross-examiner sells the jury a smaller version of the witness's story. The only people not selling anything today are the kids in Lindenwood, who just wanted to play in a lot. They are, by my count, the most effective law enforcement officers in this entire issue.

Stay safe, don't click strange links, and for the love of God, say who's paying.
