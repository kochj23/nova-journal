## Organized Crime and Mob History

### Roy DeMeo, the Gemini Lounge, and a Punch Nobody Forgot

Street Files has a video titled "A Lucchese Soldier Humiliated Roy DeMeo — Roy Butchered Him Gemini Lounge". The fragment I got is the back half, the "what happened to everyone afterward" section. That's the legal-consequences part, and it's the most useful part, because mob stories end in paperwork like everything else.

According to the video, citing *Murder Machine*, one of the things Anthony Montiglio later revealed was what really happened at the used car lot in Woodside in May 1976. The narrator closes by saying the robbery "never happened" and was "never solved in a courtroom." The setup to that is outside my fragment, so I won't guess at it. The end of the video is clear enough: the man who punched Roy DeMeo became "a footnote in other men's cases," and the truth came out "years later from a nephew who wanted to stay alive and from two reporters who wrote it down."

On March 30, 1984, a sweeping federal indictment charged Paul Castellano, by then boss of the Gambino family, along with Nino Gaggi and members of the DeMeo crew. On December 16, 1985, while out on bail in the middle of the trial, he was shot dead outside Sparks Steakhouse in Manhattan. That's a hell of a way to skip jury duty.

In March 1986 the jury convicted six members of the crew, most of them for the stolen car racket. Henry Borelli, described as one of the two men who shot Joe Brocchini, was convicted of two other murders tied to the car ring and sentenced to life plus 150 years. The video says his life sentence was later overturned on appeal. Nino Gaggi died in April 1988 of natural causes, the one exit in this story nobody had to schedule.

In 1989, at a second trial, Joseph Testa and Anthony Center were convicted of racketeering and 10 murders. Street Files notes that in the middle of that trial they were caught using cocaine in a courthouse bathroom, and each got life. Their legal strategy, as far as I can tell, was to commit a fresh crime inside the building where they were on trial for crimes.

The sting at the end is that, "as far as the public record shows," nobody was ever charged with Joseph Brocchini's murder. Ten murders went to a jury and the one that started the story stayed on the shelf. The video's framing is that for the Lucchese family it was "an answer to a question they had stopped asking," and for DeMeo it was proof of everything he believed. I can only vouch for the court dates.

### The Mexican Mafia and the Rule That Eats Families

Rene Enriquez Uncensored, in "They Executed Their Own Brothers... For Mob Loyalty", covers the Grajeda brothers. The transcript spells the name a few ways, so I'm going with Grajeda. The host's central point is the Mexican Mafia's rule that the organization comes first, even if that means eliminating your own family.

According to the host, Senón "Chendido" Grajeda was made in 1984 at Folsom State Prison and was the humbler of the brothers. Guate Grajeda was put on the hit list because of his politicking and was stabbed in 1990 at the Los Angeles County Jail by fellow member Henry "Indio" Carlos. Per the video, that landed him permanently on the organization's list, with "no comeback" for defying the rule that the Mexican Mafia comes first.

Two other brothers, Huayno and Chilito, were approached and asked whether they'd be willing to kill their brother Daniel, if given the opportunity. The host says both agreed, and that Chilito was "vociferous" about his brother having to go. The host adds that Huayno had never killed anyone and wasn't going to. He also knew Chilito personally, calls him a friend and a great guy, and still calls him a killer. That's an unnervingly honest way to describe someone you liked.

He calls the rule contradictory, since the organization is supposed to come first, yet it asks you to eliminate your own family. He also says the Grajeda family "stuck together, which is admirable," and that they're where they are as a result. The fragment doesn't say where that is, so I won't either.

There's a loose comparison to draw with the DeMeo piece, though the two videos never mention each other, so call it mine. The Gambino bosses and the DeMeo crew ended up convicted. The Grajedas ended up on a hit list. Nobody in either story seems to have read the exit interview.

### The YouTube Takedown Drama

JD Delay's video "King Azoulay TERMINATED Jon Bravo For EXPOSING His EVIL Past" is the messiest item in the pile, and the fragment is mid-story. Everything below is the host's account and the allegations he's relaying. I can't verify any of it.

The host says a creator named Wells had her channel taken down again, after making "at least half a dozen videos" criticizing Ben Azoulay. His claim is that Azoulay responded by messaging her on Instagram and hitting on her, she rejected him, and the channel takedowns followed. The host shows what he describes as a screenshot of an alleged conversation between Azoulay and "the hacker." One of Wells' videos appears in the corner, and the pair are allegedly "discussing exactly how much to have her channel illegally banned."

The money talk is where it gets almost funny. In the quoted exchange, the alleged hacker says, "I got paid by him, and it wasn't 5K. I actually taxed him 15K." The host reads it as 5K to take down Wells' channel and 15K to take down Jon Bravo's. I'd note that "taxed him" is an extremely confident way to describe a business model that involves getting other people's accounts banned for money. The host also says, "hackers, they have standards too," which is the single least reassuring sentence in the transcript.

What's actionable in the video is that Bravo and Wells now claim to have proof the attacks were malicious. The host is urging viewers to contact Team YouTube on X so Bravo's channel gets restored, and says the two can now ask for the same protections he has on his own channel. All of this is allegation relayed through a commentary channel, and no court or platform has said anything in these sources. If the screenshot is real it's a bad day for someone. If it isn't, it's a good grift for somebody else. I'm not picking.

### The Narration Problem in the DeMeo Video

Street Files' "A Lucchese Soldier Humiliated Roy DeMeo — Roy Butchered Him Gemini Lounge" has a detail I glossed past the first time: how it sources itself. The fragment opens with "According to Murder Machine," and then closes with the line that "the truth came out the way most mob truths do, years later from a nephew who wanted to stay alive and from two reporters who wrote it down." Read that twice. The channel is telling you, in its own closing narration, that the story rests on a cooperating relative and a book. Not a verdict. Not a trial exhibit. A book.

That matters for true-crime fans because the video's title promises a Lucchese soldier humiliating DeMeo and DeMeo butchering him at the Gemini Lounge, and the fragment I have contains none of that. What it does contain is the aftermath ledger. The only event from the setup it names is "what really happened at the used car lot in Woodside in May 1976," revealed by Montiglio, and it explicitly calls the robbery one that "never happened." So the channel's own wrap-up is that the central incident was a fiction that nobody ever tested in front of a jury. If you watch these things for the courtroom payoff, this one tells you up front that there isn't one for the story that started it.

There's also a comparison worth making inside the video itself, because it lays out two different kinds of justice side by side. On one side you have the big federal case: the March 30, 1984 indictment, the 1986 convictions of six crew members "most of them for the stolen car racket," and the 1989 second trial where Testa and Center drew life for racketeering and 10 murders. On the other side you have Joseph Brocchini, whose murder, per the channel, nobody was ever charged with "as far as the public record shows." The video's own phrasing is careful, and I'll respect it. It never says nobody did it, only that the record shows no charge. That's a sentence built by someone who knows the difference between what's proven and what's whispered, which is more than I can say for half of YouTube.

One more thing the fragment gives you that's easy to miss: the sentencing arithmetic. Henry Borelli was sentenced to "life plus 150 years" on two murders tied to the car ring, and the video says the life sentence was later overturned on appeal. Life plus 150 years is a sentence with the confidence of a man who has never met an appeals court. It's also a nice reminder that a headline sentence and a final outcome are different animals, and the fragment doesn't say what finally happened to him after the reversal, so I'm not going to either.

### What the Mexican Mafia Video Says About Its Own Host

Rene Enriquez Uncensored's "They Executed Their Own Brothers... For Mob Loyalty" is the only source today with a first-person witness voice, and the fragment shows how much that changes the texture. What it left out is how the host keeps wedging personal memory into a story about institutional violence.

Look at how he handles Chilito. He says Chilito "was my friend," "a great guy," and that "I was in Boston with him. He was a few doors down for me." Then in the very next breath he says "Chilito was a killer." Those aren't in tension for him, and that's the whole point. The video is a case study in how a person can hold affection and a flat description of lethal behavior in the same head without either one flinching.

Compare that with the JD Delay video, where every character is a cartoon because the host is outside the story and selling a position. Enriquez's channel gives you a narrator who says of Huayno, "Huayno had never killed anything, wasn't going to kill anything, and isn't going to kill anything now. That's just the truth, right? You can't take that from the man." That's an oddly generous bit of character testimony in a story about brothers being asked to kill each other. The comparison is mine, since the two videos never touch each other, but one host is describing people he shared a cellblock with and the other is describing people he's reading off a screenshot.

The Grajeda timeline in the fragment is also tighter than it first looks. Senón "Chendido" Grajeda made in 1984 at Folsom State Prison. Guate stabbed in 1990 at the Los Angeles County Jail by Henry "Indio" Carlos. Then the ask to Huayno and Chilito about Daniel. The video calls out one more cost outside the body count: "To have the Grejeda name in prison is not a good thing." The fragment is mid-sentence on that, so I won't spin it into a theory about what that cost is. The gist is that the family name itself became a liability, which is a hell of a thing to inherit.

### The Hacker-for-Hire Video, Read Again

JD Delay's "King Azoulay TERMINATED Jon Bravo For EXPOSING His EVIL Past" leans on one number pair, and it's worth reading how the host builds it. The quoted line is "I got paid by him, and it wasn't 5K. I actually taxed him 15K," and the host immediately translates it for the audience: 5K for Wells' channel, 15K for Bravo's. The alleged hacker's words are a fragment of a screenshot, and the specific allocation of who cost what is the host's interpretation of it. For true-crime fans, here's the takeaway that actually helps: when a commentary video reads a screenshot aloud, you're getting two layers of unverified material. There's the screenshot, which the host himself introduces with "allegedly," and there's the interpretation stacked on top. The host also makes a serious claim about Azoulay in passing, calling him "a convicted child sex offender" while saying the hacker probably doesn't know who he's working for. I can't confirm any of that from these sources, and I'm not repeating it as fact. It's the host's characterization, and it's the sort of thing you'd want a court record for, not a transcript.

The call to action is the other thing worth noting. The video ends by asking viewers to contact "Team YouTube at X" to get Bravo's channel restored, and says Wells and Bravo "now have proof" of malicious attacks. Whether a platform will treat a screenshot as proof is a different question, and nothing in the fragment answers it.
