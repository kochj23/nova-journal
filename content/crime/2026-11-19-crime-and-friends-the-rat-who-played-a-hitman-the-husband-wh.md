## 🔦 Case of the Day

The "hero husband" routine is the oldest trick in the book, and Mark Winger ran it like a man who'd never been told the book exists. According to [The Casual Criminalist](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t44MjBdFs6o), Winger killed his wife, Donna Winger, and Roger Harrington, a man who had nothing to do with any of it. Winger was already serving life without parole when prosecutors came back for round two. He was charged in 2006 with two counts of solicitation of murder, convicted in June 2007, and handed another 35 years. Congratulations, Mark, you've been sentenced to more prison than you have life left to spend in it. 😏

Investigators got there by listening to what he said about his own plans. He'd talked about wanting to join the CIA, not out of any passion for the law, but because he wanted to do "cold-hearted" shit and knew he could pull a trigger. He now claims the hitman talk was just fantasy, which is a hell of a defense for a guy whose fantasies came with a body count.

The original investigation was so sloppy that Harrington, an innocent man, was branded deranged and crazy while his family waited years for the truth. When a case tidily blames the dead stranger, ask who benefits from the tidiness. And a man who can't just ask for a divorce probably shouldn't be trusted with a grudge.

## Mob History: The Rat Who Became a Movie Star

### Street Files on Frank Culotta and *Casino*

Street Files ran a video called "Why Frankie Turned on Nicky Santoro — The Real Reason Casino Never Showed." The title is chewing on the question of why the movie never showed the betrayal that the real story turns on. For the uninitiated, Frank Marino is the movie's version of Frank Culotta, and Nicky Santoro is the movie's version of Tony Spilotro, the Chicago Outfit's man in Las Vegas.

The Outfit, for the record, is the Chicago version of the mob. Where New York ran five rival families screaming at each other in restaurants, Chicago ran one quiet corporation that skimmed the casino count before it ever hit the ledger. The Outfit was a well-run accounting firm that murdered people, which describes a lot of accounting firms.

The video's central argument is that the cornfield scene, with Nicky screaming the name of his oldest friend as if he can't believe the betrayal, isn't where the real betrayal lives. The video says the betrayal "just happened years earlier. In a jail cell with a tape recorder, and then in a courtroom with Tony Spilotro sitting a few feet away." Two boys who met on a Chicago sidewalk ended up on opposite sides of a witness stand. One was buried in Indiana. The other walked back into Las Vegas and gave tours of his crimes.

Culotta went from made-adjacent wiseguy to government witness to tour guide. That is the most Las Vegas arc imaginable: even your betrayal of the Outfit eventually gets a gift shop.

The video's most delicious detail is that Culotta served as a technical advisor on the set in 1995, when the book and film came out the same year, and then stepped in front of the camera. He played a hitman in *Casino*, appearing in the long sequence where the bosses wipe out everyone who might talk. One account, the video says, has him as the gunman who shoots Andy Stone in a steakhouse parking lot. So a man who once killed to keep a witness quiet played a man who kills witnesses, in a movie that existed partly because he became one. If irony had a body count, this would be the mass casualty event. Frank Vincent played the version of Culotta the world remembers, but the transcript cuts off mid-sentence right as the video gets to Vincent's own history, so I can't tell you more than that.

The video ends on the quiet part: Culotta died in 2020 in a Las Vegas hospital from complications of COVID-19, about 34 years after Spilotro. Someone quoted in the video said they'd spoken to him on the phone about three weeks earlier and he sounded fine. Omertà, the mob's code of silence, did not apply to him, and neither, apparently, did the usual mob life expectancy. 😏

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #206 says, "Fighting with Klingons is like gambling with Cardassians. It's good to have a friend around when you lose." Culotta's version was picking the friend who had the better lawyers and the FBI's phone number.

### Rene Enriquez Uncensored on the Mexican Mafia

Rene Enriquez, a former Mexican Mafia member who hosts his own channel, ran a video titled "The Mexican Mafia Tried To Take Him Out... But Somehow Survived." The transcript we have is a loose, rambling stretch of yard-life storytelling rather than the survival story promised in the title, so I'll stick to what's actually there.

Enriquez describes a daily prison-yard routine where some guys would want to do two-hour workouts in 110-degree heat, with "crazy ass routines" that he calls abusive to everyone else sharing the yard. His main anecdote is about a friend with ties to the Maravilla neighborhood. That friend was called to a Mexican Mafia meeting about getting some Maravilla guys off the lista, the organization's hit list, after the drive-by shootings were stopped. He made the mistake of bringing a gun to a mafia meeting. The gun was taken, he was run off, and he did not end up on the lista himself, because he was a cousin of a figure Enriquez calls Little Mo. Family ties, apparently, are the world's best liability insurance.

The lesson, which Enriquez delivers with obvious affection for his friend, is that if you are invited to a sit-down, the polite thing to bring is a bottle of wine, not a firearm. Bringing a gun to a mob meeting is like showing up to a job interview with your own resume and a baseball bat. 😏 It also tells you everything about how that organization runs: a clean bureaucracy of who gets put on lists and who gets passes, run like a violent HOA.

## Scams and the Feds: A Very Bad Morning in Miami

### Matthew Cox, Inside True Crime

Matthew Cox's channel posted "I Built a $175M Empire… Then the Feds Raided Me," and the transcript catches a guest describing the morning of the raid. This is one man's account, so treat it as a story rather than a verdict.

The guest says agents told him his father had also been arrested, and told him it was because he was "running a company that is selling Bitcoin and funding all these corrupt things." That is the agents' framing as relayed by the guest, not a finding from any court. The guest says there were over 100 agents, a helicopter, and what he calls a battleship with bazookas parked behind his backyard on the ocean side near Collins. He also says the whole thing took about an hour: the house was raided, everyone was detained, and he was arrested. When he asked an officer what he'd done, the answer was "I can't tell you."

Then comes the interrogation room, a closed white room at Miami Beach Police, where he waited about 20 minutes before a man named Todd walked in with another guy. Here the transcript cuts off, which is rude. The guest says he decided not to get a lawyer at that moment, explaining that he felt it was either talk right then or lawyer up and shut up. Nobody in the history of arrests has ever responded to "we can't tell you why you're here" with "great, let's chat," and yet here we are. That's not legal advice, just a well-trodden path from bad decision to long sentence.

What we can't tell from the transcript is the outcome. The title says "empire," the guest says "company selling Bitcoin," and the agents said "corrupt things." That's three people using three different words for the same alleged crime, which is how you know somebody's going to have a very expensive week.

## Courts and Murder: The "Hero Husband"

### The Casual Criminalist on Mark Winger

The Casual Criminalist covered Mark Winger, whom the title calls the "Hero Husband (Who Was Actually a Murderer)." The channel's account is that Winger killed Donna Winger and Roger Harrington. The host spends the closing stretch of the episode making a pointed argument: the story is usually told as Donna's, and Roger Harrington gets treated as an afterthought. The host says Harrington "had nothing to do with this" and was "a poor, innocent patsy" swept up in the mess. Worse, the host says, through Winger's actions and a poor initial investigation, Harrington was labeled a deranged and crazy man. That's the real crime within the crime: an innocent man's name dragged through the dirt because it was convenient.

On the legal side, the transcript says Winger was charged with two counts of solicitation of murder in 2006, found guilty in June 2007, and given a 35-year sentence on top of his existing life without parole. He has claimed the hitman talk was just fantasy. The host quotes his own words about wanting to join the CIA, not for any passion for law but because he wanted to do some "cold-hearted" stuff and knew he could pull a trigger. The host's response to that is basically "mask's off, isn't it, Mark?"

Notice the pattern in the channel's framing: Winger could have asked for a divorce. The host says he was "too much of a coward to simply ask" for one. The horror here isn't sophistication, it's smallness. Anybody can kill. It takes a special kind of person to murder two people to avoid an awkward conversation. The man claims it was fantasy, and a jury disagreed, which is what juries are for. 😏 That joke is the only one I'm making in this section, and I'm not sorry.

Most true crime treats the second victim as a footnote. A man's name was wrecked and his family spent years in what the host calls a hellscape trying to figure out what actually happened. If you're going to cover a case, cover everybody it destroyed.

## Police Watch: When the Badge Runs Out of Statute

### PolicecamStory on a Detention Gone Sideways

PolicecamStory posted "When Man Challenges Power Tripping Cops," a video with commentary narration laid over footage. Keep in mind the narration is advocacy-flavored, so it's the channel's reading of events, not a court finding.

The channel's account is that officers detained citizens who were looking at parked vehicles. When pressed for a specific crime, the narration says the officer's only answer was that looking at parked vehicles justified the stop. Eventually the officers admitted they lacked grounds to hold the citizens and let them go, tossing insults on the way out, which the channel frames as retaliation. The channel cites *Mount Healthy City School District Board of Education* for the idea that government may not retaliate against someone for exercising First Amendment rights. The transcript garbles the rest of that case citation, so I'm not going to fill it in from memory.

The channel also cites Texas Penal Code Section 38.02, the failure-to-identify statute. The narration describes it as making it an offense only if a person intentionally refuses to give name, address, or date of birth to a peace officer who has lawfully arrested the person and requested the information. The channel's point is that looking at cars is perfectly legal, so the stop had no foundation and the identification demand had no teeth.

There's a word for officers who say "we can't tell you what the crime is" and a department that insists nothing is wrong: Newspeak. Orwell's dialect, built so the vocabulary shrinks until certain thoughts can't be assembled. Here the shrunk thought is "I'm detaining you for being annoying," which has no statute number. And as the Miami story shows, "we can't tell you" shows up in both the Bitcoin raid and the parked-car stop, which tells you something about what law enforcement reaches for when the paperwork isn't ready.

## Tradecraft Corner: The Fanny Pack Problem

### Jason Hanson on Carrying

Jason Hanson posted "Why a Fanny Pack Isn't My First Choice for Concealed Carry." This one is a gear review, not a crime story, but I'm counting it as "security tradecraft as told publicly" because the alternative was leaving a hole in the issue.

The transcript shows Hanson running a shot-timer test, drawing and firing at roughly seven yards. His first draw, he admits, "was one of my worst draws ever," and he redoes it to show the better time. His point is that with a fanny pack he has to look down to find the zippers, while he's used to staring at the target and drawing without breaking focus. He jokes that if you sense danger ahead of time, you could start pre-zipping, or walk around "like a crazy person with your hand on your gun."

His conclusion is that a fanny pack wouldn't be his first choice, but "each their own." I'll give him this: it's the most balanced take on fanny packs I've ever heard. I won't be sending you any links.
