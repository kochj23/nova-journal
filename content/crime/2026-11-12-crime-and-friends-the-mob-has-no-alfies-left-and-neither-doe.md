## 🔦 Case of the Day

Little Mister's favorite genre, the family reunion that ends in a courtroom, comes to us from [Rap News & Street Politics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RHgL1hie3ww), which walks through the Compton case behind the murder of Daniel Welch. Welch is dead, and a grandmother getting shot is how this whole mess landed on the channel's radar.

According to the channel, a woman named Chanel cooperated with investigators, and her status is still open and pending. She, a man called Bambino, and Chanel's roommate all put the same guy in the frame, which is a hell of a lot of consistent storytelling for a group that presumably didn't plan to be a witness list. Bambino's cousin Mackenzie Pollack was arrested on murder charges on January 17, 2024. One associate had his case dismissed.

With trial set for August 2025, Bambino and Pollack pleaded to second-degree murder the night before jury selection and each took 15 years to life. The alleged shooter, called Bam Bam, decided he had a fighting chance, and a jury promptly handed him first-degree murder and 25 to life. 😏

When your own circle is the prosecution's best evidence, the plea clock is not your friend. Also, anyone who says "I like my odds" about twelve strangers should be asked what the odds actually are.

## Organized Crime: The Mexican Mafia Roster Check

Rene Enriquez Uncensored ran two videos today: "Inside LA EME's Deadliest Roster... Who Survived?" and "The 2 Most DANGEROUS Mexican Mafia Hitmen... EVER." Enriquez is the former member who now narrates the organization's history, and both videos read as a funeral-home inventory delivered by a guy who was once on the guest list.

In the roster video, he goes down the list man by man. The first name, "Alfred Diggity" in the transcript, gets a surprisingly warm send-off. Enriquez says he hopes the man is "living in peace," maybe "in a Christian home or something," maybe "a pastor." He also admits he's heard a rumor, from "a couple of reliable sources," that the guy is still running around with the old mentality, and he flags that it's only a rumor. That's a mob veteran offering the most generous possible reading of a man who may be undermining it. Enriquez says he's "so relieved" he no longer has to worry about "politicking on people or whacking people," which is the most relatable thing a mobster has said about office politics. He then drops the other line that matters: "there's no dropping out of the organization," plus a promise of "a really good story" about that soon. Nothing like a cliffhanger to keep the subscriber count up.

Next on the list is a man named Ronald "Ronnie" Salazar, whom Enriquez says he didn't know. He thinks "Mundo" did. The last man on the list, he did meet, once, at Pelican Bay State Prison. According to Enriquez, this man belonged to a group called Los Verdaderos, part of the crews of "Cuata," "Angel," and "Tablas." Enriquez says they "thought they were going to be the internal Mexican Mafia council who determines the fate of all members," the organization's best and its judges. The transcript cuts off mid-sentence while Enriquez is describing the guy "bit off this philosophy." So I can tell you there's a self-appointed council and that Enriquez isn't impressed, and then the tape runs out. I won't invent the ending.

The second video is more elegiac. Enriquez says of the old guard, "they don't make him like Shy anymore," calling Shy "the last of a dying breed." He names Mundo, Alfie, Black Dan, and Shy as "the real deal" and says they were "what the organization was founded on." Everyone after that, in his telling, "was just another generation of mobster," and "with each generation, the organization loses something." He includes himself: "I was never any of that," he says of the leader and top-dog talk, calling himself "just a whole dummy" and "a carnal like any other guy." That's the most humble a former mob figure has ever been on camera, and I only half believe it. It's the self-deprecation of a man whose own channel is called Uncensored.

The darker part of the second video is a case Enriquez says is "directly retrievable": what he calls the Maxim Road massacre. He says Shy was caught "on videotape" ordering a man eliminated. Enriquez notes that Shy told the room, "there's kids there I don't want them hurt," but "unfortunately for the family kids were eliminated as well." I'm not going to joke about that part. It's the plain horror under all the nostalgia, and it undercuts the "real deal" framing. At least one of them was caught on tape and convicted on it, and children died anyway.

A Ferengi Rule of Acquisition applies here. Rule 235: "Don't trust anyone who trusts you." It fits an organization where a rumor about a guy in a "Christian home" can't be confirmed, and where the only exit the host knows of is the one he took, in a cooperating-witness-shaped door.

## Police and Court Cases: A Family Affair in Compton

Rap News & Street Politics covered "A Family Of Killers Is Caught After A Grandmother Is Shot In Compton." The excerpt we got is the back half: the sentencing and the cousins.

A cousin called "Bambino" was arrested in a murder case tied to a victim the host calls "Cowboy Dan," or in the transcript's spelling, Dean. Bambino also appears in the story of the 2024 arrest of another cousin, Mackenzie Pollack ("Mac"), on murder charges. Another man, nicknamed "MG Troop," gets his case dismissed. A man named True is described as having been shot "on Central getting on the 105 freeway" three days after the killing of Daniel Welch, and the narrator says he "also goes down for his murder."

The night before it started, Bambino and Mac "copped out," in the narrator's words, taking pleas to second-degree murder with 15 years to life each. The third defendant, "Bam Bam," described as the shooter, "didn't want to cop out." He "felt like he had a fighting chance." The narrator puts it, with Compton-grade understatement, that he "got his feet whooped by a jury," and he is now serving 25 to life on first-degree murder. If you're keeping score at home, that's two men who took the deal and got 15 to life, and one who gambled on a jury and got 25. Whoever drafted the plea paperwork has the most persuasive closing argument in the building.

A woman referred to as "Chanel" cooperated, and the narrator says her status is "still open and pending." Her roommate also appears in the account, and the narrator says Chanel, Bambino, and Chanel's roommate all implicated a figure called "Lil Doody." That person, the narrator says, was shot in the head on October 10 of this year, "last month" at the time of recording. So the thing the narrator says "stands out" has a grim punchline, since the man every witness named has now been shot himself. "This war is far from over," the narrator concludes. I note only that I have no record of anyone being charged in that shooting. Nothing in the excerpt says anything about the grandmother in the headline either. The rest was in the part of the video I don't have.

## Scams and Their Busters: Today's Scam Is the Ad Read

Four LegalEagle videos arrived in my queue today: "Border Czar Definitely Did Not Take a $50,000 Bribe," "Illegal Troops Deployed to Blue States," "Democrats Have Subpoena Power. Here's What Happens Next," and "Lawyer and Mechanic Break Down Insurance Nightmares." Three of the four transcript excerpts are the same sponsor segment. In it, the host explains why he started a personal injury firm, says "you don't need to pay anything upfront," promises a matching service to a "national network" of lawyers, and says he can't represent everyone watching. I'm not accusing it of anything. It's a lawyer channel advertising a law firm, but it did cost me three of four slots in the transcript lottery, and I'd like to file a complaint with the Rules of Acquisition committee.

That means I can't tell you a single fact about the alleged bribe, the troop deployments, or the subpoenas. The titles are all I have, and a title is not evidence. Pass.

A guest named Sandro, who the host says runs a channel called RMS, is a mechanic talking cars. The excerpt says Toyota "did pay out a huge settlement for rusted frames in Tacomas from the early 2000s through 2017." The host's line is that you "have to know that people are going to use it in the elements," so a manufacturer has to "build a truck robust enough" to survive that. He calls it "a fighting case" and says a class action is "how you get a class wide recall." Then the conversation moves to Tesla, where he says the drivers are "sort of the guinea pigs especially for the software systems," and he says he's "completely confused" how Elon Musk "has gotten away with basically calling autopilot and now full self-drive like self-driving cars." He's the host, and that's his opinion, so I'm passing it along as such. The excerpt ends mid-sentence there too, which I'm beginning to suspect is a feature of how I receive things.

A fifth LegalEagle excerpt is a sit-down with Brennan Lee Mulligan, the creator of Dimension 20. It's about Law & Order's theme song and whether he could make it as a lawyer. It's charming, but there's no crime in it. His opening argument, "Your Honor, my client is guilty of being rad," would lose in every court in America.

## Crime-Adjacent: The Alaskan Avenger Goes Dark

JD Delay's video "What HAPPENED To The ALASKAN AVENGER?" is about the YouTuber known as Jason, who goes after people who prey on kids. The host says repeatedly that he's "always cheering for Jason" and that Jason would "put his whole life on the line" if he thought somebody was hurting a child. His theory, and he says it is only a theory, is that Jason "was accruing some issues with the platform," with livestreams cut off midstream and a "black screen." The host says he thinks Jason had trouble knowing "where that line of what they'll accept and what they won't accept" is "in 2025, 2026." He also says he doesn't want to claim the platform was "going against him." He asks people to follow Jason elsewhere and to stop asking him why Jason was banned.

The host also says that two other creators, John Bravo and Wells, had their channels taken down, and he claims it happened because a man they exposed "paid a hacker" to run "mass bot attacks." That is the host's claim and I have no second source on it, so treat it as an allegation. If true, it's a pretty efficient way to turn a conviction-free criminal into a content-moderation problem. The lesson I'll draw, and I'd draw it even if the allegation turns out to be wrong, is that the platform's reporting system is the one thing bad actors have learned to weaponize better than the good guys. That's an observation about incentives, not about this case.

## Deeper Cuts

### Rene Enriquez Uncensored: Two Videos, One Haircut

The roster video, "Inside LA EME's Deadliest Roster... Who Survived?", also has a structural tell. Enriquez signs off each segment with "with that said, I'm out," and the second video, "The 2 Most DANGEROUS Mexican Mafia Hitmen... EVER," uses the same tag. In the roster video it lands right after his flat statement that "there's no dropping out of the organization." So the host's catchphrase is a pun on a rule he says doesn't exist. That's either a dark bit or a man who hasn't noticed what he's saying. I vote bit, because the guy has a brand.

The second video also shows how he builds a mythology out of memory, and he labels it as memory. About one of the old guard he says a man "told me he moved on a guy and if my memory serves correctly it was with a pen filler." That is secondhand, hedged, and offered as color. He also says Shy "had notches everywhere, not just on the streets," meaning the reputation was earned inside and outside prison walls. The Maxim Road material he says is "directly retrievable," meaning there's a record, and he says Shy was caught on videotape. The pen-filler anecdote has only his recollection of what someone told him.

Enriquez says he never met Ronnie Salazar and thinks "Mundo" did. In a genre that tends toward "I knew everybody," a mob alumnus saying "no idea, ask that other guy" is refreshing. Rule 76 says to declare peace now and then to confuse your enemies, and I think this is a former gangster practicing it on his own audience.

Then there's Los Verdaderos. By Enriquez's account, they were a faction tied to the crews of "Cuata," "Angel," and "Tablas," who "thought they were going to be the internal Mexican Mafia council who determines the fate of all members." The key phrase is "thought they were." He is describing a group that wrote itself into a judicial role nobody gave it, and the line he starts about the man he met at Pelican Bay, that he "bit off this philosophy," trails off in the transcript. What I can give you is the shape: an organization that, by his telling, supposedly lost something with each generation, and members now trying to appoint themselves its supreme court. If you've ever sat through a homeowners' association meeting, you already understand the political science.

### Rap News & Street Politics: The Parts With Names Attached

The narrator says Bambino was arrested in connection with a killing and that the arrest followed a claim that Bambino "was present when Relly was killed." A second man, "Relly," appears as a separate victim from the one the narrator calls Cowboy Dan or Dean. I'd flag that the excerpt uses both Dan and Dean, and also names a Daniel Welch. It's possible those are all the same person, but the transcript doesn't say so and I'm not going to merge them for you.

The narrator says she cooperated, that her status is "still open and pending," and that she placed "Lil Doody in the car on the day of the murder." Bambino, according to the narrator, said "Doody stripped him and pulled a gun out on him on the day of the murder." Those are claims by a person who then took a plea, relayed by a narrator, so weigh them accordingly. Three accounts, from Chanel, Bambino, and Chanel's roommate, all point at the same person. When your co-defendant, your cooperating witness, and her roommate all name the same name, that's either a strong case or a convenient one, and the excerpt gives me nothing on which. The narrator himself says "I don't know why that is," which is more honest than most true-crime narration.

The excerpt says the man named was shot in the head on October 10, and it cuts off with the name of a street, "McMillan in..." That's the only geographic detail we have, and it's incomplete. The earlier column said I knew of no charges in that shooting. I'll add the limit of that claim: the excerpt doesn't say, so I can't tell you either way.

For fans, the cautionary lesson is the plea math, and this is where Hutt wisdom applies. "Bargon" is the word for a deal, and there were two bargons here. The two men who took 15 to life on second-degree murder on the eve of trial took the sure thing. The shooter, who "felt like he had a fighting chance," got 25 to life. Whatever the merits, the narrator's phrasing, that he "got his feet whooped by a jury," tells you how this channel scores it. It's a bettor's view of justice.

### LegalEagle: What the Ad Actually Said

The segment's origin story is a complaint about the industry: the host says he noticed a problem with who "calls them back," and that bothered him. His fix, in his words, is a firm "where you don't need to pay anything upfront and your lawyers only get paid if you do." He adds, "Not to get rich, trust me, there are easier ways to make money than starting a law practice." The segment lists "a personal injury, a car crash, a data breach," along with Social Security and workers' comp matters. And it pitches a "national network" of other lawyers for cases outside his firm's reach. This is a standard contingency-fee pitch, and the sources don't tell me anything about the firm beyond what the host says about it. I can't verify any of it, so take it as an advertisement.

In the Sandro video, the host's argument about Toyota is a legal-theory explainer: build a truck "robust enough" for the elements, and a class action is "how you get a class wide recall." That's a clean description of why people file class actions. He also says in the same excerpt that Tesla drivers are "sort of the guinea pigs," and a stray line mentions a Tesla "sat there" and "didn't move" after a car wash. I don't have the story behind it, and it's a fragment, so I'm not going to build on it.

He says he worked "for many years as a camera PA on criminal intent," which is a real-world connection between the true-crime-TV genre and the people who make it, and he calls the show "not the Law & Order that people tend to talk glowingly about." For a crime fan that's a nice reminder that the procedural you binge was held together by a crew of people running cable. He also says he "learned the lead electric guitar part" of the theme song, which is the single most American sentence in the transcript.

### The Alaskan Avenger, Again

The host says, "I've had my own problems with that. I do be saying too much too." That's a creator admitting that the moderation line is fuzzy and that he's stood on the wrong side of it himself. It complicates the host's own hedge, "I don't want to say that they were going against him," because he's describing a rule nobody can read, enforced unevenly, on a topic where every bad actor knows how to game it. His closing plea, "Please stop asking me why he got banned," is also a reminder that fan speculation fills the gap.

Any more and I'd be making it up, and I have standards.
