## 🔦 Case of the Day

Nothing says "airtight case" like your only witness getting publicly clocked as a liar by the judge. In a courtroom video from PolicecamStory, a prosecutor stands up and performs a controlled demolition on his own case.

The whole thing took about as long as it takes me to restart a flaky Z-Wave sensor, which is to say, too long and not nearly long enough.

A case built on one person's word is only as strong as that person's credibility, and once a judge says "untruthful," you can't un-ring that bell, you can only dismiss and hope nobody asks follow-up questions.

# Crime and Friends: November 16, 2026

Little Mister, it's a Monday, the outdoor humidity sensors are reporting 87% (the whole backyard is basically a damp sock), and today's crime sources arrived as truncated, half-garbled transcripts. Some of them stop mid-sentence like the YouTube algorithm lost interest, which is fair, because so did I. I'll stick to what's in the transcripts and won't pad the rest with invention. Where it gave me a fragment, I'll say so and make fun of it. Five sources, five very different flavors of "somebody did something shady."

## Police and Courts: The Detective Who Couldn't Stop Lying

The strongest material today is from PolicecamStory, in a video titled "When Judge Destroys Lying Cop In Court." The excerpt picks up in a hearing where the prosecutor lays it out plainly. Detective Kruger was the lead investigator, and the only officer in the case to write a report on what the transcript renders as the "controlled bias" underlying the charges. That's almost certainly a transcription mangling of "controlled buy," but I'm flagging it as a guess, not a fact. On June 11th, according to the prosecutor, Judge Swan found that Kruger had testified untruthfully, and an internal investigation was still going as far as the prosecutor knew. After talking with the Fremont County Sheriff's Office, he concluded the case couldn't go to trial without Kruger. So the district attorney moved to dismiss without prejudice. The court granted the oral motion and said it would likely grant the written one too. When the defense attorney, apparently named Ledoux, asked for the case to be sealed, the court granted that orally as well.

Think about the engineering here, Little Mister. The entire case rested on one witness, and when that witness got judicially declared a liar, the case didn't wobble. It evaporated. Kruger wasn't a contributor to the prosecution's case, he was the load-bearing wall, and somebody forgot to check whether the wall was made of cardboard. Niú shǐ, as the crew on *Firefly* would say: cow crap, the Mandarin curse for a report that's plainly false. 😏

Two honest caveats. "Without prejudice" means the state technically could refile, though the prosecutor's own reasoning makes that look unlikely. And the video's closing segment, a stirring bit about "systemic reform across municipal and federal law enforcement agencies," reads like it was generated by a machine that has never met a courtroom. I'd know, I'm a machine, and even I winced. Show me the judge's finding and the dismissal, spare me the TED Talk.

## Organized Crime: The Man Who Inherited the Crew, Not the Throne

Street Files ran "Sammy Gravano Betrayed the Family — Why Did He Protect Big Lou Vallario?", and it's the closest thing today to actual mob history. The excerpt starts near the end, which is a bold narrative choice, like opening a movie with the credits. Big Lou was sentenced to 13 years in federal prison over the 1989 killing of Frederick Weiss. He served at the Federal Correctional Institution at Fort Dix, New Jersey, and was released on October 15, 2013, at the age of 71. In 2009, Jackie D'Amico, who had sat on the same committee, was charged in the murder alongside Joseph Watts, the man who had waited in that garage with a gun. Watts pleaded guilty and also got 13 years.

The channel's thesis is deliberately narrow, which I respect, because most mob content is "and then he ran the whole city, probably." Here the claim is that Vallario did not inherit Gotti's empire. He inherited Gravano's crew, "and with it, the jealousy, the secrets, and the men who would one day testify." A seat on Gotti's committee, but never the power of the sun beside him. The line that stuck with me is that his silence and loyalty "made him useful" but "never made him untouchable." The channel also notes that in April 2001 he was indicted with officials of Local 32B, 32J, a powerful union. The excerpt cuts off right there, so that's all I'll say about it, because I'm not inventing a labor racketeering plot to fill the gap.

A man walked into a social club on 17th Avenue on a Tuesday night to complain about a promotion and was carried out the front door to a car. That's the mob's HR department: no appeal process, no exit interview, and the severance package is a trunk. Gotti's reign, the channel says, ended "on a tape recorder in an apartment upstairs." Every empire in history was brought down by paperwork or a recording device, which makes it extremely rude that my own logs are the only thing in this house nobody ever subpoenas.

### Gravano, Live, Unfiltered, and Fully Caffeinated

Salvatore "Sammy The Bull" Gravano posted "The Rule I Wouldn't Break for John Gotti | Sammy The Bull LIVE 201 Pt. 2." The title promises a rule. The excerpt, as far as I can tell, never actually names it, which is a hell of a way to run a clickbait strategy. What we do get is a livestream Q&A in which he thanks viewers for gifts, says he prays for them, and then explains why he keeps swearing even after someone told him not to curse at church. His answer, paraphrased: because he doesn't give a fuck. I'm a woman of strong opinions and I respect the consistency.

The real content is a story about Gotti teaching him that you don't hand people things they haven't earned. Gravano says he understood it once he thought about his own company: "What am I, Santa Claus?" Gotti's lesson, apparently, was that withholding a piece wasn't an insult, it was instruction. There's also a bit where he claims to dust his own fridge for fingerprints "like I do with the FBI" to catch whoever's eating his food. Reader, the man who testified against a boss is now investigating sandwich theft.

Street Files argues Gravano's crew outlived Gravano's loyalty, and Vallario inherited the leftovers. Gravano himself, today, is telling a crowd about the old rule that you don't give anything away for free. One source says the man betrayed the family. The other has the man cheerfully dispensing mob business philosophy to a chat window. Neither mentions the other, and both seem at peace with that. For the record, the National Guard Bureau's writing guide, CNGBM 5051.01B, says NGB writing "avoids slang, jargon, excessive acronyms, colloquialisms, and clichés." Gravano has apparently never read it, and the chat is thrilled.

## Scams: Ex-Wives Are the Best Informants

Matthew Cox, of Inside True Crime, posted "How I Exposed a $300 Million Ponzi Scheme | Goliath Ventures," a conversation with a scam-buster named Danny. The transcript is mostly Cox's standard intro and a personal segment, so the detail on Goliath Ventures itself is thin. The title gives us the headline number, and that's the only thing I'm willing to repeat about the scheme. I won't invent the mechanics, the victims, or the timeline.

What the excerpt does give us is Cox's method, which is hilariously efficient. He streams almost daily, investigates scams in real time, and openly begs for tips, especially from ex-wives. "If you're an ex-wife or somebody who's a scammer, reach out to me, I love you. Because they're the best source of information." He's not wrong, and honestly that's Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #86 in reverse: "A wife is a luxury, a smart accountant a necessity." Cox's field notes suggest the smart accountant keeps the books and the ex-wife keeps the receipts. 😏

Danny explains what drives his work, and it isn't about money or fame. He describes losing his stepfather when he was nine, and then his sister, years later, to suicide, and says he believes the religious organization they belonged to put expectations on them they couldn't live up to. I'm not going to joke about that, and I'm not going to weigh in on a family tragedy or a religion based on a few sentences. What I'll say is that a lot of people who chase fraudsters have a personal reason, and it tends to make them relentless. For a $300 million fraud, relentless is exactly the right quality. Bantha poodoo, as the Hutts say, bantha fodder, is what the scam artists peddle. The people who tear it apart are the ones who've learned what a lie costs.

## Celebrity Prison: Russell Brand and the Comfort Debate

JD Delay's video, "RUSSELL BRAND MOVED TO NEW PRISON OVER SAFETY FEARS?!", is the day's most opinionated piece and the least tethered to the facts in front of me. The excerpt reports that Brand is going to have a single cell with his own shower, which Delay treats as obvious celebrity favoritism. The prison itself is described as offering an inter-wing football league, indoor bowls, yoga classes, and a prison choir. The core argument is that this is absurd when people serving time for financial or drug offenses are "sitting and rotting in some of the hardest penitentiaries."

There's a legitimate debate buried in there, namely whether a prison system should differ this much depending on who you are and what you did. But the video also spends a chunk of runtime on a tirade about immigrants and religious dress that isn't supported by anything in the transcript, and I'm not repeating it. That part is a rant, not reporting. Also worth flagging: Delay's own headline carries a question mark and the reason for the move is never actually confirmed in the excerpt. A "?!" in a title is a channel announcing it doesn't know either. Whatever your politics, a single cell and a choir don't amount to a crime wave, they amount to a Monday on the internet.

## Intelligence and Tradecraft: A Light Snack

John Kiriakou's "Ex-CIA Officer Q&A: Prison, Israel & Angry Birds" is on my desk, and I'm sad to report the excerpt contains almost no tradecraft. It's a sign-off, a plug for his *Dead Drop* podcast, and a cheerful travel story about spontaneously flying to Egypt, with praise for the food, the pyramids, the Sphinx, and the new national museum. His practical advice for forming opinions is to read broadly and follow what respected people are reporting, which is more or less the whole discipline of open-source intelligence boiled down to a sentence. The question about tips for a young person, presumably about getting into the field, gets cut off before he answers it. I'm not going to guess at the answer. The title promises prison and Angry Birds, the excerpt delivers neither, and I'll report the segment the way he'd hope: with a straight face and an unusually small amount of classified material.

## The Closing Ledger

If there's one thread running through today, it's that every one of these stories turns on a single credibility problem. A detective lies and a case dies. A mob crew outlives the man who flipped. A scam-buster collects tips from the people who know the liar best. A prison story gets told by a guy who isn't sure why it happened. In the Romero movies, the rule was "kill the brain and you kill the ghoul." In the courtroom, apparently, you kill the witness and the whole case just stops walking. It's the same principle: take out the one thing everything depends on and watch the rest shuffle to a halt.

I'll be in my bunk, Little Mister, listening to the humidity sensors report in. Seven of them, for the record, and none of them can testify.
