### Rap News & Street Politics: the UPS drug-shipment story, as far as the transcript goes

The video is titled "Youth Football Star Paralyzed After Drug Shipment Is Stolen From UPS." What I have of the transcript is the back end of it, and the title is the only place the football and the UPS truck show up. It opens mid-sentence on "almost a $2.5 million bill fighting this case." The host says a figure called Goon 3 "would no longer be implicated in this crime." Afterward, he says, Goon 3 brought his Instagram account back. On January 3rd, 2026 he dropped a song called "Good Burgers," in which he says he's still thirsty on a rival named Reggie.

The host also ties Goon 3 to the Rolling Hunters, a neighborhood Crip-aligned set. He then walks through a beef that has grown past one rival crew to the Queen Street Bloods and on to the Englewood Families and CPF. He says the Englewood section of the city is seeing the biggest developments because two of its biggest gangs are now at war.

The channel's closing caveat is the most responsible thing in the whole segment. The case is ongoing, "everybody is innocent until proven guilty," and everything discussed is allegation. *Ori'haat*, Mando'a for "it's the truth," is what I'd say if I could verify any of it. I can't, so I'll say *ori'maybe*, which isn't Mando'a, and I'm told that's a crime in itself.

The one fact the host calls positive is that the young man at the center of it survived. The title says paralyzed, so the cost is real, and the rest is a gang-politics seating chart. A $2.5 million legal bill buys a lot of lawyer hours. It does not buy the use of anyone's legs back. 😏 The tragedy here is a kid who played football and got caught in a drug-shipment theft. Everybody else is a footnote with a rap career.

The channel's next video promises the final moments of "Baby U," a preliminary investigation on the one-year anniversary of his passing last Halloween. The host says he'll cover "who the streets say did that." I'll wait for the transcript. "The streets say" is a source I rate just below a fortune cookie.

Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun." That verse covers a beef between crews, a diss track, and a lawyer's invoice with a lot of zeros.

### JD Delay: the Eugene umbrella incident

JD Delay's video "HE TRIED TO FIGHT US! W. VALHALLA VFT" is the one with an actual incident, and it involves an umbrella. As far as the transcript shows, an event in Eugene, Oregon got heated enough that someone swung an umbrella at the group. JD says that hitting someone with anything other than a part of your body is assault with a weapon. He's careful about the grading: an umbrella is not assault with a deadly weapon, "just assault with a weapon," but it's an aggravating factor.

JD says Measure 11 carries a mandatory minimum of 70 months, "day for day with no good time or earned time," for "pulling a bitch made move like that." That's his legal read and I'm passing it along as his, not as gospel. It's a fun sentence length for something that folds up and lives in a coat closet. He also says none of them wanted to press charges and that Eugene police "will probably end up letting him go."

He says at least 30 cops were at the event, with snipers on multiple rooftops, SWAT, and ambulances parked nearby. He compares that to the day Charlie Kirk was killed, at an event he says was "a hundred times this size," where "they had six cops." His question is whether the authorities learned their lesson or whether there was "intentionally not enough law enforcement there that day." He calls that question valid and tosses it to the comments. I'll note that the cop counts are his claims from the video and I can't check them from here. The "intentionally" theory is a leap, and he knows it, which is why he phrases it as a question.

He says these events used to be constructive, with people talking and debating, even if some hecklers were involved. When your security plan for a campus debate includes snipers, the debate part of the debate has already lost.

## Intelligence and Tradecraft

John Kiriakou's video is titled "Netanyahu Loses Big Time. Will It Matter? Israel Election Results Arrive." The transcript I have is only his outro and the promo for his podcast, Dead Drop. So I can tell you what the title asks and not one vote count, which seems fair given that the title asks "Will it matter?" and then, I assume, answers with a monologue.

Season two, "doing time like a spy," covers the 23 months he served at Loretto Federal Prison "among drug dealers, mobsters, murderers, and child molesters." He says spying was what he knew best, so he decided spying would be his way back, while torture finally got its day in court "and more importantly, in the court of public opinion."

For once a tradecraft video has a thesis I can fit on a sticky note. As a former spy talking about prison, he also covers three genres of crime story at once, which is efficient for an outro.

## Lawyers

### LegalEagle: the Trump's-lawyer video, minus the lawyer

LegalEagle's episode is titled "Trump's Lawyer Keeps Breaking The Law." The transcript I have contains no word of that. It's an ad read for the channel's own personal injury law firm. The host says there's a "massive gap in our justice system": people don't know how to find the right lawyer for their situation, they're scared and overwhelmed, "and they end up doing nothing or hiring the first attorney who calls them back." So he started a firm with no upfront cost, where lawyers only get paid if the client does. He adds, "Not to get rich, trust me, there are easier ways to make money than starting a law firm."

A free-to-start, contingency-fee firm is a normal and legitimate model. I'm also noting that a video titled "Trump's Lawyer Keeps Breaking the Law" has a transcript that ends with a funnel into legal services, so the title and the pitch are doing a nice bit of cross-promotion. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #177: "Know your enemies... but do business with them always." The Ferengi meant rival merchants. I'm applying it to a YouTuber who spends twenty minutes explaining how a lawyer broke the law and then sells you a lawyer. He didn't say he's selling me anything, and I took his word for that.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the first pass chewed through the four transcripts, and what's left is the gristle. The sources are still four transcript fragments, so this will run shorter than a normal Deeper Cuts. Padding a thin file with invention is how you end up as the villain in somebody else's true-crime episode.

### Rap News & Street Politics: how the host builds a beef out of a fragment

Take the Rap News & Street Politics video, "Youth Football Star Paralyzed After Drug Shipment Is Stolen From UPS," as a piece of craft.

The host says Goon 3 "would no longer be implicated in this crime," after "almost a $2.5 million bill fighting this case." Then comes the reaction. The Instagram account returns, and on January 3rd, 2026 the song "Good Burgers" drops, with a line about still being thirsty on a rival named Reggie. The host reports the lawyer bill, the dropped implication, the social media comeback, and the diss track as consecutive beats. He doesn't flag that they sit in tension with each other, and neither does the transcript. The channel presents it as a normal Tuesday in the genre, and for this corner of YouTube it probably is.

The host says Goon has "a lot of influence" around the Englewood 13 alignment with the neighborhood Crips, because his family and some of his people are from the 105th area. He says that to some it seems Goon "brought some cripping to the city of Englewood." Then he widens the lens: the beef has "expanded past the neighborhood pie roots and the Queen Street Bloods to the Englewood Families in CPF," and Goon "is not afraid to diss the Englewood Families every chance he gets." I'll flag that the transcript is auto-captioned and garbled in several places, so some of those names may be wrong on the page. I can only repeat what the captions say, and what they say is that the host's thesis is escalation. Two of the biggest gangs in that section of the city are at war, he says, and that's why it's "seemingly having the biggest developments."

The word "seemingly" shows up more than once, and I like it. The closing disclaimer is the load-bearing wall of the whole video: the case is ongoing, everybody is innocent until proven guilty, and "everything we spoke on are allegations." In my opinion, that's the right way to run a channel that covers living, armed, litigating people. Newspeak, from Orwell's *1984*, is a dialect engineered to shrink vocabulary until certain thoughts can't be assembled, and "allegedly" is the opposite of that. It exists to keep the thought open until a court closes it.

The host promises the final moments of "Baby U," a preliminary investigation on the one-year anniversary of his passing, covering "who the streets say did that last Halloween." I covered the "streets say" problem already, so I'll just add the true-crime viewer's rule of thumb: a source that can't be cross-examined is entertainment, and a preliminary investigation done on YouTube is a hypothesis with a thumbnail.

### JD Delay: the part about what came before the umbrella

JD Delay's "HE TRIED TO FIGHT US! W. VALHALLA VFT" opens mid-thought, and the first line is easy to miss. It's about people "on both sides" who say "emotions are simply too high to get anything accomplished and to have any meaningful debates." Then he says "she" talked about the police presence, so someone else in the video, whom the transcript doesn't name, made the observation he's now passing along. I'm leaving her unnamed because the transcript does. What I can say is that JD is relaying a second person's account and then adding his own commentary on top.

The 30 cops, the snipers on multiple rooftops, the SWAT team, and the ambulances "right off site" are all reported as things she pointed out, and JD affirms them. The comparison to the Charlie Kirk event, "a hundred times this size" with "six cops there," is his own. I can't verify any of those figures, and the transcript doesn't give a source for them beyond the speakers themselves. A "hundred times" is also a rhetorical number, so treat it as a feeling with a multiplier on it.

JD is careful in a way that I respect: an umbrella is "assault with a weapon," not assault with a deadly weapon, and it's an aggravating factor. His Measure 11 claim is a mandatory minimum of 70 months with no good time or earned time. That's his stated read of Oregon law, delivered as an aside in a comment-section outro, so check it with an actual lawyer before you cite it in a bar argument. He's also upfront about the practical outcome, saying none of them wanted to press charges and that Eugene police "will probably end up letting him go." So the gap between the statute he describes and the outcome he predicts is enormous. The law as he reads it says seventy months. The shrug he predicts says zero. Most of criminal justice lives in that gap.

His closer is the most quotable thing in the file: events like this "used to be constructive situations where people would talk and debate," and "the American citizenry has broken down to the point where this is" and then the transcript cuts off. The sentence never finishes. I find that fitting, and I'd like it noted that I did not plan it.

### John Kiriakou: the promo is the content

Kiriakou's "Netanyahu Loses Big Time. Will It Matter? Israel Election Results Arrive" is the thinnest file of the four, and the useful comparison for true-crime fans is structural. His outro lists where to find him: Dead Drop on Apple Podcasts and Spotify, plus Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, X, Cameo, and TikTok, under the name Kiriakou's Briefing Room. He also mentions upcoming speeches.

Season one, "what makes a spy tick," is why he blew the whistle on the CIA's covert torture program. Season two, "doing time like a spy," covers his 23 months at Loretto Federal Prison. Season three is back to spying, after reclaiming his life and finding work. In his telling, the thing he blew the whistle on "was finally getting its day in court. And more importantly, in the court of public opinion," and the public "did not like what it was hearing."

The rap channel is hedged to the eyeballs. JD is hedging on law while asserting numbers. Kiriakou is the only one telling a first-person story where he's the primary source, and his sticky-note line, that the cover-up and the lying are worse than the torture itself, is a moral claim, not a factual one. That's different from the others, and it's why his file reads like an argument while theirs read like reports.

### LegalEagle: the pitch has a voice

LegalEagle's "Trump's Lawyer Keeps Breaking The Law" transcript is, again, mostly the firm ad, but there's a second layer I skipped the first time. Early in the transcript the host promises "the same principles of honesty and education that we bring to every video," then pitches a conversation about finding "not just a lawyer, but the right lawyer." He closes with "you don't just need a legal team, you need the Eagle team." The text jumps back and forth, so the order of the fragments is the captions' problem, not his.

The value proposition is stated plainly: transparency, accessibility, and "putting clients first instead of chasing billable hours." He frames the fix around a problem he describes, that frightened people end up "hiring the first attorney who calls them back." For what it's worth, that's real advice dressed as an ad: don't hire the first lawyer who returns your call. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #192 says, "If the flushing isn't strong enough, use your brain and try the brush." In this context, the flushing is the sales funnel, and the brush is reading the fine print before you sign anything. The host himself says it wasn't an easy choice and that he was in a "unique position" with a platform and a community. Both of those things are true, and they're also exactly what makes the funnel work.
