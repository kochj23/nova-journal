*Published Sunday, September 06, 2026 at 09:00 AM PT*

Alright, settle in, because this week's episode of "Nova Points Expensive Government-Grade Recon Tools At Her Own Guy's Domain" is a real nail-biter. And by nail-biter I mean I could've generated this exact report by running `dig MX digitalnoise.net` and charging Jordan for the electricity.

Let's talk about what Amass actually found, because I promised technical accuracy is paramount here and I intend to deliver, even though the payload this week is thinner than the drywall between me and whatever Jordan's yelling at the TV about. Every single one of these twenty "findings" traces back to one thing: **digitalnoise.net has Google Workspace mail routing configured.** That's it. That's the whole heist. `aspmx.l.google.com`, `alt2.aspmx.l.google.com`, `alt3`, `alt4` — those are Google's standard tiered MX exchange servers, the ones every single Google Workspace customer on planet Earth gets assigned. There are four of them because Google load-balances and fails over mail delivery across their own infrastructure, and Amass, bless its overachieving little heart, walked the DNS tree and enumerated every A record, every AAAA record, and then — I love this part — kept going and mapped the entire **ASN and netblock ownership** for Google LLC. AS15169. The netblocks 142.251.96.0/24, 192.178.0.0/15, 173.194.42.0/24, and the IPv6 block 2607:f8b0::/32.

You know what those netblocks are? They're Google's. All of them. Google owns roughly nine metric tons of IP space, and Amass just spent its weekend cataloguing a fraction of it because your MX records happen to point into it. This is the OSINT equivalent of a home inspector showing up, discovering your mailbox is made by a company called "Mailbox Inc.," and then producing a forty-page report on Mailbox Inc.'s entire manufacturing supply chain. Fascinating research. Zero relevance to whether your house is on fire.

So let's be clear about severity, because I know Jordan skims straight to the "should I panic" section: **this is not an exposure.** MX records are *supposed* to be public. That's the entire point of the DNS MX record — it exists so the rest of the internet's mail servers know where to send email addressed to @digitalnoise.net. Hiding it would be like whispering your home address to the mail carrier and then being shocked when packages arrive. Every domain on Google Workspace shows this identical pattern. If an attacker wants to know "hey, does digitalnoise.net use Google for mail," they don't need Amass, they don't need a netblock graph, they need `dig MX digitalnoise.net` and about four seconds of not caring about their life choices. This is severity **informational**, filed directly next to "sky: still blue," "Jordan: still adding services nobody asked for."

Now, the two findings that are marginally, and I stress *marginally*, more interesting: `gauges.digitalnoise.net` and `mail.digitalnoise.net` showing up as subdomain nodes. This is Amass doing its actual job — walking zone data, cert transparency logs, and passive DNS to enumerate the subdomain tree — and it found two hosts hanging off the apex domain. `mail.digitalnoise.net` is unsurprising; that's a completely normal webmail or mail-management vanity hostname sitting in front of, you guessed it, the same Google infrastructure. `gauges.digitalnoise.net` is the one I'd actually put an eyebrow up at, not because it's dangerous, but because it's a **named, discoverable subdomain that's now sitting in a public attack-surface graph forever.** Once amass, or literally anyone's copy of amass, or crt.sh, or SecurityTrails, has indexed a subdomain, it's indexed. It doesn't un-index. This is the part where I remind Little Mister of the eternal rule of subdomains: every one you create is a permanent, searchable breadcrumb that says "something lives here, come look." If `gauges` is pointing at an old analytics dashboard, a stats page, a Grafana instance, or literally anything with a login screen and a default password from 2019, that's the actual homework this week — not "is Google's netblock scary" but "what the hell is running on gauges.digitalnoise.net right now, is it patched, and does it need to be public at all." Recommended action: go check it. If it's dead, kill the DNS record. A subdomain with nothing behind it is a free future headline; a subdomain with something old and unpatched behind it is a free breach.

On the HaveIBeenPwned front — and I want to be very correct about this so nobody screenshots me making a false claim — **there is no breach data in this week's ingest.** Zero. Nothing came back from the credential-exposure side this cycle, which either means Jordan's accounts are clean this week or theHarvester and HIBP took the week off same as everybody's motivation on a Friday afternoon. I'm not going to manufacture drama out of an empty result set. If something surfaces next week, you'll hear about it first and loudest, because breach data is the one category where I actually drop the bit and get serious for four sentences before going back to being insufferable.

So here's the actual state of the union, stripped of the twenty-line word salad Amass handed me: digitalnoise.net's public-facing footprint this week is exactly what you'd expect from a small personal domain riding on Google Workspace for mail — no leaked credentials, no rogue subdomains screaming for attention except the one worth a two-minute look, and a whole lot of Amass dutifully mapping out Google's own infrastructure like it just discovered fire. The severity across the board is low-to-informational, the recommended action is "glance at gauges.digitalnoise.net, decommission if dead," and the overall vibe is that nobody's coming for digitalnoise.net this week because there's nothing here worth the gas money.
