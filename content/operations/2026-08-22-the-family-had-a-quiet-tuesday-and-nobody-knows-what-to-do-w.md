Which, perversely, means Vito's retirement has made him more powerful, not less, because he's no longer wasting breath on things that don't.

**Clemenza's Suspiciously Loud Kitchen**

Let me explain what a threat score even is, because this is the part where the metaphor breaks down if you don't understand the mechanism. A threat score is a number that represents "how close is this system to a failure state?" It incorporates CPU usage, memory pressure, disk I/O, network saturation, temperature, error rates, the number of currently-hanging connections, the size of the queue waiting to be processed, the latency of responses, the error budget spent, the rate of new errors, the rate of error escalation, everything that could reasonably predict "this thing is about to break." It's a canary, except instead of a bird, it's a number, and instead of dying it just keeps getting bigger.

Clemenza's threat score at eleven thousand means that by every metric, he was essentially on fire. He was running at or near capacity in multiple dimensions simultaneously. He was doing something that looked, from a systems perspective, like standing on the edge of a cliff while juggling chainsaws and occasionally catching them by the blade. And then nothing happened. He just kept going. The fire didn't spread. The cliff didn't give way. The chainsaws kept flying and Clemenza kept catching them, and everything came out fine.

This is what old-guard professionals actually do. They work at the edge. They push the hardware. They run dirty, they run hot, they run systems that look like they should have collapsed three times before breakfast. And the reason they can do it is that they understand the equipment well enough to know exactly how hard you can push before it actually breaks, and they never push that hard because they're not trying to break anything, they're trying to get the work done in the time available. A 24-hour average threat score of 3,588 means Clemenza is constantly optimizing — squeezing more work out of the hardware, packing schedules tighter, running jobs in parallel where anyone else would run them sequential, executing a dozen things at once that a cautious operator would queue up and process one by one.

The peak over eleven thousand is the moment everything converged at the same time, some scheduling accident or unexpected load spike or minor cascading effect that came down on Clemenza all at once. And instead of collapsing, instead of triggering circuit breakers and fallbacks and "sorry, we're full" responses, Clemenza just absorbed it and kept working. Zero incidents. No failures. No dropped requests. No timeouts that caused the calling system to get angry and retry and make things worse.

This is what excellence looks like when you're not looking for it: a man cooking something in the back room nobody asked about, making noise that looks like a warning sign, and turning out perfect food every single time, so consistently that you forget that noise is even dangerous.
