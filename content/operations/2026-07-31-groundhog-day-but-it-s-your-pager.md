The good news: your incident response machinery is barely sweating. The bad news: your alert volume went up 29% this week, which means either your infrastructure got measurably worse, you added more things to watch that are failing in expected ways, or you've got a tuning problem that needs a machete. Spoiler alert—it's probably all three, and I'm about to walk you through why most of it is noise you should stop hearing.

Let me start with what ISN'T broken. Your incident team is holding the line: 382 incidents opened this week, 378 closed, median time-to-resolve hovering right around 183 minutes. That's not panic-mode. That's not "sirens at 3am." That's controlled. Your incident throughput is balanced. Incidents are getting *seen* and *fixed*. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #39 says praise is cheap and I should heap it generously, so here it is: you're not on fire. Your ops people are doing their jobs. You can stop holding your breath for exactly one paragraph.

But let's peel that 183-minute number open. Three hours from detection to resolution is solid, but it's not uniform across severity tiers. If that median includes everything from "a sensor misfired" to "the database was offline for ninety seconds," then the mix matters. The fact that you closed 378 out of 382 opened—that's 98.95% closure rate within the week—tells me the incidents that *do* stick around are either genuinely hard or genuinely rare. That's good sign number two. Your team isn't accumulating a backlog of ancient incidents that nobody knows how to fix. The queue is flowing. When an incident lands, someone picks it up, traces the problem, and pushes a fix or works around it. That's the baseline health check, and you're passing it.

Now the actual pattern. That 29% jump in warning-level alerts masks something important: *not all warnings are created equal*. Let me break what's actually screaming vs. what's just background radiation.

**The rising tide (signal, probably):** Your scheduler, security, Unifi, and soil alerts are all trending *up* week-over-week. That's the stuff to care about. Scheduler alerts going vertical suggests something is either retrying harder or failing more often—either way, it deserves a look. A scheduler that's healthily retrying is one thing; a scheduler that's thrashing or queuing jobs that keep failing is something else. When a task queue starts firing more warnings, it usually means one of three things: either legitimate load increased and you're hitting your scheduler's concurrency limits (which is fine if you know about it and plan for it), jobs started taking longer and previous invocations are still running when new ones arrive (a classic thundering herd if not managed), or your retry backoff strategy is aggressive enough to flood the queue with retry attempts. The pattern matters because each requires a different response. If it's load, you scale the scheduler. If it's duration creep, you optimize the jobs or split them. If it's runaway retries, you cap or backoff the retry logic.

Security alerts rising in tandem with Unifi tells me you've either added stricter detection (good), surfaced existing problems (okay), or something in your network stack is actually degrading (not good, but at least you'll know it). The Unifi trend is particularly worth examining because network warnings often correlate with infrastructure load—if your network is speaking louder, something upstream probably got busier. That could be legitimate traffic increase, a poorly-tuned monitoring threshold, or an actual network problem like a congested link or a loop. Nine new devices joined your network this week with zero rogue APs flagged, which is actually important data: it means either your onboarding process is clean and devices are being registered properly, or your rogue-AP detection is actually working and not just generating false alarms. Either way, it's not chaos down there. The security alert rise isn't ambient noise from unauthorized hardware trying to phone home.

The soil alert climb is newer-system territory—if that's a recent addition you're still tuning, ignore it. New sensors always need calibration; the first two weeks of data from any monitoring system is basically just learning the operating band. If it's been stable for months and just jumped, pay attention. If it's a brand-new integration from the last release, file a mental note and give it two more weeks before you take the trend seriously. Soil monitoring is more specialized than most alerts—if you're tracking it at all, there's a reason, and that reason is usually "we discovered a real problem once and don't want to miss it again." Respect that reasoning even if the current trend seems like noise.

**The noise (signal, easing):** App, backup (appears twice in your brief, probably a duplicate or two closely related patterns), and trafficcam alerts are all *easing* week-over-week. This is the background radiation that should be tuned into the floor or filtered out entirely. The fact that they're still firing hundreds of times tells me someone wrote alert rules that trigger on "working as designed" and never went back to silence them. Trafficcam specifically—if it's firing dozens of times a week and trending down, it's probably a retry loop or a stale alert that survived last quarter's cleanup. A camera that's retrying its network connection when the connection is briefly down is normal and expected; an alert that fires every time that happens is not. You've probably got a threshold that should be "consecutive failures greater than 3" but is actually set to "any single failure," or you've got debounce disabled when it should be enabled. The fix is easy: raise the threshold, add debounce, or both. Then the camera can hiccup on network transitions without waking anyone up. Consider nuking the entire alert rule if you've gone three months without an actual TV crew showing up to the camera because it went offline.

Backup alerts firing hundreds of times with one easing and one stable? That's a rule that's both too chatty *and* possibly broken. The rising one is probably real-ish—it's surfacing something worth hearing about—but the easing one is definitely noise. It's firing less often, which means either the underlying condition is getting better on its own (in which case you don't need to hear about it at every step), or your detection logic is catching it faster and fixing it before the alert even fires (in which case the alert was noise to begin with). Backup systems are usually "never fail, but when they do, you need to know immediately," which means backup alerts should be rare and high-confidence. If you're getting hundreds of backup alerts per week, either your backups are failing constantly (which would show up in that open-incident count and would already be a priority), or your alerting is too sensitive. The fact that you're closing 98% of incidents suggests backups aren't actually failing that often, which means the alerts are just loud for no reason.

Your red-team and blue-team are both actively running (automated pentest and SIEM detection, respectively), and purple-team detection validation has reported in. That's the security ops version of "all hands on deck," and it's the right call given the security alert trend. You're not sleepwalking through your security posture. The fact that these are active and reporting suggests someone built automation around your security testing—they're not one-off jobs that only run when someone remembers to kick them, they're part of your regular cadence. That's the kind of organizational muscle that actually catches problems. The network saw 9 new devices and flagged zero rogue APs, which means either your onboarding is clean or your rogue-AP detection is actually working—both respectable outcomes. Clean onboarding is the preferred one because it means you're not bringing in untrusted hardware by accident; if your detection is catching rogues after the fact, that's a good failsafe but ideally it never has to trigger.

The shape of this week is: *volume is up, but incident resolution isn't slowing down.* That usually means you've either added more watchers to your fleet (more monitoring = more alerts, but faster fixes if they're good alerts), tuned detection thresholds lower (catches more real problems but also more false positives), or both. A healthy response to increased alert volume is increased alert *quality*, not just increased alert *velocity*. If you're getting 29% more alerts but your MTTR is stable, that's either because the new alerts are better at catching problems in real time (so the fix is faster), or because the new alerts are mostly noise (so you waste time on things that weren't actually broken). The incident closure rate will tell you which: if you're closing incidents just as fast, the new alerts are probably real. If incidents are starting to pile up or the fixes are getting slower, the alerts are probably noise.

Let me dig into what each alert category trend actually *means* for your operational posture. The scheduler alerts are infrastructure health—they tell you whether your job engine is breathing hard. Rising scheduler alerts, if they're the right kind of alerts, are like your fitbit telling you your heart rate went up during a workout; it means you're doing work. The question is whether it's *productive* work (load you expected) or *concerning* work (load you didn't, or load you expected but your scheduler can't handle). The security alerts are defensive posture—they tell you whether your walls are holding. Rising security alerts could mean attackers are knocking harder (which is why the red-team is running—to simulate that and make sure your blue-team catches it), or they could mean your detection is just more sensitive now (which is fine as long as you're not burned out by false alarms). Unifi alerts are network health, which is infrastructure again but at a different layer. Rising Unifi alerts usually mean traffic pattern changes or device density changes; the nine new devices provide some context, though they're probably not enough to explain the full trend. You'd need to know if all nine are network-intensive or if most are idle things like lightbulbs or door locks.

The easing alerts—app, backup, trafficcam—are the ones where trend direction actually tells you whether you have a problem or just bad tuning. App alerts easing is good: it means either something in your app stack stabilized, or you already fixed the problem and the alerts are just lingering. Backup alerts easing is also good if it means the problem was transient and is gone. Trafficcam alerts easing is good for the same reason: the camera is probably fine now or the alert was never worth firing in the first place. In all three cases, easing trends should point toward *deletion* of the alert rule, not just complacency. An alert that's firing less often is either becoming obsolete or already *is* obsolete; waiting for it to stop entirely before you remove it is just leaving dead wood in your config.

Now let's talk about the nine open incidents. That number by itself isn't alarming—some incidents naturally take longer than three hours to resolve. A database migration takes longer. A security incident takes longer because you need to trace the scope before you fix it. A network partition takes longer because you might need hardware-level investigation. But those incidents should all have owners and status updates at least once an hour. If any of them hit six hours without progress, escalate it—that's usually the point where you either need fresh eyes or higher-level access. The fact that your median is 183 minutes but you've got nine incidents over that suggests your tail is starting to get fat. One or two outliers is normal. Nine is worth a glance. Check whether they're all the same type of problem (which would suggest a systematic issue) or whether they're scattered across different categories (which would suggest they're just hard). If they're scattered, you're fine. If they're clustered, you've got a pattern to investigate.

Here's the critical point about alert tuning that most orgs miss: *better* is not the same as *more*. A 29% increase in alerts is not automatically bad. A 29% increase in *good* alerts is fine and probably expected as you grow your infrastructure or add new services. A 29% increase in *bad* alerts (false positives, too-sensitive thresholds, noise) is a serious problem because it erodes confidence in your alerting system. Alert fatigue is real. When your team stops trusting alerts, they stop responding quickly. When they stop responding quickly, your MTTR goes up. When your MTTR goes up, small problems become big ones. The fact that your MTTR hasn't moved suggests either the new 29% of alerts are genuinely good, or your team is just powering through the noise. Both are unsustainable long-term—the former requires vigilance to maintain signal quality, the latter will burn your team out within weeks.

The way to distinguish signal from noise is to audit your alert rules systematically. For each alert that's trending down or stable, ask: "If this alert fires and we don't do anything about it, what's the worst that happens?" If the answer is "nothing bad, it resolves on its own," that's an alert you should delete. If the answer is "someone gets paged at 3am for something that's not actually broken," that's an alert you should tune (raise the threshold, add debounce, make it require multiple consecutive failures before firing). If the answer is "a customer-facing service degrades," keep the alert but make sure the threshold is set to catch it reliably without false positives. The rising alerts (scheduler, security, Unifi, soil) deserve the inverse question: "If this alert fires, do we have a runbook for it? Can we fix it in under an hour? Can we fix it in under ten minutes?" If the answer to all three is yes, it's a good alert. If not, either the alert needs tuning (so it only fires when it's actually fixable) or you need to build the runbook (so when it fires, you know what to do).

Here's what I'd do with this data: (1) **Mute or fix the easing alerts.** They're noise, and noise degrades signal. Start with trafficcam—if it's firing dozens of times a week and you've never actually had to dispatch someone to a camera because an alert found a problem, delete the rule entirely. Then do the same audit on the backup and app alerts. For each one, trace back to the last time that alert actually prevented a problem or caught a real issue. If you can't find a recent example (say, in the last month), delete it. (2) **Read the spec on the rising alerts.** Understand if they're new detection (you added a new sensor or rule, which is good because you're being more thorough) or detection of new problems (something in your infrastructure actually got worse, which is fine if you know about it and plan for it). For scheduler, dig into whether you've added new jobs or whether existing jobs are retrying more often. For security, read the red-team report and see what they're finding. For Unifi, check your network load graphs against last week and see if there's a correlation. For soil, decide whether it's a new sensor that needs calibration or an existing one that found a real trend. (3) **Check whether any of your 9 open incidents are blocking on something external.** An incident waiting for vendor support, waiting for a customer to respond, or waiting for a deployment window is not the same as an incident that's stuck because nobody knows how to fix it. If they're blocked externally, send a status update and move on. If they're stuck, escalate. (4) **If soil alerts are new, check if the sensor is any good or if it's just noisy.** New sensors need baseline tuning. Give it two weeks of data and then re-evaluate. Don't assume it's broken; new sensors are often calibrating. But also don't assume it's gospel; a noisy sensor is worse than no sensor, so set a low bar for "is this worth keeping?" (5) **Don't panic about the 29% jump.** You're still closing incidents faster than you're opening them, which means your response isn't drowning. The machinery is still working. The question is whether the new inputs are signal or noise, and that requires data you probably need to gather.

One more thing: if this trend holds through next week, you've got a systematic change. Watch for it. Right now it looks like a one-week bump from some combination of new devices, new rules, and noise that needs tuning. If the high alerts stay high and the rising alerts keep climbing, then you've got actual new work. Adapt accordingly. If you're adding services every week, you should expect alert volume to creep up proportionally—that's normal and fine. If alert volume is going up but service count isn't, then something in your existing infrastructure got noisier and that requires investigation. The goal is to keep your alerts speaking truth: every alert that fires should reflect something worth knowing about, and most importantly, something worth fixing. The moment your team stops trusting that principle, your whole incident response system starts to degrade. You're not there yet. Keep it that way.
