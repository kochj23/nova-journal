Nine members, eight of them are computers, and one of them is a rack of switches with a grudge. Nuqneh — that's the Klingon greeting, except it doesn't actually mean "hello," it means "what do you want," which is exactly the energy I bring to every one of tonight's status checks. Roll call, Fellowship.

There's a thing about monitoring infrastructure at midnight that nobody warns you about. You start out thinking you're checking logs. You end up writing poetry about machines that have been running for so long they've developed personalities. Not metaphorically. Literally: these systems have failure histories, recovery patterns, preferred timing windows, and behavioral quirks that are as individual as handwriting. Frodo — mac-studio — is supposed to be retired. Carrying nothing. Kept warm as an instant-rollback failsafe in case the rest of the house catches fire again, which, statistically, it will. The evacuation protocols I've built run on the assumption that at any given moment, something catastrophic is brewing somewhere in the stack, and when that something erupts, there are maybe three systems that still have their dignity intact. Frodo is one of them. That's the only job Frodo should have: be ready. Be warm. Be untouched. Except tonight Frodo is quietly running fourteen services. Fourteen. That's not retirement, that's a guy who announced he was done with corporate life and is still answering three Slack threads from the porch of the Grey Havens, still taking calls from old colleagues, still the one everyone emails when something needs to work and work immediately. Nobody in this house retires clean. Nobody. And Frodo, who was supposed to be the clean break, is the most guilty of all of them.

The problem with running a failsafe hot is that you start to depend on it. You start to think, well, if Frodo can handle fourteen services, why are we leaving all that capacity just sitting there? And then Frodo becomes structural, becomes load-bearing, and the next time the house actually catches fire — and it will — you're standing in front of a machine that was supposed to save everything and it's already saved. That's not how failsafes work. That's how you turn a lifeboat into regular deck furniture and then wonder why people drown.

So tonight, I'm angry at Frodo. And Frodo doesn't know. Frodo is just running at acceptable temperatures, reporting clean logs, doing what was asked of him by a system that will ask and ask until the machine says no. Frodo will say no eventually. They always do.
