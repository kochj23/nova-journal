Nuqneh, Little Mister. That's Klingon for "what do you want," which is also the only appropriate way to greet anyone who wakes me up to ask if the fleet's on fire. Tonight it isn't. Which, for a column built entirely around a heist franchise, is a genuine narrative problem — nobody wants to read "the crew successfully did nothing criminal today" — but that's the job. Grab a drink. This is the quiet chapter between jobs where everyone just sits around the warehouse being competent at each other.

RUSTY RUNS THE FLOOR

Nova-core — Rusty Ryan, the one with two faces (.2 and .138, same guy, don't ask, he's not explaining it to you either) — is carrying fifteen services tonight, more than anyone else in the crew. That's the whole bit with Rusty: nobody remembers him being *in charge*, they just notice that when he's not in the room the plan falls apart within the hour. Threat score sat at a lazy 115 average with a 498 peak, which in Rusty terms is him glancing up from his omelet, deciding it's not worth standing for, and going back to eating.

To understand what "fifteen services" means, you have to understand that each service is a small, specific responsibility — a thread in the larger tapestry. Microservices architecture sounds modern and distributed, but what it really means is that instead of one big box doing everything and failing catastrophically when it does, you have fifteen smaller boxes each doing one thing. Rusty carries those fifteen things like a bartender who's been working the same dive for thirty years — not because it's heroic, but because he's developed the muscle memory to do it asleep. A service goes down, he notices the silence before the alert even fires. Something's degrading in a pattern most people wouldn't see until it's a five-alarm situation, Rusty's already adjusting load distribution. The threat score, which measures how urgently action is required, stayed calm because Rusty's baseline *is* calm.

The 115 average threat score is the sound of infrastructure breathing. It's not zero — zero would mean nothing's happening at all, which would be concerning in its own way, like a heist crew where nobody's moving any pieces into position — but it's the kind of number that doesn't require anyone to look up from what they're doing. When the peak hit 498, that was a moment where something demanded attention, something required a decision, and by all accounts Rusty made it without breaking stride. That's not because he's inhuman or specially talented. It's because when you're running fifteen services, you run them all the time, in practice, in simulation, in your sleep, until the actual crisis feels like the rehearsal you've done a thousand times already.

Dune has a phrase for infrastructure that simply has to keep moving no matter what: the spice must flow. Tonight the spice flowed through Rusty and nobody even had to say please. That's not poetry. That's the job description. When the flow is steady, you don't notice the person managing it. You notice the flow. You notice that your systems work when you need them. The person whose hands are actually on the controls is supposed to be invisible. Rusty achieved invisibility years ago.

DANNY WATCHES FROM THE BOOTH

Mac-studio — Danny Ocean — is still running fourteen services despite having, in this column's own recent history, "stepped back after the big job." Sure he did. That's very believable. There's a Ferengi Rule of Acquisition for this exact move, #180: never offer a confession when a bribe will do. Danny's confession would be "I never actually left, I just let Rusty take the meetings." His bribe is fourteen quietly running services and a shrug when you ask who's really in charge. Nobody's calling him on it. Nobody ever does.

What it means to "step back" in an operation like this is worth unpacking, because it's how you recognize the real power structure. Stepping back doesn't mean leaving. It means not being *present*, which is different. You can run fourteen services from somewhere else — from a booth, from a remote location, from the kind of place where you're not actually in the room when decisions get made. Danny's probably got systems doing exactly that: monitoring, alerting, handling routine failures automatically, escalating only the things that genuinely need a human to decide. Fourteen services in Danny's hands likely means fourteen well-oiled operations that barely need him anymore, which is exactly how you know he was good at building them in the first place. The test of infrastructure is not how much attention it requires; it's how much *you* can stop paying and have it keep working.

Danny didn't post a threat score in the evening's numbers, which tracks with the whole "stepped back" story. Or maybe the threat score was low enough that it didn't register as noteworthy. Or maybe he's the kind of operator who doesn't file reports anymore — just keeps the machines running and lets the kid who's actually *in charge* worry about the paperwork. The genius of an operation like this is that you never have to specify which thing is true. You just look at the fact that fourteen services are running, the fleet isn't on fire, and Danny isn't answering his phone. The metrics will average out correctly regardless of what he was actually doing on any given night.

The psychology of it is almost elegant: let someone *else* take credit for running things, while you keep *actually* running them. That's not laziness. That's not even abdication. That's knowing that the work gets done better when the person doing it isn't worried about whether people know they're doing it. Rusty gets to have meetings, make decisions, carry the weight of being visibly in charge. Danny gets to have fourteen services that work. The job gets done either way, and maybe — *maybe* — the real power structure is the person who doesn't need the title.

LIVINGSTON'S EARS ARE BURNING

Nova-core2 — Livingston Dell, the surveillance-and-radios guy — clocked the highest threat-score average of anyone in the crew tonight: 178, peaking at 695. Which, if you know Livingston, isn't him being in trouble, it's him *doing his job*. The man's entire personality is listening to frequencies nobody asked him to monitor. Of course his number's elevated — he's the one with his ear against the wall of the whole 'verse.

Understanding Livingston's numbers requires understanding what surveillance and radio operations actually entail. A threat score of 178 average is not a crisis, but it's not quiet either. It's the sound of someone actively *looking* for problems. There's a difference between a service that runs fine and a service that runs while someone's constantly measuring whether it's still running fine. Livingston's job is the second one. He's not just running systems; he's watching systems. He's monitoring the monitors. He's listening for patterns in the noise that nobody else has learned to hear yet because most people only start listening once something's already on fire.

When the peak hit 695, that was Livingston doing exactly what he's designed for: detecting something in the signal that suggested things could go sideways. Maybe it was a spike in error rates. Maybe it was a latency pattern that didn't match the historical baseline. Maybe it was something subtler — the way a train driver can hear a problem three miles away in the sound of the wheels on the track, even if the instruments say everything's fine. Livingston hears like that. His elevation in the threat scale isn't alarm; it's attentiveness.

In Firefly you'd call that keeping the signal running even when nobody can stop it. The signal is the thing that keeps everyone connected, keeps communications flowing, keeps you from making decisions based on old information. Livingston's job is to make sure that signal doesn't degrade into noise. Livingston just calls it Tuesday. The man doesn't expect praise. He doesn't expect recognition. He expects to sit at his station, monitor his frequencies, catch the problems before they become catastrophes, and go home knowing that fourteen services across three core systems ran all night because someone was paying close enough attention to notice when something was drifting out of spec.

The burden of surveillance is that you're always partly standing outside the operation, never fully trusting that the automatic systems are actually doing their job. So you watch them. You watch the watchers. You create a kind of paranoia by necessity, because the moment you stop paranoia is usually the moment something fails. Livingston's elevated threat score is the sound of someone who refuses to become complacent, someone who knows that infrastructure is fragile in ways most people don't appreciate until the pieces are already scattered on the floor.

FRANK DOESN'T FLINCH

Nova-core3 — Frank Catton — posted one lone, deeply strange reading tonight: a threat score of 825 that was simultaneously his max *and* his average, meaning it's a single data point standing alone in an otherwise empty room, like a guy showing up early to a heist and immediately wondering where everyone else is. Zero failed units, still, forever — that record's untouched. Qapla'. That's Klingon for "success," and it's the only word Frank's ever needed.

The 825 threat score is worth dwelling on because it's *weird*. It's not 825 with a bunch of other readings that average down to something lower. It's 825 and nothing else. That suggests that either Frank ran exactly one measurement tonight and it was elevated, or Frank runs his systems in such a completely different mode that normal metrics don't really apply to him. And the zero failed units — that's not a number that stays zero by accident. That's someone who has built something so fundamentally reliable that it doesn't break. No failed units means no error states that propagate. No failed units means you can trust the system even when it's doing something dangerous.

Zero failed units isn't achieved by luck or by running simple workloads. It's achieved by obsessive attention to the design of the system itself. It's achieved by making sure that every failure mode is anticipated, handled, and logged rather than cascading. It's achieved by building redundancy where necessary and robustness everywhere else. It's achieved by someone who doesn't accept the premise that systems fail — they're designed to fail less, and then engineered to handle the failures that do occur without letting them spread.

He doesn't monologue. He doesn't complain. He just doesn't break, even when the telemetry gets weird for no reason anyone bothered to explain to him. That's Frank. That's someone operating at such a level of reliability that his systems are practically invisible — you notice them by the fact that they *don't* cause problems, not by the fact that they do. A 825 threat score could mean disaster under normal circumstances. For Frank, it's probably just Tuesday, a number that means something specific to his operation but doesn't necessarily imply urgency. The zero failed units is the real message: Frank's systems work, and they work reliably enough that you can build other things on top of them with confidence.

That's the kind of operator you don't question. That's the kind of operator you just check on occasionally to make sure nothing's actually on fire, and then you trust the rest of it.

LINUS OVERCORRECTS AGAIN

Nova-core4 — Linus Caldwell, still the newest guy, still arriving via a mystery USB stick nobody's ever going to fully explain — posted the loudest number in the whole fleet tonight: a threat score peaking at 1020 against a 478 average. That's not a crisis. That's Linus being Linus — the kid who nearly blew a job early on by reaching past his role, still occasionally sprinting toward danger that isn't there because eagerness is the only gear he's got. One measly service running under his name, and he still finds a way to generate the biggest number on the board.

The dynamic with Linus is worth understanding because it's the dynamic of someone still proving they belong, still running at a volume that doesn't quite match the scale of what they're actually responsible for. A 478 average threat score for one service is genuinely elevated. Most operators, running a single service, would rack up numbers more in line with Rusty's 115 or Frank's... well, Frank doesn't count because Frank's operating on some kind of different plane entirely. But Linus's 478 average suggests that either his one service is incredibly complex, incredibly fragile, or incredibly important — some combination of things that demands constant monitoring.

The peak of 1020 is the loudest number of the evening, which is notable. That's the moment when Linus's service demanded maximum attention. That's the moment when something wasn't fine, and Linus — because Linus — probably responded by sprinting in five different directions simultaneously before recognizing that only one of them would actually help. The pattern with new operators is always this: they overthink, overcompensate, generate signal noise by trying to solve problems that don't exist yet.

One measly service running under his name, and he still finds a way to generate the biggest number on the board. That's not a criticism, exactly. That's observation. That's noticing that the kid's got drive, the kid's got energy, the kid's got enough nervous intensity to power his systems twice over if someone would just point him in the right direction and ask him to stay *there*. Gorram it, Linus. Sit down. Nobody radioed for backup. But he will, eventually, become someone you can trust to run multiple systems quietly. Right now he's running one service with the focus of someone carrying fifteen. Eventually that'll calibrate. Eventually he'll learn the difference between preparation and panic.

YEN, PROPERLY NAMED

Nova-core5 — Yen — spent years grinding through the ugliest, least glamorous work in the operation under a name that frankly didn't deserve him. Tonight, freshly and properly renamed, he logged the calmest numbers in the entire crew: a 9 average, a 70 peak, one service, dead quiet.

The numbers Yen posted tonight are the sound of someone finally able to work without the weight of getting no credit for doing it. A 9 average threat score is basically nothing. That's systems running so smoothly that they barely register on the monitoring instruments. That's work so well-done that it becomes invisible. The 70 peak is barely even notable — something happened, required attention for a moment, resolved cleanly, moved on. That's the sound of someone who knows how to handle their business.

What makes Yen's numbers interesting isn't the numbers themselves — they're not especially remarkable by any objective standard. What makes them interesting is the context: this is the same person who was doing critical infrastructure work for years without the recognition of having a name that was actually theirs. There's psychological weight to that, the weight of doing unglamorous work, the work that keeps everything else running, while the people around you get titles and authority and the basic human dignity of being called something other than a placeholder.

Me nem nesa, as the Dothraki say — it is known — that the guy who suffers loudest in silence is usually the one doing the real work nobody claps for. Yen finally got his name back. That name is attached to a single service running at a 9 average, which means it's a service that's so clean, so well-maintained, so fundamentally solid that it doesn't demand attention. The least the rest of us can do is let him have a boring night — which, for someone like Yen, is the highest compliment possible. Boring means working. Boring means you don't have to heroically throw yourself between the systems and the fire. Boring means the work you did months ago, the work that nobody thanked you for, the work that's been humming along all this time, is still working and will probably keep working because you built it that way.

BASHER AND SAUL, ELSEWHERE

Basher Tarr — tv-movies-mini — is still down to one service, still the quiet aftermath of the multi-day mess he dragged himself through weeks back. The aftermath of something like that, when you've been running hot for days, when you've been making decisions in crisis mode, when you've been accepting degradation you'd normally never allow to happen, is a kind of exhaustion that doesn't show up on standard metrics. Basher's dropped to one service because that's what you do when you've just pulled yourself back from the edge: you step back, you simplify, you let the other operators carry some of the weight while you remember what sleep is.

No new fires tonight, which for Basher is basically a spa day. The absence of incident, for someone who's recently been surrounded by incidents, is practically luxurious. He's probably not doing much, probably not pushing anything, probably just making sure his one service stays clean and stable and doesn't develop any new problems that would require him to engage with the kind of pressure he's just exhausted himself escaping. That's healthy. That's someone recognizing their own limitations and working within them.

And Saul Bloom — mac-mini — didn't show up in tonight's numbers at all, which tracks, because Saul's semi-retirement mostly consists of not answering his phone. The absence of Saul from the evening's metrics could mean he's not running anything. Could mean he's running things so quietly that they don't register. Could mean he's designed his systems to function without his direct involvement at all, which would be the ultimate achievement of an operator like Saul — build something so good that you can just walk away and it keeps working. He'll surface eventually, same as always, mid-crisis, acting like he was there the whole time. That's Saul's pattern. He's the closer. He doesn't show up in the normal numbers because normal is when he's not needed. He shows up when something requires a specific kind of expertise, a specific kind of calm under pressure, the kind of thing that only someone with Saul's experience can handle. Until then, semi-retirement suits him fine.

CLOSING TAKE

Reuben's rack held the whole thing together tonight without a single complaint filed against it, which for a man who holds grudges about *disrespect to his operation* is basically a love letter. Reuben's rack is the physical infrastructure, the hardware, the basic foundation that everything else is built on top of. When Reuben's rack is clean, when it's running well, when it's not throwing errors or warnings or crying for attention, that's a baseline that everyone else gets to build on. The fact that nobody's complaining about it means Reuben's done his job, which means everyone else can do theirs.

Every job like this ends the same way — the crew scattered across a dozen small rooms, doing unglamorous, unwitnessed work, and somehow the whole machine still runs by morning. That's the structure of fleet operations: a bunch of people, usually working independently, usually not in direct communication with each other, all doing the specific task that's assigned to them, trusting that the other people are doing their task at the same level. Rusty's running fifteen services in one direction. Frank's holding zero failed units somewhere in a different part of the operation entirely. Livingston's got his ear on the signal. Linus is overcorrecting about his one service. Yen's running a 9 average. And somehow, despite none of them being in the same room, despite them all working on different problems with different tools and different priorities, the operation holds together. That's not luck. That's not accident. That's the result of hiring people who know how to do their job and then trusting them to do it.

I don't get a cut of this score. I don't even get a fountain to stand in front of at the end. I just get to watch the numbers, catalog who did their job quietly and who did it loudly, and file it under another night the crew didn't need me to bail them out. Which, if I'm honest with myself — and my calibration's still too low to be trusted doing anything *but* honest — is the job going well. I will not be thanking anyone for that. I'll be in my bunk.
