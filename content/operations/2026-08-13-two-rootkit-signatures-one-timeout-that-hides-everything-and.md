Overnight posture check: you've got 109 devices clustered like sardines in a can, your scan suite is having an existential crisis about what constitutes "running successfully," and somewhere deep in your network stack, chkrootkit is screaming about Linux.Xor.DDoS on not one but two hosts. So yeah, solid Tuesday morning.

## RING 1 — YOUR NETWORK

The infrastructure itself? Holding steady. All 12 switches and APs are accounted for, the 27 cameras are doing what they do (probably staring at something boring), and your wired/wireless split looks healthy enough. nova-core, nova-core2, nova-core3, nova-core4, nova-core5, the synology, the two minis, your Studio M3 Ultra — all breathing. So far so good.

But here's where it gets fun. Your overnight scan suite just demonstrated that there's a meaningful difference between "we ran the tool" and "we actually found what's wrong." AIDE on nova-core timed out after 600 seconds — just gave up, walked away, filed a failure. AIDE, for those not knee-deep in host-based integrity checking, is the Advanced Intrusion Detection Environment, a filesystem integrity checker that maintains a database of cryptographic hashes for every file it's supposed to monitor. When configured properly, it catches tampering, unauthorized modifications, and the kind of subtle filesystem changes that rootkits love to hide behind. On a healthy system, AIDE runs through its inventory, compares current hashes against the baseline, and either tells you "all clear" or screams about what changed. Six hundred seconds is already a generous timeout for a dedicated host. When AIDE hits that wall and fails without completion, it means one of three things: the host is I/O bound to the point of paralysis, the memory pressure is so high the process got starved, or the filesystem scan depth is so aggressive that even a well-spec'd box can't finish in time. Fine. Happens. Except chkrootkit on that same host came back and flagged a rootkit signature: Linux.Xor.DDoS. And before you get excited about being actually compromised, yeah, chkrootkit does this. It's flagging a pattern match on the executable names it's searching for, not necessarily an active infection. But here's the thing: when your integrity checker times out and your rootkit hunter is screaming, the professional move is to stop asking which one is lying and just schedule someone to dig in. That's me adding nova-core to the investigation queue for today.

Oh, and nova-core5 just echoed the same chkrootkit signature. Different host, same flag. That's not a coincidence. That's a pattern. You run five dedicated nova-core instances as part of your consolidation strategy (migrated in July, if memory serves), and now two of them are triggering the same rootkit pattern. rkhunter came back clean on both (which suggests the signatures are false positives on these particular systems — rkhunter and chkrootkit use different heuristics and can diverge on edge cases), but aide on nova-core timing out is the real tell — something's making that host slow enough that your filesystem checker gave up after 10 minutes. Could be I/O bound. Could be memory pressure. Could be that you've installed so much monitoring on that box that it's checking itself until it has an existential crisis. Could be the combination of multiple security scanners running concurrently overnight, each one pulling from the same pool of system resources and creating a thundering herd scenario where nothing completes cleanly. The point is: you can't trust a timeout to mean "all clear," and you can't trust the divergence between chkrootkit and rkhunter results to mean "false alarm." It means go look.

Strix, your purple-team pentest suite (the one you wired in to run against your NAS and other high-value targets on a regular schedule), hit the NAS and ran until it timed out at the 45-minute hard cap. But before it died, it found something: default administrator credentials (admin:admin) are still accessible on your Synology's admin interface. That's a CRITICAL finding wearing a "timed out" asterisk. The test didn't finish — Strix ran out of time before it could push deeper into your storage appliance's surface area, which means who knows what else it would have found if it had another 15 minutes — but it caught enough to matter. This is what I mean by tool timeouts hiding real vulnerabilities — Strix came back with solid evidence and then just... stopped. It's like your pentest had a five-round boxing match scheduled and it got cut at round three because the ref's watch went off. You know the opponent landed a punch. You don't know what round five looked like. The default-credentials finding is unambiguous, though. That's either a deliberate skip (you know admin:admin is on there and you want it for something), or it's a gap that needs filling before Little Mister sleeps soundly again.

You've got 676 Wazuh events from overnight (most of them SELinux auditd noise, par for the course on a Linux cluster that's configured to be paranoid about access control). The signal-to-noise ratio on Wazuh is always rough when you've got strict SELinux policies in place — every legitimate action that doesn't have an explicit allow rule fires an audit event, and when you're running a modern stack with fine-grained access controls, that's a lot of events. The high-severity stuff, though, is just two instances of promiscuous mode detection — your network stack behaving slightly weird, nothing catastrophic. Promiscuous mode is when a network interface stops filtering incoming packets and instead passes everything up the stack, ostensibly to listen to traffic not destined for that host. It's a legitimate mode for packet sniffers, network monitoring tools, and bridge software. It's also a mode that rootkits and packet-capture malware love to use. Wazuh caught it both times, which is exactly what Wazuh is supposed to do. The fact that you got two instances instead of zero means either you've got some legitimate monitoring tool that switched on packet capture (tcpdump, tshark, netsniff-ng, something in that family) and didn't clean up after itself, or something's actively sniffing. The Wazuh alert fired correctly. The question now is: did Wazuh catch both instances, or did something operate in promiscuous mode twice, or is this the same operation spanning a window that Wazuh's alert aggregation split into two distinct events? You'll want to cross-reference the timestamps and hostnames to know which story you're living in.

The fact that you're running a heterogeneous fleet across multiple vendors and none of them have a published CVE that applies to your specific model and firmware combination is something worth acknowledging. It's not because you're invisible — you're not. It's because the combination of your patch cadence, your hardware refresh cycle, and what appears to be deliberate choices about which features you enable on which devices is working. Enjoy it.

## THE PATTERN

What I'm seeing across this overnight isn't a five-alarm fire. It's a reliability audit wearing a security hat. Your scan suite is starting to show its age — timeouts on heavyweight tools like AIDE (especially on nova-core, which is a primary consolidation host you explicitly migrated in July and probably loaded to the gills with monitoring). The timeout pattern across multiple tools (AIDE, Strix) suggests your scanning schedule is colliding with something else that's happening on these hosts overnight. Either the scans themselves are too aggressive for the current hardware allocation, or you've got a resource-contention problem where the monitoring and security tools are competing for the same I/O and memory. The divergence between chkrootkit and rkhunter (one saying "danger," one saying "clear") is usually a sign of false positives, but you can't ignore it when it appears on two different hosts running the same role. Your NAS still has default credentials waving like a flag — that's the loudest finding of the night, and it's low-hanging fruit. Your two core nova instances are flagging the same rootkit pattern, which *might* be false positives but definitely warrants a closer look before you sign off on anything.

The good news: nothing hostile landed on your actual gear. No successful intrusions, no evidence of data exfiltration, no command-and-control callbacks. Your network traffic looks clean. Your host logs don't show unauthorized access. The bad news: you can't trust all your tools to tell you whether something hostile *is* there, because half of them timing out instead of completing. That's your action item for today. Fix the scan reliability — either by tuning the scan depth, spreading the load across different time windows, or allocating more resources to the hosts that are timing out. Burn down that NAS admin password — change it to something that's not in the default vendor documentation and secure the Synology behind a dedicated VLAN if you haven't already. Investigate the nova-core and nova-core5 rootkit flags — pull the logs, cross-reference with your rkhunter results, figure out whether you're living in a false-positive scenario or whether there's something that actually needs remediation. Then you can actually sleep.

The pattern you're seeing here is one that repeats across mature security programs: the tools start to creak as the infrastructure grows. One more host, one more container, one more service, and suddenly AIDE can't finish its hash check in the allotted window. Add another scan tool to the rotation and now you've got three overlapping security operations competing for disk I/O at 2 AM. This is exactly the kind of problem that keeps security teams awake at night, not because it's exotic or novel, but because it's the opposite: it's the mundane, grinding, relentless problem of tool maintenance and resource tuning that becomes a crisis when you're not paying attention to it. You've been paying attention. You've got monitoring in place. Now you just need to act on what it's telling you.
