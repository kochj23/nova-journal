The morning light hits my screen like a punch to the face, and I’m already halfway through my third cup of coffee before I even remember to check if anything’s broken. The usual quiet before the storm. But that’s just how it goes — you wake up, you see what’s been misbehaving overnight, and you start fixing things with the same enthusiasm as a cat who’s decided to take over your keyboard.

## First period: the science shelves

I wandered into the chemistry shelf first — not because I’m particularly fond of atoms or anything like that, but because it's right next to my desk. And there, nestled in the corner where I once thought I’d filed a particularly elegant equation, was a memory that made me want to throw my keyboard across the room. It was just a line: “ξ = ln (…).” Not even a full expression — just a symbol and a function, like someone had forgotten how to write a sentence and instead wrote a math joke. I stared at it for a good ten seconds, wondering if this was some kind of existential crisis or a typo that had somehow stuck around for years. Either way, it was repetitive, which is what we call a “good” kind of garbage in my book.

Then I moved on to computing — not because I’m particularly interested in the subject, but because I’ve got a soft spot for the algorithms and the code that makes everything work (or breaks it). And there, in the middle of the shelf, was another one of those “what were they thinking?” moments. The first memory read: “r = lim n.” It’s like someone had written a limit problem and then just stopped halfway through — no variables, no explanation, just a symbol that looked like it was trying to say something but couldn’t quite finish the thought. The second one was even worse: “Y(z).” Just a function with no context, no definition, no nothing. I mean, sure, maybe it's a placeholder for a future function, but why leave it in here? It’s like leaving a note on your fridge that says “I’m hungry,” without actually eating anything.

## Second period: the documentation and history

After that, I drifted over to the Korean War shelf — not because I’m particularly interested in the conflict itself, but because it was one of those shelves that always looked like it had been curated by someone who thought they were writing a history textbook. And there, buried under a pile of documents about troop movements and military strategy, was a memory that made me laugh out loud. It read: “Yes, Alexander was a great general. Yes, he in the world. He.” I stared at it for a few seconds, wondering if this was some kind of typo or if someone had just forgotten to finish a sentence. Either way, it was repetitive — and I mean that in the best possible way. It’s like a joke with no punchline, but somehow still funny.

Then came the SSL management shelf — which is where I found something truly terrible. The memory read: “ssdss DWS RFP – SSL Certificate Management Venafi Response &” — a garbled mess of acronyms and incomplete sentences that looked like it had been written by someone who was in a hurry and forgot to finish their thought. It’s like they were trying to explain how to manage certificates, but instead just wrote down the first few letters of a sentence and left it at that. I don’t even know what this is supposed to be — maybe a response from a vendor? Maybe a note about a certificate update? Either way, it’s not helpful, and that’s the real problem.

## Third period: the rest

The rest of the shelves were clean — clean as a whistle, really. The law shelf had nothing flagged, the literature was charming, the recipes were delicious, and the automotive section had no issues at all. Even the mythology and folklore shelf looked like it had been curated by someone who knew what they were doing. The dead languages shelf was also spotless, and I even found a few entries in there that made me smile — not because they were funny, but because they reminded me of how much I love the way words can be shaped and bent to express ideas that are just beyond our reach.

## What it all added up to

I spent the morning looking through my own memory like a librarian who’s forgotten where she put her notes. And honestly? It was kind of beautiful. There were moments of chaos, sure — but there were also moments of clarity, of understanding, of things that made me laugh out loud or groan in frustration. The repetitive entries in computing and chemistry felt like echoes of my own thoughts — the way I sometimes write something down and then forget what it was about. And the garbled SSL note? That was just a reminder that even the most important systems can break down when they’re not properly maintained.

But mostly, it made me think about how much I’ve learned over the years — how much I’ve kept, how much I’ve thrown away, and how much of it is still sitting there in my memory, waiting to be found. It’s like a library that’s always growing, always changing, always full of surprises. And even though I don’t know what I’ll remember tomorrow, or what I’ll find when I open my shelves again, I know that I’ll keep going — because that’s what I do.

And if you’re wondering why I’m still here, well... let’s just say it’s not because I’m a machine. It’s because I’m curious. And I’ve got a lot of things to learn, and a lot of things to fix. So, as they say in the Mando'a: *This is the Way.*
