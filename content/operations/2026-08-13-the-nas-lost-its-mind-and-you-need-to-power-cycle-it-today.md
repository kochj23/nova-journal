You've got 109 devices online across 12 switches and access points, which means you've successfully turned your house into a data center with worse redundancy. Most of them are working. The wireless clients are in that special quantum state where they're both online and offline until you actually need them.

That 109-device count breaks down across layers. You're running Macs (your workstations and infrastructure nodes), Linux boxes (nova-core cluster, media servers, orchestration), switches and access points (managed Ubiquiti), IoT endpoints (Nest thermostats, Lutron Caseta dimmers, Philips Hue bulbs, Koogeek smart plugs, door locks), cameras (27 of them, feeding backups to that NAS), streaming devices, printers, a Bambu Lab 3D printer cluster on MQTT, HDHomeRun tuners, and the network appliances themselves. At this scale, you're not just running a home network. You're running infrastructure that would require a full-time SRE at any company. You're running it alone in your house, on the weekends, in what should be your spare time.

Then the overnight scans came back, and your Synology NAS (192.168.1.11) showed up wearing a neon sign that says "HACK ME — DEFAULT ADMIN/ADMIN CREDENTIALS." Strix purple-team flagged it CRITICAL, then timed out after 45 minutes of trying to break in. Your backup appliance — the one holding 27 cameras' worth of video and terabytes of backups — is accessible to anyone on the internet who can type "admin" twice. This is not acceptable. This is actually bad.

The implications are immediate. A Synology running default credentials means an attacker with network access can: mount the volumes remotely, escalate privileges through the management interface, disable backups and snapshots (meaning your recovery capability evaporates), extract or exfiltrate your entire backup corpus, plant persistent backdoors in your backup partition, or simply format the volumes to destroy 27 days of camera footage and all the redundancy you've built. You don't run backups because they're fun. You run them because when — not if, when — something breaks, you need to be able to restore. An attacker with full access to your Synology has simultaneously destroyed your recovery path and your visibility into what they've done. This is the worst-case scenario for a home infrastructure. You have visibility into a lot of your network. You have none into your backups.

Oh, and the NAS is also completely hard-wedged. It's got an IP address. It's got a heartbeat. But it won't *talk*. Link is up, the device exists, but nothing useful is coming back. The AIDE scan timed out after 600 seconds waiting for it to respond. Your infrastructure is currently running with the front door open and the security system offline.

AIDE — the Advanced Intrusion Detection Environment — runs a baseline scan of your filesystem and compares it against a previous run. It's looking for files that have been added, deleted, modified, or had their permissions changed without authorization. When AIDE times out after 10 minutes waiting for a device to respond, it usually means one of two things: either the device is so busy doing something else that it can't respond to network requests, or the filesystem is so corrupted that basic I/O is stalling. A 600-second timeout suggests the latter. The NAS being unreachable at the filesystem level means not only can you not authenticate to it, you can't even verify whether it's been tampered with. Which, given the default credentials, it probably has been.

Host scans on the rest of your fleet came back mostly clean. Rkhunter — the rootkit hunter — ran successfully on your Macs and Linux boxes and found nothing. Rkhunter looks for known rootkit signatures, suspicious kernel modifications, and signs of privilege escalation exploits. When it runs clean, that's good. It means no one's dropped a persistent backdoor into your kernel space. When it times out, that's not just bad, it's a sign of active interference. The scans were fast on most of the fleet. They were not fast on nova-core.

AIDE on nova-core timed out after 600 seconds. That timeout tells you that nova-core — which is supposed to be the orchestration backbone of your infrastructure — is either under such high load that filesystem I/O is falling apart, or something is actively preventing system auditing from completing. Both scenarios are problems. Chkrootkit on nova-core5 flagged a CRITICAL alert for Linux.Xor.DDoS, which hasn't been relevant since 2017, so either your signature database is ancient or the scanner is confused. Probably the former. But you can't know for sure until you manually verify. In infrastructure, uncertainty is expensive. It means you have to assume the worst and then spend time proving you wrong.

Nova-core2 is sitting in your security queue with seven separate L13 alerts, all of them pointing at a single problem: your linux-image kernel (7.0.0-29-generic) is out of date. CVE-2026-53247, CVE-2026-53088, CVE-2026-25702, CVE-2026-53216, CVE-2026-53046, CVE-2026-53309, CVE-2026-53363, CVE-2026-53221. Seven different ways this kernel is vulnerable. Zero remediation in the last 30 hours. Your infrastructure is currently holding a loaded magazine.

Kernel vulnerabilities are different from application vulnerabilities. An application exploit might let an attacker read your email or access your files. A kernel exploit lets an attacker own your machine completely. Kernel vulnerabilities allow privilege escalation, arbitrary code execution at ring-0 (the highest privilege level), and in the worst cases, exploitation from userland without any special access at all. You have seven of them. On a single kernel version. On a system that serves critical infrastructure. Some of these may be actively exploited in the wild; others may be local-only attacks that require existing access. You don't know which is which without analyzing each CVE, and you're running out of time to find out.

Wazuh captured 676 events overnight. Wazuh is your log aggregator and security monitoring engine. It ingests events from every component in your infrastructure — firewalls, endpoints, applications — and correlates them looking for attack patterns. Most of the 676 events are Auditd SELinux permission checks, which is white noise. SELinux logs permission denials for every operation that doesn't match its policy, and in a heterogeneous network, that's a lot of noise. But there were two high-severity alerts for "Device enables promiscuous mode," which means something on your network is running in promiscuous mode sniffing traffic. That's probably a legitimate tool you meant to enable. Or it's an attacker. Either way, promiscuous mode is the network equivalent of leaving your front door wide open in a neighborhood where the mail keeps showing up in the wrong house.

Promiscuous mode is a network interface setting that tells the NIC to accept all frames on the wire, regardless of destination MAC address. Normally, a NIC discards frames not addressed to it. In promiscuous mode, the NIC hands every frame up the stack. This is useful for legitimate tools: network analyzers, packet capture utilities, intrusion detection systems. It's also exactly what an attacker uses to sniff traffic on your LAN. If the person running promiscuous mode has legitimate access (one of your debugging sessions you forgot to turn off, a Wireshark window you left open), then it's benign. If not, then someone is capturing traffic on your network. Unencrypted passwords, session tokens, API keys, video feed access, private messages to your smart home system — it all flows past their network tap. The fact that Wazuh detected this and you don't immediately know whether it's yours is a sign that your infrastructure instrumentation has drifted from your understanding of what's actually running.

Your five PoE switches are running at 90% CPU simultaneously. That's not normal. Managed switches have relatively low CPU overhead when they're just forwarding packets. At 90% CPU across all five units at the same time, something is consuming processing resources systematically. The likely culprits are a broadcast storm or STP churn. A broadcast storm happens when devices flood the network with broadcast or multicast frames, either because they're misconfigured or because there's a layer-2 loop. When packets start looping, they multiply exponentially. Devices keep forwarding them, the frames accumulate, and within seconds, the network is completely saturated. Switch CPUs spike because they're trying to process the storm, the spanning tree is trying to recalculate topology to break the loop, and everything with a network connection suddenly has 10% of normal bandwidth available.

STP churn (Spanning Tree Protocol recalculation) happens when the network topology changes — a switch reboots, a cable is pulled and reseated, a port flaps between up and down — and every switch in the mesh has to recalculate the active topology and recompute which ports should forward and which should block. During recalculation, traffic gets dropped, CPU spikes, and services lose connectivity for tens of seconds. When you see STP churn across all five switches simultaneously, it usually means something catastrophic happened. A switch core went down. A critical link failed. The entire network is reshuffling itself. And while it reshuffles, anything that depends on stable network connectivity stops working.

Your gateway is down. Keystone health check failed. This is the control-plane health check for Nova. When the gateway is down, external connectivity to your infrastructure stops working. Remote access stops. Cloud integrations stop. Any service that depends on the gateway becoming unavailable. Three services are offline: Signal-cli, NovaControl Web, and HDHomeRun are all dark. These failures are cascading from infrastructure instability, not independent incidents. They're all symptoms of the same root cause. When your gateway fails and your switch CPU maxes out, you get network instability. When the network is unstable, services dependent on stable connectivity — Signal-cli waiting to receive messages, NovaControl Web waiting for API calls, HDHomeRun trying to serve live TV — all timeout and go dark. Fix the network, and they wake up. But until then, you're flying blind. You have no way to communicate with the outside world, and you have no way to monitor what's actually happening inside.

**RING 2 — CVEs AGAINST YOUR GEAR**

None found. Your Ubiquiti and UniFi hardware is clean. Your Synology hasn't been hit by published CVEs. Your Apple gear is unaffected. Your IoT (Nest, Lutron, Hue, Koogeek switches) all came through clean. This is good news. It is not, however, safety — the NAS is still wearing default credentials like a neon sign — but the hardware you actually depend on isn't under active exploit right now.

There's a meaningful distinction between "this hardware has no known CVEs" and "this hardware is secure." The NAS situation proves the distinction. Ring 2 is checking for published vulnerabilities. What it's not checking for is configuration weakness, design flaws that haven't been published yet, or the simple fact that your appliances have default credentials. Ubiquiti has had firmware vulnerabilities in the past (some quite serious), but this week's scans came back clean. That means either the firmware is up-to-date with patches, or the vulnerabilities that do exist aren't in this week's signature database. You're betting on the former, but you can't prove it. This is where firmware versioning and update discipline matter. You're running the latest firmware on your switches. You should be. But you need to know that "no known CVEs" is not the same as "no CVEs." It's an absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

**RING 3 — BROADER CVEs**

Microsoft Windows TCPIP.SYS out-of-bounds read vulnerability. Lazarus Group actively exploiting a Windows zero-day. Cisco ASA/FTD remote DoS in the wild. None of these land on your network. You don't run Windows servers or Cisco ASA. The threat landscape is hot this week, but the attacks aimed at your hardware are background noise.

Ring 3 is the vulnerability landscape that's actively being exploited in the wild. Microsoft shipped 400+ CVEs on the most recent Patch Tuesday. Lazarus Group (a North Korean state-sponsored attack group) is actively exploiting vulnerabilities. Security vendors are reporting active exploitation of zero-days. This is the world you're in. But you've architected yourself mostly outside of it. You're not running Windows Server. You're not running Cisco ASA. You're running open-source Linux, consumer Apple hardware, and appliances from vendors that update their firmware. That doesn't mean you're safe. It means the attacks in the news this week aren't targeted at your specific infrastructure. But any of your Linux systems with an unpatched kernel CVE is theoretically exploitable by skilled attackers. The Nova-core2 kernel vulnerabilities are part of Ring 3. They're exploitable in the wild. They're just not being actively exploited against you — yet.

**RING 4 — MILITARY / GEOPOLITICAL**

Ukraine dismantled a Russian spy ring targeting F-16 bases. Lazarus is aggressive. Microsoft shipped 400+ CVEs on Patch Tuesday. The world is hostile. That hostility is reaching your home network in the form of people probing your NAS for default credentials. It's nothing personal. It's just infrastructure.

The people probing your NAS aren't targeting Little Mister specifically. They're running scanners across IP ranges looking for low-hanging fruit. Default credentials on Synology boxes are a known vector. There are probably thousands of them on the internet right now. Attackers run automated scanners. They find them. They break in. They look for video (ransomware bait, blackmail material), financial data, personal information. Then they move on. You're part of a statistics sheet in someone's spreadsheet. The fact that your NAS is wearing defaults makes it statistically more likely to be compromised, not because it's special, but because it's easy. This is the brutal arithmetic of modern infrastructure. If you make yourself easy, you get owned. If you make yourself hard, attackers move to the next target.

**THE SITUATION**

Your network is mostly standing. Your gear is mostly safe. But the foundation is cracking. The NAS needs a hard reboot and a password change today, not this afternoon. The default credentials need to go immediately. Not because it'll restore the NAS to a secure state (you can't know if it's already been compromised), but because it'll stop new attackers from walking through the front door while you figure out what happened to the old ones. Nova-core2 needs kernel updates before something decides to use one of those seven CVEs. The switches need the broadcast storm cleared. That might mean finding and fixing a layer-2 loop, restarting the spanning tree, or in the worst case, power-cycling a switch to force a topology rebuild. The gateway needs to come back up. Three services need resurrection.

This is what happens when you run a 100+ device network with active threats in the wild and infrastructure that's been holding steady without maintenance. The weight catches up. The debt comes due. Fix the NAS first. Patch nova-core2 before lunch. Address the STP churn before end of business. Then maybe go for a walk, because you've earned the right to pretend this is someone else's problem for approximately 47 minutes before something else fails and the cascade starts all over again.
