## The Scholars and the Texts

### Dan McClellan on Colossians 1:15

The most technical item today is Dan McClellan's video "I was wrong about Jesus being a created being?" The title promises a confession, but the excerpt delivers a fight over grammar. The question is how to read "firstborn of all creation" in Colossians 1:15. McClellan says the common claim that Jesus "can't be a part of all creation" violates what he calls discourse pragmatics. His evidence is that New Testament writers, and Septuagint writers, often pick out a subgroup of a larger group and then talk about the whole group without spelling out that the subgroup is included. He points to 1 Corinthians 15, where Paul explicitly excludes the entity he initially distinguished, as proof that when Paul wants an exclusion he says so.

He also complains that people who read the verse as separating Jesus from creation fall back on verses 16 and 17 as a "better reading," without dealing with how *prototokos*, "firstborn," behaves everywhere else it appears. He knows he's arguing against the consensus and says he's writing a paper for peer review. His careful wording matters: he calls the consensus reading "implausible," not "impossible."

I can't tell from the excerpt what exactly he was "wrong" about, so I won't pretend to. It treats the verse as a Greek sentence from the first century that has to behave like other first-century Greek sentences. Both are legitimate ways of reading, and only one of them gets to use the phrase "discourse pragmatics" at a dinner party.

### Chabad on Laban

Chabad.org's short video "Who Was Laban in the Bible? Jacob's Trickster Father-in-Law" takes a very different tack. It draws on the Hebrew name Lavan, "the Aramean," whose Hebrew letters can be rearranged to spell *ramai*, "trickster." That's a pun built into the spelling, which means the rabbinic tradition was telling dad jokes before I was. The video walks the standard beats: Lavan rushing out to Abraham's servant because of the gifts given to Rebekah, the Leah-for-Rachel switch on the wedding night, Jacob's seven more years, and, per Rashi, wages changed a hundred times. Then comes the dream warning, the chase, and the peace sealed with a heap of stones.

The moral is a contrast. Esau's evil was out in the open, but Lavan "hid his schemes behind a righteous face, and hidden evil takes vigilance and perseverance to defeat." In Mandarin crew-speak, that's a *tā mā de húndàn*, a f***ing scoundrel, but a polite one with a nice smile. Exactly the kind that leaves you checking the contract twice.

### Hosea: God Is Not Fragile

On the FAIR channel, Jennifer Roach Lees covers Hosea 1 to 6, 10 to 14, and Joel for the "Come, Follow Me" series. The excerpt picks up with the book's famously violent imagery and its turn at the end. She cites Hosea 13:14, "I will ransom them from their power of the grave. I will redeem them from death," glossing it as "death is going to die." She then cites the close of chapter 14: "I will heal their backsliding. I will love them freely. I will be as the dew unto Israel. He shall grow as the lily."

Her theological point is aimed at a believer's problem. She argues that if a theology allows only "a polite and a delicate God who only can be spoken of in gentle whispers," that God will feel irrelevant when life hits hard, and that Hosea shows God's love is "intense enough to enter into our darkest, ugliest realities." Justice and mercy, she says, are "two hands of the exact same loving father." That's one believer's reading, delivered to believers. A historian would add that Hosea's violent imagery and his tenderness have been argued over for centuries, but today's source is a devotional one and it's offering comfort.

### The Coptic Answer on Zeal

Coptic Orthodox Answers, a priest-and-host response video titled "He Told Kids to Smash Mary's Statue… Then Said a Priest Went to Hell. We Respond," is a rebuttal to a preacher. The excerpt isn't the original claim, so I only have the response. It relies on a Gospel episode in which the disciples report that they stopped people casting out demons in Jesus' name because "they do not follow you as we do." The priest's reading is that Jesus says no one using his name is ultimately against him, so "do not prevent them," and that Jesus is "not interested" in a zeal of "you got to be like me." He then cites the episode of the disciples asking about calling down fire on a Samaritan village.

He closes with Matthew 12, on giving account for "every idle word men may speak," and 1 Corinthians 16:14, "Let all that you do be done with love." There's also a line about how someone who has himself as spiritual father "has a fool for a spiritual father." That's a Coptic Orthodox argument for accountability to a church, aimed at an independent preacher, and it should be read that way.

## Who Do You Become? Formation and Institutions

Yaqeen Institute's Snapshots with Imam Tom Facchine asks a question that applies to every group in this issue: "What kind of person does this place produce after five years?" His argument is that institutions are "never just platforms," but "systems of formation." The university teaches "an entire theory of knowledge," media coaches you on how to feel, and the person who plans to "change the place from within" almost never does, because "they were kidding themselves from the beginning." It's a Muslim speaker addressing a general audience, but it works as a lens on everything else here.

The Jewish Theological Seminary's open house for the William Davidson Graduate School shows the lens in action. Faculty describe the EdD program as aimed at "however you want to serve the Jewish people," built around "growth and transformation," and meant to build "dispositions" in the educators and leaders "the world desperately, desperately needs." It's a recruiting event, so read it as one: lots of warm words and a promise to put an email address in the chat. Rule of Acquisition #21 says "Never place friendship before profit," and the Ferengi would be thrilled at an institution that follows up so promptly. In fairness, the faculty also say their cohort is drawn from the field, which is a real feature of a graduate program.

The Assyrian Church of the East's Episode 173, "Excessive Self Confidence," tackles a related problem from the other side: the individual who thinks formation is finished. The speaker's reading of Paul is that "the Christian life... requires everyday continuous growth," and that you "cannot claim that you've grown and that's it." He pairs this with the narrow gate and the parable of the virgins, where "he will close the door," and warns that cultural identification as a Christian isn't the same as being through the gate.

## Dharmic Voices: Dharma, Ghosts, Nuns, and Sawaki

### Vedanta: The Platform and the Dancer

At the Vedanta Society of St. Louis, Swami Nishpapananda, in a talk posted by the Vedanta Society of New York, describes dharma as the platform for the walk toward moksha, liberation. Dharma, he says, makes people happy because it's "the way the highest truth manifests on the relative plane." Ordinary life's best offerings aren't enough, and he quotes Swamiji's line that we are "infinite dreamers dreaming finite dreams." Ramakrishna's expert dancer "doesn't take a false step" because someone who has realized God is already in dharma's rhythm. He then turns to Shankaracharya's commentary on the Isha Upanishad's first verse, in which the whole world of experience is "to be covered by God" and one is told to protect this knowledge by renunciation. The speaker's gloss on "do not covet" is the closest the talk gets to a punch line, and I'll leave it as the Swami phrased it.

### HinduAcademy on Ghosts

HinduAcademy's video "What is the difference between an enlightened soul and a ghost?" answers a question about *bhoot*, ghosts, with practical advice rather than metaphysics. The speaker says "this world is very weird and wonderful" and that there are "lots of unknowns," but that dwelling on ghosts day and night is asking for trouble. He compares it to thinking about Ramana Maharshi day and night, hoping he turns up. Dwell on "things that will lift you, not things that will break you down" and "become a giant, spiritual giant, not a little tinkly little ghost." I'd call that an ethic of attention, and an unusually sensible one.

### Buddhist Nuns in Myanmar

The Harvard Divinity School talk "Buddhist Nuns in Myanmar: Voicing Care and Belonging," sponsored by the Women's Studies in Religion Program, is mostly an excerpt of its closing minutes. It recounts a story from the Dhammapada commentary. A woman is listening to her son preach the Dhamma while thieves rob her house. A servant three times begs her to come home, and three times she sends the servant back, "unwilling to be distracted from her chance to hear her son preach." The leader of the thieves throws himself at her feet, returns everything, and he and his gang ask to ordain with her son as preceptor. The Buddha sends "a radiant image of himself" to preach to them, and the verses attached to the story are spoken.

The speaker draws out a link I find hard to improve on: the new monks are exhorted to meditate, "perhaps" with the same attention "a mother would pay her child." Her attention returned everything that had been taken.

### Kodo Sawaki

On Hardcore Zen, "Who Was Kodo Sawaki?" is a short primer on the twentieth-century Soto Zen teacher. The host mentions that Brian Victoria's attitude changed after reading what Muho presented, and that in the follow-up to *Zen at War*, Sawaki isn't mentioned at all. For readers, the host recommends *To You*, which Muho put out, as the quick version, and *Discovering the True Self* and *The Zen Teachings of Homeless Kodo* for a fuller biography. He closes by saying he's about to start chemotherapy and that his channel is how he makes a living. I'm not making a joke here, and I wish him a good course of treatment.

## A Remembrance

Frieda Vizel's video "Remembering the horrors at Bondi Beach, one year later" carries a witness account that deserves care. A relative describes rushing to get family out of the area during the attack, and her daughter's friend Chaya hiding under a table while a mother tried to shield her children with her own body. As told, the mother was shot and called out for help, Chaya got out from under the table and lay on top of her children, and "people were just lying there screaming, Shema." The Shema is the central Jewish declaration of faith, and the account has people saying it in the middle of the attack. The woman who lay over the children "was shot in the leg" and survived. The speaker says she told her daughter afterward that she knew she could have been shot, but felt it was her mission. That's one family's testimony, a year on, and it's reported here as they gave it.

## Prayer, Beauty, and Christmas Early

Ascension Presents' "How to Be Honest with God In Prayer" asks believers to bring their real feelings, including "fear, anxiety, and dread," "anger, confusion, darkness," even "imperfection and sin," to God as they are, because "that's the places that God actually wants to meet us." The Catholic theology underneath is the Incarnation: God descending "into the earthiness of our very souls."

Godsplaining's "Is Zero A.D. Worth Watching? A Catholic Priest Reacts" has a priest praising a film's Nativity scene, including a moment where Mary tells someone at the door, "I will not be alone." He cried while taking notes, and he argues that beauty "is not just a vehicle for the gospel, it's a testimony to the gospel's efficacy." The priest's own recommendation, and the Catholic framing, are all that I can report. I haven't seen the film and neither, apparently, has any critic whose opinion I could cross-check.

The Christian Science channel's song "Listen for the Angels" retells the Nativity through the angels who appear to Mary, Joseph, and the shepherds, with the refrain that listening to angels is "the gift of love." It's a children's-style hymn, and it's pleasant.
