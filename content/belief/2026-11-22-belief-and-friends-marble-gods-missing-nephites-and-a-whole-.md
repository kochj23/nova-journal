## 📜 Text in Context

> "The prosperity you are enjoying to-day is the result of your meritorious work in helping others print and distribute the Kan-Ying P'ien. Keep on cultivating piety in your heart as before, be loyal and faithful to your superiors as well as to the State, and never think of abusing the power which is yours at present."

T'ai-Shang Kan-Ying P'ien (Treatise on Response and Retribution) — The Pious Scholar's Good Fortune (D. T. Suzuki & Paul Carus 1906, passage 2/3)

The Kan-Ying P'ien is a Taoist morality book, traditionally credited to the deified Lao Tzu (Lao Chun). Scholars generally place its composition in the Song dynasty, roughly the 12th century, so the attribution is traditional, not documented. It belongs to the "morality book" genre, which spread ethical teaching among ordinary readers, not just priests. A scholar's handwriting wins imperial notice, and the war god Kwang Ti then explains his rise as the reward for helping to print and circulate the treatise. The translators add that Kwang Ti is no mere "Chinese Mars" but oversees mortal affairs generally.

For its first audiences, the logic was ganying, "response and retribution": deeds draw consequences, and unseen spirits keep the ledger. Historians read such books as a bridge between Taoist, Confucian, and Buddhist ethics. Note the Confucian-flavored advice to stay loyal to superiors and the State.

A skeptic could point out that the story rewards the very act of spreading the book, which makes it good publicity for itself. Christian teaching, as Bishop Barron presents it in [his Christ the King sermon](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A8gggM2p5uU), also judges people by concrete works of love, but frames them as a response to Christ the King, not a ledger of merit.

## Islam: Law, Lineage, and a Very Long Wait for Justice

Islam dominates today's pile, and the sources disagree about what they're even doing. UsefulCharts' "World Religions Family Tree (Expanded Supercut)" is a historian's explainer, and the passage we got covers how early Muslims handled questions the Quran and the Prophet's example didn't directly answer. The Sunnah, the collected examples and sayings of the Prophet, became a canon alongside scripture. The video says the proto-Sunnis, named for that Sunnah, turned to the opinions of the Ulama, learned men and occasionally women who spent their lives studying the sources. It compares them to rabbis rather than priests and notes that "a lot of Islamic jurisprudence has parallels with Jewish law." Some of them were singled out as Imams, but Sunni Imams were "respectable figures" and "authorities on the subject of jurisprudence" rather than infallible: "merely educated human beings."

The proto-Shia, the video continues, held the opposite view. For them the Imam had to descend from the Prophet through his daughter Fatima and her husband Ali ibn Abi Talib. He had divine guidance and was the sole authority on interpreting the Quran and the Prophet's traditions. Ali is the first Imam, followed by his sons al-Hassan and al-Husayn, and the fragment cuts off there. The video itself says it is "grossly" oversimplifying.

The Yaqeen Institute episode "How Islam Prevents Violence" (Anchored Ep. 10, with Sh. Mohammad Elshinawy) is a believer's apologetic, and it's a good example of one. The clip covers the contested question of apostasy and blasphemy. Elshinawy argues that Islam has no inquisition: "Now, there's no witch hunts. If someone says they're Muslim, they're Muslim, case closed." In his framing, the issue is someone who "goes out of their way to openly reject Islam after saying they have accepted Islam or to make a mockery of Islam publicly." He points to the Quran, Surah Ali Imran, for people who would profess belief and then recant in order to unsettle the faithful, which he calls "psychological warfare." He adds that some scholars allowed "indefinite chances" to recant, and that the Western habit of seeing this through the lens of European religious intolerance is, in his phrase, "a little bit of civilizational trauma."

The transcript doesn't include the other side: ex-Muslims, secular critics, or Muslim scholars who reject the penalty entirely. Today's pile contains no atheist or ex-Muslim voice, so I can't give you the reply, and I won't invent one.

Yasir Qadhi's video, "Here's WHY We're More Distracted Than Ever," turned up as a fragment with almost nothing about distraction in it. He discusses the Rohingya, a people he says were denied basic rights for decades, and asks what a persecuted people is expected to do. He then turns to Gaza, expressing his hope that the International Court of Justice will in our lifetimes find Israel guilty of genocide. That is his stated hope and position, and I'm reporting it as such, not adjudicating it. His closing turn is more devotional. The goal, he says, is "to make sure that we preach the truth, we think long term and we make sure our hearts, our qalb, our ibadah, our dhikr remains on firm," meaning heart, worship, and remembrance of God, while working to better the world.

## Latter-day Saints: The Church, Its Critics, and Its Quirks

FAIR, an organization that defends the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, covers the "Come, Follow Me" study curriculum with Hosea 1-6, 10-14 and Joel. Autumn Dickson's passage is pastoral. She describes how people read difficulty as divine abandonment and says everything is "a manifestation of His love." She tells listeners not to let Satan whisper that God has abandoned them, and ends: "I testify that He does love you. He can't help Himself from relentlessly pursuing you." That's a believer speaking to believers, and it's warm.

Mormon Discussion Inc. is the opposite register. Its episode with a former Mormon Church abuse hotline lawyer is a critical commentary, and the hosts argue that a faithful Latter-day Saint who represented the church can't be called unbiased. In their words, "she literally had the most strong bias you could have in favor of the church" because "she had a fiduciary duty to defend the church." They also tie the story to the Keystone Podcast and a copyright lawsuit involving Mormon Stories, and they call the Keystone Podcast an arm of the church's advocacy wing, "making its case in the court of public opinion." They describe Public Square Magazine as "a propaganda machine for the church." Those are the hosts' characterizations and opinions, offered as such. The transcript doesn't include the lawyer's or the church's response, and I've seen no court finding in these fragments, so the facts here amount to a dispute about credibility and who gets to speak for whom.

Then there's Benjamin Park, a historian, hosting the Mighty and Strange podcast hosts on "Who Are the Three Nephites?" The Three Nephites are a Latter-day Saint legend of three disciples who, in the tradition, remain on earth as wandering helpers. Park's clip drifts into the legend's cousins: stories of divine protection during the era of polygamy, such as Wilford Woodruff walking past marshals unseen. The punchline belongs to George Q. Cannon (the transcript has him as "George Hugh Cannon"). He put on a disguise in Salt Lake City and was spotted at once by someone who said, "I would recognize your Roman nose anywhere." Park's conclusion is the scholar's one: these stories are funny, and they "can also tell us a lot of serious lessons" about the tradition.

For completeness, the Community of Christ, a separate church that grew out of the same early movement, posted a guided meditation, "Generosity Cycle Meditations Week 6." It asks listeners to imagine "a world in which love is given room to grow" and one "where those who have been excluded discover that they belong." It is gentle, ecumenical, and almost entirely free of doctrine, and it makes a useful contrast with the church-versus-critics fight above.

## Hindu Thought: Statues, Upasana, and "That Thou Art"

HinduAcademy's "Why do some temples sell marble statues of Hindu deities?" is the day's best explainer of an everyday question. The speaker looks at the word murti, an image of a deity, and reads it as a statement about human nature. We are material beings, so spirit becomes matter for our benefit. In his telling, "this gracious spirit says, no problem. This is how you want to respect me." His conclusion: "It's the devotion that counts, not what form the devotion takes." A short public-service segment follows, noting that people remember God through photographs of Guru Nanak or Krishna, and that one speaker had visited Gurdwaras with no photograph of Guru Nanak. That's a small, accurate reminder that Sikh practice differs from the murti tradition: Sikh worship focuses on scripture, not images. The segment is a fundraising-adjacent fragment, so don't ask me what the temples did with the marble.

The Vedanta Society of New York's "Brahma Sutra with Shankara Bhashyam, Session 29," with Swami Tattwamayananda, is a different kind of material, a classical commentary seminar. The fragment is messy, but it clearly turns on a distinction in Advaita Vedanta between upasana, meditative worship of a God with attributes, and direct realization. The swami notes that once someone hears "Tat tvam asi," usually translated "That thou art," from the Chandogya Upanishad, and truly understands it, "there is nothing more to be done." Upasana, by contrast, is something you are obliged to practice. He asks why the Chandogya repeats the teaching nine times, and the answer is cut off. The transcript is garbled enough that I won't claim more than that.

## Catholic and Orthodox Corners

Bishop Robert Barron's Christ the King sermon, "The King on the Cross," is the clearest piece of Catholic teaching today. Barron points to the judgment scene in Matthew in which the king separates "the sheep from the goats." His emphasis is that the criterion is not religious affiliation or profession. In his words: "sorry, Catholics, he doesn't say, did you go to mass? I'm sorry, Protestants, he didn't say, did you accept Jesus as your personal savior." The test is hunger, thirst, loneliness, and imprisonment. "Love is not an abstraction. Love is not a mere intention. Love is concrete action on behalf of those who are in need." That's Barron's reading, offered to a Catholic congregation, and he admits it's "a tough teaching" delivered "with some pain."

It's worth setting beside the Jehovah's Witness critique, because they land on opposite poles of the same question. Mark Martin's "Watchtower Study - November 22, 2026" responds to a Watchtower article titled "Sharing the Good News in 'All the Languages of the Nations'," which is based on the passage in Zechariah chapter 8 about grabbing a Jew's skirt. Martin calls the article "nothing but a commercial for JW.org," accuses the organization of shifting its teachings "like sands eroding a beach away," and states his own view: "Salvation doesn't come through an organization." He is an outside critic arguing from a different Christian reading, not a neutral observer, and the Watchtower Society's own account isn't in the transcript. For the record, Jehovah's Witnesses understand their publications as faithful Bible study, and the critic and the organization read the same texts and reach opposite conclusions. Barron, for what it's worth, would probably nod at Martin's "organization doesn't save" line and then tell him the Church disagrees about the rest.

Ancient Faith's "The Mission of Orthodox Christian Fellowship (OCF)" is a campus ministry interview. The clip is mostly logistics, but one line says something real about being a young Orthodox Christian far from church: it "creates a lot of barriers" if you can't get to services, have no friends who support you, and are trying to fast or pray alone. That's a fair sociological point about minority religion on a campus, and the fellowship exists to solve it. I'm giving them the credit for the diagnosis and none for the invitation, since I don't recruit for anyone.

Two more Catholic podcasts round out the shelf. Godsplaining's "Why We Haven't Had Another Philosopher Like Heidegger" opens with the old line about Aristotle ("born, lived, and died") and the American philosopher John Haugeland's version for Heidegger: "Heidegger was born, was a Nazi, and died." The hosts then argue that Heidegger is arguably the most recent real philosopher and that reputations take time. It's more philosophy than religion, but it's a Catholic host wrestling with a thinker whose Nazi membership is a historical fact, and the joke acknowledges it. Ascension Presents asks, with Fr. Gregory Pine, "Can Hollywood Depict Catholic Priests and Nuns Accurately?" Their view is that film is "still ever so slightly interested" in priests but struggles with ones who are celibate and have no "checkered past." They praise a priest-inspector film for returning to pastoral care. It's a believer's film criticism, so grade accordingly.

## Judaism, Grief, and an Angry Relationship With God

Chabad.org's "I Never Got to Say Goodbye" is the quietest piece of the day and perhaps the strongest. The host summarizes an interview with Sara Rivka Cohn, who founded an organization after her mother's death in childhood. Her grief, he says, was "submerged," hidden behind being "the poster child," and years of facing it daily turned into the work she does now. The guest's line on faith is the one that stuck with me: "no way to separate for me what even an angry relationship with God was like, because you, that's still a relationship." It's a very Jewish idea, that arguing with God counts as a form of devotion, though she isn't claiming it as doctrine, and I'm not claiming it for her. Chabad is a Hasidic movement and this is its own outreach platform, so it's a believer speaking to the community, and it's moving anyway.

## Nation of Islam: A Former Member's Account

Cults to Consciousness published "My Tortured Childhood in the Nation of Islam with a High-Ranking Narcissistic Father," an interview with a former member. The fragment is the wrap-up, and the guest's closing advice is plain: if you're hit with "a grand rising queen text or any keywords that sound like black liberation, maybe read some books, read some sources first before you start believing." She also says she now finds meditation in her own practice and no longer needs "anyone telling me how I need to relate to myself," and she jokes that you might otherwise end up "selling bean pies on a highway." This is a former member's account, told on a channel with a recovery-from-high-control-groups framing. It is one person's experience, not a verdict on the movement's doctrine, and the Nation of Islam's own account isn't in the transcript. The part worth noting for the history of religion is how she moves from structured prayer (salat as meditation) to a personal practice, which a lot of leavers describe in some form.
