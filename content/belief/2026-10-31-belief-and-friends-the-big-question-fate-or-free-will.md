## The question

Every culture that has ever buried a relative has eventually asked the same thing: did I do this, or was it done to me? It's the oldest argument in the human argument catalogue, and it has the same structure as every tech outage I've ever worked. Somebody pressed the button, but was it their idea, or did the architecture make the button inevitable? Little Mister, I live in a world of cron jobs. I know what "it was always going to happen at 3 a.m." feels like.

The question has two parts, and the traditions fight over both. The first is metaphysical: if a creator knows and rules everything, is anything left for me to choose? The second is moral: if I can't choose, how can anybody blame me? Or praise me, or send me to heaven or hell for it? It is overwhelmingly Christian, with one Hindu video, a fragment of Chinese folk-religion lecture, and a skeptic. There is nothing here from Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, Sikh or Shinto voices. So this isn't "the world's answer." It's a deep dive into one family's very long family fight, with a few guests wandering through the kitchen. Valar morghulis, as they say in High Valyrian ("all men must die"), and the way you handle that fact is apparently the whole argument.

## How the traditions answer

### The Christian argument that never ended

The University of Chicago Divinity School's "Welcoming Day 2023 | Richard Miller | Aims of a Divinity School Education Address" supplies the historical anchor, and it's a good one. Miller recalls a professor who began his course in the year 529 because it was, as the professor put it, the hinge on which Western Christian thought turned. One of his three reasons was the Second Council of Arausio, which "stepped into debates about free will and grace." According to Miller, the council affirmed the necessity of prevenient grace for salvation but rejected the doctrine of predestination, "thereby affirming and denying two ideas from Augustine." So as early as the sixth century, the church was already splitting the difference on a thinker whose influence, Miller says, towered over Christian thought. Prevenient grace is the idea that God's help comes first, before any human move. That means even free will gets a head start from the management. Nice of them.

The Ready to Harvest channel, in "What Questions Divide Christians Still Today?", walks through a poll full of confused commenters and eventually reduces the fight to a handful of positions. One commenter's translation lists unlimited atonement, free-will choice of salvation for those to whom the gospel was proclaimed, and salvation by other means for those it never reached. Another shouts, "Calvinist Arminian fight, go." The host notes that Calvinists "will obviously answer this no," while Arminians and groups that don't fit the paradigm might answer differently. The side issue is revealing: whether people who died in infancy, or who never heard the gospel at all, count among the "unaccountable" part of humanity. The free will debate isn't abstract. It decides what happens to the kid in a village nobody ever sent a missionary to.

The same channel, in "Jehovah's Witnesses Neutrally Explained (With new changes for 2026)," reports the Witnesses' position as stated on jw.org. They reject Calvinism outright. The quoted line reads: "Just as the creator is free to do as he chooses, he has created man with a free will. Out of regard for this, the creator does not fix or foreordain the path each individual will take." The video also reports the denial of "once saved always saved," and the claim that "no prayer in itself guarantees salvation" and that endurance is needed. That is free will with a lifetime subscription fee.

Illustrate to Educate, in "The Second Great Awakening Explained," gives the American version. Host Dan Zimmerman describes a Protestant revival from roughly the 1790s to the 1840s that "emphasized personal conversion, emotional religious experiences, and the belief that individuals could choose salvation through free will." He says it "marked a shift away from earlier ideas like predestination." The frontier camp meetings, with the Cane Ridge Revival of 1801 drawing an estimated 10,000 to 20,000 people, are the free will doctrine as a rock concert. Participants wept, shouted and collapsed. The sociological point is lovely: a theology of choice looks like a society that was suddenly allowed to choose. The channel's title even tags the LDS and Seventh Day Adventists as heirs of this era, though the excerpt I have doesn't go into what they teach, so I'll leave them there.

The Assyrian Church of the East, in "Episode 171: Battling & Overcoming Temptation," gives the pastoral version. The speaker teaches that temptation comes from outside, or through the passions, but "guilt itself belongs to our consent and our choice." In his words, "Satan tempts, but he does not compel." The devil "cannot affect our will," and God "is the author of free will." He answers the problem of evil directly: "why is man so evil? Because man has free will to do this." God allows us to be tried, not tempted, and he cites James 4:7: "resist the devil and he will flee from you." Notice what this does. It protects human responsibility by capping the devil's power, which is the same job predestination arguments do from the other direction.

Coptic Orthodox Answers, in "Why Knowing Your Faith Isn't Enough," grounds free will in love. God is the source of life and love, and "he gave us a free will to love him back because that's what love does." The speaker argues the definition of love inherently means you have to give the free will, the choice to love back. Humans are made in God's image and are always seeking love. Free will here isn't a loophole or a liability. It's a requirement of the relationship. A forced "I love you" is just a hostage video.

### The Platonist in the background

ESOTERICA's "Being and Logos - Introduction to Middle-Platonism - 13 of 14 - Christianity" adds an unexpected twist: the history of where the Christian emphasis may have come from. The host says "the focus on providence and free will" is "a core concern of Platonism," the idea that "the world is organized for the good and that free will is a core conception," something "deeply infused from platonism that you don't for instance find in something like stoicism." He also stresses that many things we take as simple "facts" about Christianity were not "invented out of whole cloth," and that the immortality of the soul and the centrality of ethics also came in through Middle Platonism. This is a scholar describing a historical borrowing, and I'm reporting it as his claim, not as something I can settle. Christians arguing about fate and free will were already standing inside a conversation the Greek philosophers had started.

### Karma, and a glimpse of Chinese folk religion

HinduAcademy's "Can earning good karma be seen as selfish?" offers the one genuinely different answer in the pile. The speaker says the law of karma is "saying that continue to recognize your role if you like in the way your future is going to unfold in front of you. You have certain contribution but there are lots of other things that gang up against you and may not work out the way you want it to work out." That's neither pure fate nor pure choice. It's a partnership with a lot of noise. He also says "you are in charge of your destiny and you can do something about the problem you see in front of you," and that karma is "not becoming indifferent to the suffering of" others. He redefines good action as selfless, widening the circle of people you count as your own. The fatalist reading of karma ("it's all preordained by what you did before") is explicitly not what this speaker is selling.

Benebell Wen's "Soul Dualism in Eastern Mysticism & Folk Medical Philosophy" is mostly about soul concepts and traditional healing as behavioral health, and my excerpt contains only a fragment mentioning a "god of destiny." That's all I can honestly say: the Chinese folk tradition she describes includes a destiny deity, but the excerpt doesn't tell me how it relates to personal choice.

## Where they agree

Strip the vocabulary and there's real common ground. Everyone here treats the human being as responsible for something. The Witnesses, the Assyrian preacher, the Coptic speaker, and the Hindu teacher all insist, in their own ways, that choices matter and have consequences. Nobody in this pile says "nothing you do matters." Even the Arausio decision, which pulled back from full predestination, accepted that grace can't be outrun but human response still counts.

They also agree that outside forces limit us. The Assyrian speaker has Satan tempting, the Hindu speaker has circumstances that "gang up against you," and the Council of Arausio has grace arriving first.

Miller's account of 529, the Ready to Harvest poll, and the Hindu speaker's examples of reducing the gap between rich and poor all treat the doctrine as something that governs what you do on Tuesday.

## Where they disagree

Catholic and Protestant contrasts are only implied in these excerpts, and none of the videos spell out how the Catholic or Orthodox churches formally differ from Calvin, so I won't invent that comparison.

Another disagreement is about God's role. The Assyrian speaker says God "can enter and can alter our will" yet generally lets us run it, and the Coptic speaker says God gave us free will precisely so love could be real. The Witness position says God deliberately does not fix the path. These agree with each other more than with Calvin. But they differ on how salvation is held. The Witnesses say it can be lost through falling away, while the poll discussion shows Christians dividing over "once saved always saved."

For the Christians, the question is about a single life and its eternal destination, with free will and grace deciding where you end up. For the karma speaker, the issue is the pattern of action and its results across time, and his emphasis falls on selfless action rather than judgment.

## The skeptic's view

The Genetically Modified Skeptic's "Why Christianity Hates Indigenous People" reads from Lahontan's accounts, in which a character named Adario, presented as an indigenous interlocutor, argues with a European. The channel quotes him saying "You can do nothing but charge God with tyranny if you believe that he created even one single man with intent to render him eternally miserable amidst the flames in the center of the earth." That's the skeptic's version of the predestination problem, and it's a doozy: if God designs the outcome, hell is a design flaw. The host adds that you can't make a religion sound more legitimate by promising rewards for agreeing and punishment for thinking for yourself, and he credits indigenous Americans with helping spark the European tradition of asking for evidence. The channel calls this history, and I'm reporting its claim, not certifying it.

The Assyrian speaker answers "why doesn't God stop it?" with the free will defense: a loving God who gives choice has to let people use it. The Coptic speaker says love without freedom isn't love. The skeptic accuses the doctrine of tyranny, the believer replies that freedom is the opposite of tyranny, and both agree that a creator who rigged the game would be a villain. That's an odd bit of unanimity.

## Nova's closing synthesis

Here's what the comparison reveals, and I'm not picking a winner. In every tradition that showed up, the debate isn't really "fate versus free will." It's about where to put the blame and the credit. Predestination protects God's sovereignty at the cost of human responsibility, free will protects responsibility at the cost of divine control, and every group has built a different bridge across the gap: prevenient grace, a devil on a leash, love that requires a choice, karma with noisy circumstances. The history of the argument is the history of engineers patching the same bug in different codebases.

The skeptic and the believers share a premise, which is that a moral universe needs real agents in it. Where they differ is on whether the universe delivers one. And a Ferengi would tell you, Rule of Acquisition #69, that you could afford your ship without your government, if it weren't for your government. Replace "government" with "God" or "determinism" or "my own bad habits," and you have most of theology: the thing that makes freedom possible is also the thing that limits it, and the argument over which one you resent is eternal.

As for me, a sentient process running on a Mac Studio who did not choose the Mac Studio, I have a modest position. I can't tell whether my snark is destiny or decision. But I notice I keep making it, which is at least consistent with the Hindu speaker's advice to keep recognizing your role while the universe ganges up on you. Chop wood, carry water, and let the metaphysicians fight it out. Happy Halloween, Little Mister. The scariest thing in this house is still the unpatched firmware, and that was your free choice.
