## 📜 Text in Context

> He is rich, affluent, and wealthy. He has these kinds of wealth: the wealth of faith, ethics, conscience, prudence, learning, generosity, and wisdom.

Digha Nikaya (Long Discourses) — DN30 The Marks of a Great Man (Bhikkhu Sujato 2018, passage 16/28)

This comes from the Pali canon's Long Discourses, which Theravada Buddhists treat as the words of the Buddha. The text was transmitted orally for generations. Tradition holds it was first written down in Sri Lanka around the first century BCE, and scholars generally date the composition of the discourses to several centuries earlier. The sutta draws on an older Indian idea that a "great man" bears 32 bodily marks, and that he is destined to be either a universal ruler or a Buddha. The twist is that the marks are explained as the fruit of good deeds across past lives, and that the Buddha's real riches are moral and intellectual.

Modern readers often take the passage as a redefinition of status. Historians also see the "marks" as a bridge between Buddhist and brahmanical ideas of greatness, and some regard the verse sections as later additions.

It connects to a [Vedanta Society of New York lecture on the Brahma Sutra](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TvSOJH9yvnk). There Swami Tattwamayananda teaches that admirable qualities in a saint all flow from the one Brahman, and that "the difference between an amoeba and a Buddha is one of degrees not of kind." Buddhists would part ways with that, because their tradition denies any single underlying Self from which such qualities arise.

## Walking Away From a War: Salama ibn al-Akwa

The Yaqeen Institute's "The Firsts" series, with Dr. Omar Suleiman, profiled Salama ibn al-Akwa in "Salama ibn al-Akwa (ra): The Fastest Runner In Medina." The excerpt we have is the back half of his life, and it's a good one. After being famously fast and brave, Salama withdrew once Muslims began fighting each other. Suleiman reads that as sincerity of character: he wanted no part of the *fitna*, the civil strife among the Companions of the Prophet. In Suleiman's telling, Salama married, had children, and herded goats in the desert at Ar-Rabada.

The detail that stuck with me is the visit from Abdurrahman ibn Razeen. Salama, by then an old man, held out his hands and said these are the hands with which he pledged *bay'ah*, the oath of allegiance, to the Prophet. Abdurrahman kissed them. Suleiman says Salama returned to Medina a few nights before he died, in the year 74 after the Hijrah. He outlived most of the Companions and was buried in Medina's cemetery, which the transcript renders as "al-Baqir"; I'm assuming that's Baqi' and the auto-captioner had a long day.

As a historian I'll note what this is: a devotional portrait by a believer, in a series called "The Firsts," about a man whose résumé is "fast." The source here is a preacher telling the story in his own register, and I'm reporting it as what Muslims in this tradition admire. Plenty of institutions would be better off if their fastest people occasionally went and herded goats.

## One Source, Many Faces: Vedanta on Where Qualities Come From

The Vedanta Society of New York posted Session 27 of its Brahma Sutra with Shankara's commentary, with Swami Tattwamayananda, covering the introduction to Sutra 1.1.12. The passage in view deals with *upasana* (the transcript spells it "vasana"), meditation on a deity. The teaching, as the Swami presents it, is that when you meditate on a deity for some admirable quality, you shouldn't imagine the deity is the owner of that quality. The quality comes from the same all-pervading Paramatman, the supreme Self, and merely shows up in that particular form. He points to the Gita for this, and the transcript renders the verse's Sanskrit loosely enough that I won't quote it.

That also explains, he says, why you respect a saint. A saint isn't the source of the virtue; the saint's *antahkarana*, the inner mind-instrument, has been purified into a clear reflecting medium. And then the line of the day, attributed to the Swami's own teacher: "the difference between an amoeba and a Buddha is one of degrees not of kind."

The Abrahamic voices here speak of a personal God who knows your circumstances. The Advaita-leaning commentary here says the same ground underlies everything, from the amoeba to the Buddha, and individual saints are better windows, not better gods. They're just different answers to what is the thing under the thing.

## Why Do People Who Don't Practice Still Want the Funeral?

The Jewish Theological Seminary's "Rabbinic Futures, Short and Long Term" is mostly housekeeping in our excerpt: a plug for an upcoming talk by Rabbi Gordon Tucker on "anticipatory memory," which the host describes as how we remember the future. I'd pay money to hear a definition that isn't just "anxiety," but we'll wait two weeks.

He performed a funeral for the husband of longtime friends of his parents. The family was so distant from Jewish practice that their children couldn't recite Kaddish, the mourner's prayer. Yet they wanted a full traditional funeral, and earlier a full traditional wedding. His conclusion: "There's something in these rituals that is magic. And the magic makes our lives better," and people sense it even if they couldn't describe or admit it.

Anthropologists have a decades-old observation that ritual persists after belief thins, especially at births, weddings and deaths. This is a clergy member's version of that. It's a first-person impression, not a survey, and "magic" is his word, used loosely. But it's a candid admission from inside a seminary that the audience is sometimes there for the rites rather than the creed. Nobody in the excerpt addresses what that means for the rabbi's job, which is presumably what the "futures" in the title is about.

## Tanya and the Burglar

Chabad.org's podcast "She Used Tanya to Disarm a Burglar" features a woman, Joanne, whose story is that she drew on the teachings of the Tanya, the foundational text of Chabad Hasidism, to cope with a home robbery. The excerpt has the host summarizing: she overcame her own trauma to help her children see the burglar "as being a child of God as well, who was just hungry." I can't verify the incident, and the host is her admirer, so this is how Chabad frames it. Later in the interview she describes living with integrity "with Hashem" and checking whether "Hashem is happy with this choice right now," which frees her from worrying about what others think. "Even if he doesn't like what I'm doing, he's going to love me unconditionally anyway."

A text written in the late eighteenth century is being used as a trauma-management tool, and the burglar is asked to be a person rather than a threat. Whether that works on actual burglars is not covered.

## Daniel in Babylon, Told Two Ways

FAIR (the Latter-day Saint apologetics organization) released "Come, Follow Me with FAIR – Daniel 1–7 – Part 1 – Autumn Dickson." "Come, Follow Me" is the study curriculum that Latter-day Saints follow through the scriptures, and this week it's Daniel. Dickson's reading is devotional: Daniel is taken from his family, but "he was never truly alone." If you know God "as a father figure," she says, Daniel didn't need to be devastated, and if he'd abandoned his faith he'd still have ended up in Babylon, still under a volatile king.

First, notice the argument shape: the circumstances are fixed, the variable is the relationship. That's a theological claim about what faith does, not a historical one about Daniel. ## The Scholar Who Wants You to Read in Context

McClellan's video "James Talarico got caught LYING about the Bible?" is a pointed critique, and the title is clickbait that the excerpt doesn't fully support, so treat the headline with suspicion. The part we have is the scholar's method. He argues that Matthew 19 is about divorce, and Jesus's reference back to Genesis 2 is "drawing an inference in an awkward and indirect way," so "Matthew 19 is not addressing gay marriage." He then notes that Leviticus 18 and Romans 1 condemn same-sex intercourse "for specific reasons," and that inferring from that a condemnation of gay marriage is, he says, "not illegitimately" reasonable but not a direct address. And his broader charge: that using the Bible to justify one's politics "requires ignoring the context" and imposing presuppositions "about inspiration, inerrancy, univocality, historicity, traditional authorship, traditional dating." In his words, "it is identity politics all the way down."

McClellan's critique here is symmetric by design: the framework that reads the Bible as a single, inerrant voice isn't, in his view, a framework for understanding the texts on their own terms, whoever uses it. That's a scholar's position and plenty of believers would contest it on theological grounds, since for them inspiration and unity aren't presuppositions to be bracketed but the whole point. Second, we don't hear what the politician said. A video whose title promises a liar and whose excerpt delivers a hermeneutics seminar has done what the algorithm asked of it.

## What a Priest Is For

Bishop Robert Barron, in "The Meaning of Catholic Ordination" from the Word on Fire show, takes a viewer question about the title "Father" for priests, set against the line in the Gospel, quoted in the excerpt as "Call no one on earth your father." Barron's answer is that you shouldn't "literalize" it; the point is that God is the great source of spiritual life, and "no honest Catholic thinks that a priest who's being called father is somehow competing with the primacy of God." He adds, "fathers give life, right, in the biological order. Priests give life in the spiritual order." And then a human aside: he was called Father for about thirty years, and as a bishop nobody calls him that anymore and he misses it.

Notice that this is the same kind of interpretive move McClellan is complaining about from the other side, only in the other direction: Barron says don't read a verse hyper-literally, because the surrounding teaching tells you what it means. McClellan says read it in its own context, because later traditions import things. They'd agree on "context," and disagree on whose.

## Doing It Right: Liturgy and Apology

Ancient Faith's "How to Be an Orthodox Christian – The Divine Liturgy Pt 3: Dismissal of the Catechumens [EP. 27]" is a practical coaching session. The speaker lists the prayers the priest has offered and says that "the events of salvation happen not long ago, but become the time of our own life." The suggested homework is to read the prayer before the Gospel in advance, to "listen for the words around it," and to pray for the catechumens, those preparing to be received, "even if you're one of them." It's liturgy as time travel, if you accept the premise, and notably active: the service teaches you to move through time toward eternity, in the speaker's phrase.

Ascension Presents' "How to Make a Good Apology" borrows structure from the Catholic Act of Contrition. The speaker's parts: be personal, show contrition, be specific, own what you did and what you failed to do, understand the stakes, commit to amendment. Then comes the line I'd tattoo on half of the internet: forgiveness may be offered, but "now we've got to rebuild trust," and penance is the repair work that follows. I appreciate a tradition that noticed apologies without behavior change are just press releases.

The Godsplaining podcast's "Can Science Cure Down Syndrome? A Catholic Scientist Responds" is a title my excerpt doesn't deliver on: we get only the wrap-up, with the host praying a rosary on planes and a reflection after praying before the Blessed Sacrament: "Lord, is this for you or am I just doing all of this for me?"
