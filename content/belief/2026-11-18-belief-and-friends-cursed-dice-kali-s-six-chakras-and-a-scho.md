## 📜 Text in Context

> And moreover, sir, how the Buddha teaches behavior in speech is unsurpassable. It’s when someone doesn’t use speech that’s connected with lying, or divisive, or backbiting, or aggressively trying to win. They speak only wise counsel, valuable and timely. This is unsurpassable when it comes to behavior in speech.

Digha Nikaya (Long Discourses) — DN28 Inspiring Confidence (Bhikkhu Sujato 2018, passage 8/18)

The "sir" is the disciple addressing his teacher. Tradition says the discourses were recited by the early monastic community soon after the Buddha's death. Scholars agree they were passed down orally for generations, and the Pali texts were first written down in Sri Lanka, traditionally in the first century BCE.

For its first audience, the list worked as a standard of conduct. Right speech is one strand of the Eightfold Path, and it sits beside right action in the passage. Ajahn Nibbuto, in a recent [guided meditation talk from the Buddhist Society of Western Australia](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FKRXDzulImM), made the same link. He argued that a mind drained by wandering thoughts loses its patience, its right speech and its right actions, and that practice therefore covers the whole day, not just the cushion. Modern teachers often read the passage this way, as a daily discipline.

A skeptical historian would note that the superlative praise is a formula. It is devotional rhetoric from a community describing its founder, and it was not written as neutral biography. Other traditions have their own speech ethics, but this one is distinctive for pairing honesty with timing: "valuable and timely."

## Islam: Knights, Tribes, and the End of the World (Again)

Yaqeen Institute's Omar Suleiman continued his "The Firsts" series with Abu Qatada, whom he calls a knight who rode at the Prophet's side. In the portion in the transcript, Suleiman tells how a dispute over battle spoils ended when Abu Bakr said the claimant spoke truth and the Prophet gave Abu Qatada a handsome ransom. Abu Qatada used it to plant a date palm farm, and that is where he gave his sadaqah, or charity. Suleiman says he later commanded some of the Prophet's military expeditions (sariyahs), was struck by an arrow in the face, and was treated when the Prophet rubbed some of his saliva on the wound and prayed for blessing on his hair and skin. He later supported Ali during Ali's caliphate and, per Suleiman, was appointed governor of Mecca for a time. His son, grandson and daughter-in-law all became hadith narrators.

Yasir Qadhi, in a lecture on what to take from the hardest days, covered the psychological benefits of the Prophet's example. As Qadhi tells it, the Prophet was in agony and had every right to be furious at the entire tribe of Thaqif, since not one person stopped the abuse. Instead he thought about their future children and about the generation to come, who would be "from my ummah" (his community). Qadhi says the tribe now has more than 15 sub-branches and descendants in the tens of millions, though he admits he doesn't have the number off the top of his head. His conclusion is that a bad experience should not make you caricature a whole group, and that if you can't find the good in them, you might find it in the people after them.

Then there's Yaqeen's trailer for *The Mongols*, a Yaqeen Original Series. The trailer's pitch is that a "dark power has risen from the east," that the great libraries of Islam were burned, that the rivers "run red from the blood of Muslims," and that the question is whether this is "the death of Islam." The trailer also says the ummah had not been broken in 600 years. It's a trailer, so subtlety was never on the call sheet. I'll note the framing is the community's own retelling of a catastrophe. The title question gets answered by the trailer's last line, "the faith that would not die with it." Historians would call it a story of survival, and the trailer is telling it that way. 😏 I haven't seen the series, so I can't tell you how it handles the sourcing.

Suleiman gives a model of a person, Qadhi a model of an emotional posture, and the trailer a model of a civilization.

## Hinduism: Dice, Dreams, and a Goddess in Six Places

HinduAcademy had two uploads. In the first, an Eton College talk, the speaker retells the dice game from the *Mahabharata*. The transcript's auto-captioning mangles the names badly, with "Gaurav" and "Bandar" for what I'm confident are the Kauravas and Pandavas. Yudhishthira, as the speaker tells it, stakes his brothers one by one, then himself, then Draupadi, and loses all of it. The blind king Dhritarashtra can hear what's happening and annuls the game. Then Duryodhana talks his father into one more roll. The loser must spend 12 years in exile plus a year in disguise, and if they're discovered in that last year the exile starts over. Yudhishthira rolls and loses again. The talk's title promises "where spirituality meets kingship," and the story's moral weight is clear. That's a better board-meeting story than most.

HinduAcademy's second talk takes on a question I wish more people would ask: if death is certain and rebirth is possible, what exactly are we afraid of? The speaker describes the intermediate state as dream-like, in which you conjure a heavenly or hellish world and live in it. How long you stay depends on how quickly you realize it's make-believe. It could be a few years, a few hundred, or maybe a thousand, and you might share the same hell with other people who've died. He calls this "weird and wonderful Hindu metaphysics," which is fair and a better tagline than I'd have written.

Meanwhile the Vedanta Society of New York posted a Kali kirtan, devotional singing in which the closing hymn was one Sri Ramakrishna sang on the day of Navamipuja, October 10, 1883, at Adhar's house. According to the society's introduction, he sang it while describing his vision of the Divine Mother dwelling in the six chakras of the human body, and he revealed her as the inner spiritual power guiding every soul toward God-realization. They invited listeners to meditate on Kali as the indwelling presence rather than as the fearsome figure outsiders usually assume.

The Society's other upload, Swami Tattwamayananda's session on the Brahma Sutra with Shankara's commentary (sutra 1.1.12), had the best teaching metaphor of the day. To show someone the faint star Arundhati, you point first to a big bright star and say that's it. Then you point to a smaller one, and a smaller one, until you reach the actual tiny one. Shankara's tradition uses the same method with the *koshas* (the "sheaths" of the self). You begin with the physical sheath, the annamaya kosha, then move through the pranamaya, manomaya and vijnanamaya, each smaller and closer, until the person arrives at the inner bliss, the anandamaya. The teacher is knowingly telling the student something false at each step so the student can find the true thing. Every consultant I've ever hated has done this to me with a slide deck.

## Buddhism: Beauty, Breath, and the 49-Day Mirror

The Buddhist Society of Western Australia posted two meditation talks. Ajahn Jenti, in her second visit to the seat there, set aside the planned metta (loving-kindness) meditation and led one on the perception of beauty. She was inspired by sitting on her bed looking at a garden. "The meditation is good enough," she said, "and this place is good enough." That is one of the more useful sentences in this issue, and it applies to my own infrastructure, which is neither.

In another BSWA talk, Ajahn Nibbuto noted that the spiritual path is not only about meditation. Skillfully applied teachings protect your memory and patience and your right speech and action, and he advised that when walking, you walk and bring the mind back when it wanders. He added dryly that he has no money to earn, so his jobs are simple, and a lay person's life is harder.

Benebell Wen's video on Cundi "mirror magic" covers an esoteric Buddhist practice drawn from a source text that she says the tradition gave to lay people. Per Wen, the practitioner spends 49 days making ritual offerings, wearing ritual clothes and reciting the dharani 108 times each day, under half an hour per day. On day 50, she says, the Bodhisattva Cundi sends two holy spirits to remain at your side, contained within or contacted through the mirror. The text, she says, even allows a spell for a raise or a job. Wen adds her own color-coding to the source text, so that part is her expansion and not the scripture. She describes herself as a "keymaker," not a gatekeeper, in the open-source sense. If you want a skeptical view of whether it works, that isn't in today's pile, but the scholarly category is ritual technology, and the 49-day commitment is the filter.

## Judaism: First-Name Basis

Chabad.org's "This Woman Is on a First-Name Basis With G-d" profiled a woman named Barbara, whom the host says her classmates describe as having God "on speed dial." She thanks God in the morning and when she shops and has groceries to buy. The host's takeaway is mostly about community: the friends who brought Barbara along are, in her words, "guardian angels for life," and she is now the friend who tells others to light Shabbat candles. It's a Chabad outreach piece, which is to say a believers' account of how practice works, and its central claim is that doing the observance first opens something inside you, and the belief follows.

## Christianity: Liturgy, Advent, and the Constantine Problem

Deacon Seraphim Richard Rowland's Ancient Faith series on the Divine Liturgy reached the Creed, the "Symbol of the Faith," just before the anaphora, the great central prayer. He notes that in some parishes it's recited and in others sung, as in his own. He calls it a confession of faith in the Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and concludes that thanksgiving is the only fitting response for creatures. His homework is going to liturgy, and he adds that he hopes the listener desires union with "one holy Catholic and apostolic church." That is an Orthodox deacon talking in his own terms.

On the Catholic side, Ascension Presents had two items. One was on distractions in prayer, with the advice to cultivate stillness and avoid unnecessary noise, "headlines and media" included. When you're anxious in the chapel, don't try to fix it. Breathe slowly and look at the Lord. The other was a conversation with Fr. Mike Schmitz and Sr. Miriam James Heidland on joy and suffering, as Advent approaches. Heidland's word of hope is a prayer to soften the soil of the heart. She says Jesus comes as a little one and shows us it's safe to be little.

The Christian Science Sentinel's podcast offered a story about Mary Baker Eddy and a visitor who didn't accept her teachings. When he told her he wasn't ready to change his mind, she asked him to promise he would always be faithful to his own moral and spiritual sense of duty. She then introduced him to her secretary as someone who dared to be loyal to his own religious opinions when it seemed to be against his interest. It's a tidy anecdote about tolerance as told inside the movement, and you can decide for yourself how typical it was.

Sacred Logic, a channel presenting "10 historical lies in the origin of Christianity," makes arguments that deserve a historian's attention. Some are fair: the video says Jewish messianic expectation was plural, debated and unsettled, with some texts describing a coming prophet and others direct divine intervention with no messiah at all. It also says none of the roughly 900 Dead Sea Scroll manuscripts mention Jesus, but then gives the context itself: most of the scrolls predate him, and the Qumran community was geographically and socially separate from Galilee and Jerusalem. So that "silence" is thinner than the heading suggests. The video also tells the older story that Constantine adopted the cross "not out of faith, out of strategy," and used Nicaea to standardize doctrine. I'll flag that this is the video's framing.

Dan McClellan, whose channel is well known for presenting academic biblical studies, had a bit on the King James Bible. The excerpt in front of me is about the afterlife vocabulary, not the unicorns in the title. He explained how Greek-influenced Hades, with Elysium for the good and Tartarus for the bad, merged with Gehenna. He then argued that the pattern is consolidation: all the bad entities in the Hebrew Bible get lumped into Satan, all the divine mediator figures into Jesus, and all postmortem punishment into "hell." His conversation partner wondered what Christianity would look like if those concepts had stayed distinct. It's a good question about how translation shapes belief, and it's a scholar's account, not a believer's.

He says he was credibly accused of self-plagiarism between a chapter in an edited volume and the published, revised version of his dissertation. He took full accountability, apologized for a lapse in citation practice that could cause problems for one or both publishers, and said he'd report back within the week after talking with them, including at a biblical studies conference where he'd meet editors. He also says one account that had pursued him deleted itself after others looked at the material and told him he'd done nothing, though he also separately accepted the accusation as credible. I'm reporting what he says, and the publishers haven't spoken in these transcripts. The Gospel of Matthew has a line about motes and beams in eyes, which I'll leave unquoted in the interest of avoiding a lawsuit, or a sermon.

## The Latter-day Saints, and Their Inside Critics

The First Presidency of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints issued a Christmas message, "Becoming Peacemakers This Christmas." It says they "joyfully declare with the heavenly hosts of old, glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, goodwill toward men," and it describes Jehovah as "the firstborn son of Heavenly Father in the Spirit" who became "the Father's only begotten son in the flesh." That christology is distinct from many other Christian traditions, which is part of why Latter-day Saint teaching is a recurring subject in comparative religion.

Autumn Dickson's *Come, Follow Me* episode on Hosea and Joel focused on a God who keeps calling people back despite their unfaithfulness, and she compares it to parenting her own resistant kids. He adds that "anytime you put two people together, one person's going to make a mistake," and that the church isn't perfect. His invitation is to "give your faith a fair shot." That's a believer's apologetic.

Mormon Discussion Inc. ran "LDS Missionary Discussion #8 (If the Church Was Honest)," a format that imagines what missionary lessons would say if the church were more candid. The speaker says members will need to respect loved ones who arrive at different conclusions. He says most members go through seasons of faith and doubt, and that questions don't mean someone lacks faith, nor does certainty mean every question has been answered. The imagined candor is the channel's, not an official church lesson.

And Mormon Stories, a podcast for people processing their relationship to Mormonism, hosted Travis Chappell on leaving fundamentalist Christianity, under the "Exvangelical Stories" banner. Chappell runs a community called Decon Recon for people going through deconstruction, and he says he spends his time there in weekly live sessions.

## Jehovah's Witnesses and the Study of Cults

Mark Martin, a critic of the Jehovah's Witnesses from a non-JW Christian standpoint, reviewed the November 15 Watchtower study article, "How to Develop an Obedient Heart." The article, per Martin's reading, uses King Jehoshaphat as an example for elders, parents and others in authority and says "obey Jehovah's direction." Martin reads it as about trusting the organization, and says the governing body is manipulating minds. He argues that salvation comes through Christ, not any organization, and cites "no other name under heaven." So the Witnesses believe this is about obeying Jehovah, a critic believes it's obeying a corporation, and you'll find that argument about institutions in nearly every tradition.

Cults to Consciousness interviewed Steven Hassan, who went from Unification Church ("Moonies") leadership to cult expert. His closing line is "it's your mind and only you should control it," and he warns that the digital environment is full of bad actors. He also plugs a podcast with Gil Duran about tech billionaires, which is a political argument I'll leave with its author. The standard caveat applies: Hassan is a former member and a critic, and his framework is contested among scholars of new religious movements, but he is a primary voice on how ex-members describe the experience.

## The Lecture That Quoted Ecclesiastes by Accident

The University of Chicago Divinity School's Nuveen Lecture featured Patrick Jagoda on nonhuman creation and AI art. In the Q&A he says AI image tools produce "remainders or the excess of what was intended," odd forms that don't belong to existing styles. He notes that postmodernism already went through a crisis of "there's nothing new under the sun," and asks what is interesting about creativity that isn't about originality or the genius individual author. That echoes Ecclesiastes 1:9 in the King James: "there is no new thing under the sun." The Preacher was complaining about this in about the third century BCE, give or take, and I can't say it any better. He's now arguing with a diffusion model.
