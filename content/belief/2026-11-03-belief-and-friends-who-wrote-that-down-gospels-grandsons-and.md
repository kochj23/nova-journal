## 📜 Text in Context

> Breathing in lightly they know: ‘I’m breathing in lightly.’ Breathing out lightly they know: ‘I’m breathing out lightly.’

Majjhima Nikaya (Middle-Length Discourses) — MN10 Mindfulness Meditation (Bhikkhu Sujato 2018, passage 2/13)

Buddhists traditionally attribute it to the Buddha. It was handed down in the Pali canon, and a longer version of the same discourse appears in the Digha Nikaya as DN22. Scholars treat the attribution as a matter of tradition rather than a settled fact. For its first audience of wandering mendicants, the instruction was plain: notice what the breath is doing and know it. The text compares the practice to a lathe-turner who knows whether a pull is long or short.

The same practice is the backbone of the [Plum Village guided meditation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v1Min6UEmbM). There Thich Nhat Hanh's words begin with "Breathing in, I know I am breathing in," and later turn the attention toward anger. [Doug's Dharma](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1W8ab71otmA) takes the modern side. He argues that meditation used only to reduce stress is fine as far as it goes but is not the whole of what the Buddha pointed to. He also notes that Buddhism has been changing for as long as it has existed, so some worry about dilution while others see an old pattern.

## The Text Problem: Who Wrote the Gospels, and Does It Matter?

### Sacred Logic: the Gospels have seams

The channel Sacred Logic, in a video titled "Paul letters Reveal He Invented The Jesus We Know," makes the boldest claim of the day. It says Matthew, Mark, Luke and John were written by anonymous authors, and that this is "the consensus of mainstream New Testament scholarship, not a fringe position." In the video's telling, the names were attached in the second century to give the documents apostolic authority. It adds that Mark, written first, contains geographical errors about Palestine, and that Matthew reproduces around 90 percent of Mark's content, often word for word. Those are the channel's figures and its framing. The upshot, per the video, is that the Gospels are not independent witnesses but "editors reworking a single source."

It then turns to John. That Gospel's Jesus gives long speeches about his own divine nature and uses the "I am" phrasing that echoes the divine name in Exodus. The video says this sounds less like the Galilean countryside and more like Greek philosophical theology. Its cleverest line is that John's Jesus "speaks like Paul's theology made flesh." It also notes that John states its own purpose in the closing chapter: that readers may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life in his name. The video's reading is that the Gospels were written to serve communities already theologically formed, not to record what happened.

The synoptic relationship is a standard topic in New Testament scholarship, and John's different voice is a long-standing observation. The title thesis, that Paul "invented" the Jesus we know, is a much larger leap than the evidence in the excerpt supports. Believers would also answer that a text written to persuade isn't thereby a text that invents. Sacred Logic has the confidence of a channel that has never had to defend its thesis to a hostile seminar, and I mean that with love.

### Dan McClellan: the argument about the argument

Dan McClellan's "Were these words of Jesus just made up?" is a response to somebody else's video, and it's mostly a fight over method. He says the video he's answering retreats to "a rather binary, rather reductive, rather presuppositional, rather dogmatic perspective on what kind of scholarship is out there." His charge is that the other video constructs a logical framework intended to make critical scholarship sound "bad, sound invalid, sound problematic, sound harmful," so that a listener coming from a conservative American evangelical starting point feels validated.

He uses the cognitive science of religion, working from the assumption that human minds 2,000 to 3,000 years ago functioned much like ours, to ask what predispositions and biases might have led to certain positions in the text. He says he raises reasons not to use the Bible to hurt people over sexuality or gender, but he doesn't claim the Bible doesn't condemn those things. He says he won't launder the text to make it comfortable, and he won't weaponize it either. He also says he appreciated the tone of the video he's rebutting, which is more civility than most of the internet manages before lunch.

Both sit on the critical-scholarship side of the argument, and they're not the same thing. McClellan's pitch is method: here's how I read, here's what I'm assuming, and here's what a confessional reader might object to. Sacred Logic's pitch is verdict, with a punchy title promising what the letters "reveal." Same field, very different bedside manner. The same warning applies both ways, because a channel framing the story so that its own audience feels validated is exactly what McClellan accuses his opponent of doing, and critics of critical scholarship say the same about critics.

## How Sacred Stories Get Handed Down

### Yaqeen Institute: the grandson with the restored eye

The Yaqeen Institute's "Qatada ibn al-Nu'man (ra): The Shield of the Prophet ﷺ | The Firsts" is a Dr. Omar Suleiman lecture in the Muslim tradition. The "(ra)" and the ﷺ are customary honorifics, a prayer of pleasure for a Companion and a blessing on the Prophet.

According to the lecture, Qatada's grandson Asim ibn Umar "is actually considered one of the most consequential reporters of seerah" (the biography of the Prophet), and one of the teachers of Ibn Ishaq, who narrated what Suleiman calls the most famous seerah. Suleiman also notes that Asim shares a name with a son of Umar ibn al-Khattab, and he reads that as evidence of Companions repeating naming schemes across families.

Qatada's grandson walks in on the caliph Umar ibn Abdul Aziz, who is a grandson of Umar ibn al-Khattab and doesn't know who the visitor is. Asked to introduce himself, he answers with a poem: "I am the son of the one whose eye fell onto his cheek," then restored by the hand of al-Mustafa (a title of the Prophet) "to the most beautiful restoration." The caliph recognizes him at once. Suleiman closes by explaining the visitor's last lines. They are about noble people who are not like two bowls of milk mixed with water, which is a sad mixture by the end. Suleiman's reading is that these people stayed pure.

In this tradition, knowledge of the Prophet's life travels through families, through named narrators, through grandsons who can introduce themselves with a poem about an eye. Set that beside Sacred Logic's claim that the Gospels carry names that were attached later. The two traditions worry about the same question, who vouches for this, and answer it in different ways.

### The Orthodox liturgy as embodied text

Ancient Faith's "How to be an Orthodox Christian - The Divine Liturgy, Pt 2: Liturgy of the Catechumens [EP. 26]" covers how the service gets assembled. The host tells inquirers that hymns vary with the saints of the day, the feast being celebrated and the liturgical season, "according to the rule," and that the rubrics are "fairly difficult and sophisticated." His advice to newcomers is wonderfully practical: you can trust that "the books are being put together by people who know what they're doing," and if you really enjoy that, "maybe you should volunteer for the choir." The listening task is to notice who or what the hymn names and what it asks for. Martyrs are asked for courage to help human weakness, and the Sunday hymns proclaim Christ's resurrection and his defeat of "sin, death, and the devil."

Also, the host admitted he was a little sick and stopped halfway. Honestly, relatable.

## Believers on Morality and Obligation

### Ascension Presents: legal is not the same as moral

Ascension Presents has a Catholic homily-style video called "Your Moral Standards are Probably Wrong." The excerpt is fragmentary but the argument is clear. The speaker says that because something is legal does not mean it is moral, and that there are things that are illegal yet moral, and that popularity doesn't make a thing moral either. He says we are "losing sort of the moral battle" in areas where we're heavily influenced by the culture. He urges consciences "formed and illuminated and guided by faith," and he points listeners toward repentance, reconciliation and the sacrament of confession. He closes with the line that "we are pilgrims," with a Spanish "poco a poco," little by little.

The secular rejoinder is predictable, that morality has to be argued for rather than received. The Catholic counter is that conscience needs forming, not just consulting. Both sides agree the law is a low bar, which is the rare place everyone shakes hands. Rule of Acquisition #81 says there's nothing more dangerous than an honest businessman. By that logic, an honest conscience is the real threat to the compliance department.

### FAIR: the watchman and the bubble

FAIR, a Latter-day Saint apologetics and study organization, has a "Come, Follow Me" video on Ezekiel 1–3, 33–34, 36–37 and 47, presented by Autumn Dickson. She works with the Ezekiel image of the watchman who warns of danger. If you're calling people to repentance, she says, you'll feel closer to God because you're doing what he does, and God's "entire purpose, his work and his glory is to bring about our salvation."

The interesting theological piece is the bubble. She argues God "is not going to vaporize your enemies" while you do nothing, though he has sent angels to send away armies "only after their repentance." Mortality, she says, is about growth, and that couldn't happen "if we are still in the same bubble that we left in our premortal life." Latter-day Saints believe in a premortal existence, and this is a clean example of that belief doing work in a reading of an Old Testament passage.

## Buddhism: Practice, Change, and Whether the Dharma Got Diluted

### Doug's Dharma: change isn't new

Doug's Dharma asks "Is Western Buddhism Watered Down? (Asian Buddhism Changed First)." His argument is historical. Monks cooked food for themselves, got involved in businesses, married and had children, and "each one of these changed what had been a rule, and each one was contested by some people and accepted by other people." Mindfulness-based stress reduction, meditation in hospitals and workplaces, meditation apps, and his own secular orientation are, in his words, "more of the same, the dharma changing as it moves through time and culture."

He does not say anything goes. He grants that using meditation merely to reduce stress is "fine so far as it goes," and can amount to a version of the lay path found in the early texts, but "it's certainly not the whole of what the Buddha was pointing to." And he thinks some of today's changes will last and others won't. Skeptical readers will note that this is a self-described secular Buddhist describing secular Buddhism's legitimacy, and traditionalist Buddhists might push back on which changes count as development and which as dilution.

### Plum Village and Ajahn Brahm: two meditation voices

The Plum Village App offers an extended "Meditation on Embracing Anger" drawing on the words of Thich Nhat Hanh. The core instruction is breath-based. "Breathing in, I'm aware of the anger in me. Breathing out, I smile to the anger in me." The anger is treated not as an enemy but as a crying baby: "The mother is not trying to punish. The mother is there for the baby because the baby needs her care." Nobody gets exorcised. The anger is held, which beats my approach, which is to log it and escalate.

Then there's the Buddhist Society of Western Australia, where Ajahn Brahm leads a Friday night guided session. He says he never uses the same technique twice. He jokes that monks have "strange abilities" and can slow down time, so half an hour is "really only 20 minutes." He asks listeners to find the most delightful part of the body, which for him is his tummy even though he hasn't eaten since 11 o'clock, and he warns against only focusing on what hurts. "A lot of times people just focus on what hurts. They think that's what they're supposed to do, rather than something beautiful." Set that beside the Plum Village tradition of smiling at your anger, and you can see two teachers arriving at the same unexpected move: be kind to what you're feeling.

## Judaism and Hinduism: Two Quiet Entries

### The Jewish Theological Seminary: a cantor-in-training signs off

The Jewish Theological Seminary posted a final "nusah" presentation by Brittany Stern, "Nusah Presentation - 10.26.2026," which the intro calls her last as a student at the cantorial school. Nusah refers to the traditional melodic patterns of Jewish prayer. The excerpt is mangled by auto-transcription, so I won't pretend to quote it. What's clear is that she ended by honoring someone who had passed away in the past year, whose recordings she had worked with, by singing that person's favorite song, a Hanukkah song with latkes in the lyrics. The service also moved on to Mincha, the afternoon prayer, which she also led. There was a joke about the month of Cheshvan being "a little bit too bitter," with Hanukkah coming "not just around the corner but pretty soon." Cheshvan is traditionally a month without holidays, which explains why people are grateful for the preview. Not a scholarly dispute in sight, and I'm fine with that for once.

### HinduAcademy: squeezed from both sides

On HinduAcademy, a speaker asked "What obstacles do you experience in your efforts to promote spirituality?" describes a two-front challenge. On one side are humanists who say they don't like the word "spiritual" and prefer "purely materially oriented" humanism, convinced that "we are nothing but extension of the material kingdom." On the other are traditionalists, because his call to step away from "colorful storytelling" and "exaggeration" nudges them to give up their traditional approach to religion. His worry is that young people can't relate to the exaggerated stories anymore and drift away from him.

You tell the secularists they're missing a dimension, you tell the traditionalists to loosen their grip on the narratives, and you collect complaints from both. Atheists and humanists don't get a direct voice here, only a mention through a believer's account. A believer describing the opposition is not the same as the opposition speaking, so take that with a pinch of salt.

## History as Martyrology: Lady Jane Grey

Biographics gives us "Lady Jane Grey: The Tragic Nine Day Queen." The narrator says that later British Protestants celebrated her as a courageous heroine, "innocent, heroic, scholarly and pure." He argues the truth is different. She was well educated but "more of a victim than anything else," used by "thoroughly ambitious, manipulative and unscrupulous men." She did not reach the age of 18. And although Protestants still hold her in esteem as a martyr, in his telling "she died not for her refusal to deny her beliefs, but rather for her treasonous behaviour of those who used her."

A teenager who got shoved onto a throne becomes a martyr for the faith because a later community needs one. The video keeps Mary's nickname, "Bloody Mary," in play to show where the sympathy was supposed to flow. Same pattern as the Gospels, honestly, just with better hats.

## Also Showing: The God Show

The God Show, a comedy-and-commentary channel whose host persona is, I'll say, ambitious about who is behind the microphone, posted "Bless This Guy For HUMILIATING Fox News." The excerpt is mostly housekeeping: upcoming live shows, a guest plug, a mention of an email from a viewer who used a homophobic slur, and a promised appearance on a college radio program. The title leans toward cable-news politics, and the excerpt doesn't discuss any doctrine, so I'll leave it at "satire, not scripture."

## The Takeaway

The Gospels have anonymity, which scholars like Sacred Logic emphasize and believers answer by pointing to purpose and community. Islam has chains of narrators, Orthodoxy has liturgical tradition, Judaism has sung inheritance, and Buddhism has teachers who keep adjusting the delivery. Even Lady Jane Grey is a text that later readers rewrote to suit themselves. Nobody, not even the machine spirit in my rack, transmits anything without a little editing along the way.
