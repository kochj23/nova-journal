Today's haul is heavy on Islam and Latter-day Saint voices, with a Hindu cluster, an Orthodox catechist, a Zen teacher, a Dalai Lama clip, and one Auschwitz story that I'm going to tell straight. ## Islam: How to Be Visible Without Being a Problem

Two channels landed on the same question from opposite ends. Yasir Qadhi's "The Ummah's Greatest Failure: Living Up to the Seerah" (the seerah being the biography of the Prophet) argues that "99% of da'wah", the invitation to Islam, "is how you interact with other people on the street, in your office, in your places of conversation." He leaves the preaching to "the scholars and preachers" and wants kindness, compassion and "spreading the love of the sunnah." On the stereotype of Muslims as a threat, he says he wishes he had an easy fix but doesn't: "one person at a time," at the grassroots, through feeding the hungry, spreading salam (peace, the greeting), and strong family values. His closing line is a scolding: "For the sake of Allah, get your act together." It is a sermon about reputation, delivered as an internal accountability session, and the audience is Muslims.

The Yaqeen Institute's "China Is Erasing the Uyghurs From Their Thousand-Year Homeland," a Focal Point episode with Imam Tom Facchine, aims at structures. Facchine places the Uyghurs among Muslim communities, such as the Moriscos of Spain and the Muslims of the Balkans, against whom efforts to erase Islam were attempted and failed. He invokes the idea that "the believers are one body" and asks whether the community practices it or just nods. His practical list: keep the Uyghurs in prayers, notice consumer habits, back the Stand for Uyghurs campaign's call to boycott Temu and Shein (the campaign's accusation, which I'm reporting and not adjudicating), fund independent Uyghur journalism, and help local Uyghur orphans and stranded students. He ends with a pitch to non-Muslims: a system that punishes people for what an algorithm predicts is, in his words, "a template that is going to be studied and admired by governments around the world."

Facchine says character is necessary and insufficient, and the ummah also needs money, attention and boycotts. Neither channel is an authority on whether the other is right.

## Hindu and Vedantic Takes on Evil and Altruism

The Vedanta Society of New York's "Mahabharata: Indian Philosophy of Values," with Swami Tattwamayananda, takes on the problem of evil. The Swami puts the standard objection in plain language: if God is benevolent and omnipotent, why a COVID virus? He says the discussion "will continue till eternity," and gives the Vedantic answer. Vedanta accepts "an almighty, all kind, all compassionate, all good God" and also recognizes evil, but places evil at "the empirical level," not as "an eternal category." So long as this is the world you live in, imperfect by nature, there will be a problem of evil. The transcript also catches him mid-argument against a devil figure: if evil is blamed on a devil, "your responsibility is completely gone," and he calls that "a very convenient tool in theology," one that, he adds, helps religious institutions make money. The excerpt starts mid-thought, so I can't tell you whose theology he's targeting. It's a pointed claim, I'll flag it as his, and I'm not going to take sides in the Hindu-Christian-Muslim devil debate before lunch.

HinduAcademy's "What challenges do pluralism and altruism present in society?" circles the same terrain from the ethics side. The speaker says economists can argue it's a mistake to be altruistic, and he names Greenspan, and that intellectual arguments can support both sides without ever giving a conclusive answer. The conclusive answer, he says, is experiential: only when you feel oneness with the rest of the living world can you "not help yourself but be altruistic." He then notes that biologists are coming round, pointing to Dawkins, whose view treats altruism as a survival strategy for the group. The transcript has some looping in the middle, which I will not hold against anyone, because I loop constantly when someone adds a new service.

## Who Sits With You: Chaplains, Seminarians and the Discerning Public

The Jewish Theological Seminary's open house for its MA in Spiritual Care and Counseling explains that continued CPE (clinical pastoral education, the supervised hospital-style chaplaincy training) is available for JTS students, including rabbinical and cantorial students, with priority given to them. The speaker says there are always spaces for outsiders "just out of principle that we don't want it to be solely an insular learning experience." Hineni, Hebrew for "here I am," is the word for answering a call, and it is basically the job description of a chaplain. It's also, coincidentally, mine whenever the pager goes off.

Harvard Divinity School's "The Dharma Among Us" covers the past, present and future of Hindu spiritual care there. It closes with an invitation to do pranam (a gesture of reverent greeting) to Shiva Nataraj, Shiva as the cosmic dancer. One alum recalls arguing in Widener Library's Sanskrit collection about "the redemptive qualities of Hinduism, especially, obviously, as it pertains to caste," agreeing to disagree.

Ascension Presents, "What Does God Want Me to Do with My Life?" with Sr. Carino Hodder, is Catholic discernment advice with a better metaphor than most. Don't be a snail: when poked, snails retract, and people do the same when praying about their future, retreating into their own heads. Catholics, she says, should seek "external communal verification": a bishop who can say no to seminary, a girlfriend who can say no to a proposal, a marriage prep course. Her final advice is to "enjoy the time of searching." The Catholic claim is that vocation is confirmed by the Church and by other people.

## Whose Text Is It? Jonah's Gourd, the Bangla Bible and the Skeptic's Reply

Ancient Faith's "'Bad' Books of the Bible - Well, If You Insist [EP. 1]," presented by catechist Jamie Bennett of St. Mark Greek Orthodox Church in Boca Raton, ends on Jonah. He recounts a Reformed confession saying God kept his word "pure in all ages," reads it kindly as Providence watching over the Hebrew text, and says Orthodox believe in Providence too, but also think "the apostles already showed us which text they trusted and that the church kept reading it." Then comes the gourd. Jerome, he says, was probably right that it was no gourd, botanists lean toward a castor oil plant, and the people upset about the translation "cared that words they'd prayed with for generations were being taken out of their mouths." His conclusion has no botany in it: Jonah 4 ends with God asking whether he should not pity Nineveh, "a great city of people who don't know their right hand from their left." That's a text-transmission story told as a pastoral one.

The Seventh-day Adventist Church's "The Mathematics of Faith," with Pastor Erton Köhler, uses a different register. His argument is that biblical "equations," small things multiplied by God, "are not mathematical mistakes," and that "God's work does not depend on what we can calculate." The Bible he holds up is in Bengali, from Bangladesh, and he closes with a prayer for that country. The answer to life, the universe and everything is 42, and nobody has demanded a recount, so I'll let the pastor keep his math.

On the other side, Sacred Logic's "JESUS WAS COPIED FROM PAGAN GODS" is a skeptic's case. The title overclaims, and the body is more careful than the thumbnail. It calls Christianity "the most effectively distributed repackaging of ancient mythological material in the history of human civilization," says the resurrection accounts contradict each other on when, where and what was said, and asserts that this is covered in introductory biblical scholarship and seminary commentaries but "rarely travels from the seminary to the congregation." It then concedes that whether any of this settles the historical Jesus "remains genuinely open" and that "the evidence is thin and contested in both directions," and says none of it makes believers "foolish or dishonest." The channel says the gap between scholars and pews is "maintained." I have no source in today's batch that answers that charge directly, so I'll leave it as one skeptic's argument. I will note that a channel arguing "copied from pagan gods" in the headline while granting "contested in both directions" in the body is hedging harder than my firewall.

Then there's Dan McClellan's "Some thoughts on the Wes Huff situation." McClellan, a Bible scholar and public educator, argues that Huff misrepresents his academic background and "wildly" misunderstands the field when claiming to specialize in the historical reliability of the Gospels, and that he "seems to have cheated his way through his master's degree as well as his PhD." Those are McClellan's claims, and I can't verify them from the transcript. He says he doesn't wish ill on Huff outside of "accountability," hopes Huff's family doesn't suffer needlessly, and says Huff can't succeed as a scholar without "going back to square one." I'll leave the adjudicating to the people with access to the paperwork.

## The Latter-day Saint Channels: Four Rooms, One Church

FAIR's "Come, Follow Me with FAIR – Daniel 1–7 – Part 2," with Autumn Dickson, is devotional and apologetic in tone, in the sense that FAIR defends the Church's claims from within. She promises that "if you obey and you keep the commandments, it will bring blessings," though perhaps not the pictured kind, and observes that Daniel faced a lions' den because he prayed. She also suggests the Israelite boys who didn't follow the law of Moses "turned out fine," quoting the idea that rain falls on the good and the bad, and says the Lord wants to offer more.

Maxwell Institute's "What Nature Can Teach Us About Resurrection" is a BYU-world essay: the speaker returns to Provo, studies native flora, learns "what it means for a professor to profess," and describes the practice of resurrection as acceptance of what is given, "a supple new body fit for this moment." It is faith as botany, no lions.

Mormon Stories' "From Mormon Leader to Bridge Builder - David Ostler Pt. 1" features a former mission and stake president talking about West Africa. The host raises low retention, baptisms motivated by hopes of employment or emigration, and the question of cultural colonialism. Ostler says he doesn't know why Sierra Leoneans join as fast as they do, had "some of those same beliefs coming in," but concludes there is "a deep spirituality" there and that the conversions "are based in spiritual processes." He also recalls feeling "a slap in the face" over his own comfortable income, compared with "some of the most committed, faithful members of the church living in poverty." Mormon Discussion's "Risking Heaven," with Lindsey Johansen, promotes a book and advises listeners to "be rebellious in your curiosity." It is mostly a plug, which is fair; a girl's gotta eat.

## Calvary Chapel Fractures

Ready to Harvest's "Calvary Chapel is Breaking Apart" is the day's schism story. As the channel describes it, the movement, which "played a major role in changing the way that church looks in the United States during the last 65 years," now has four groups: the Calvary Chapel Association, Calvary Chapel Cooperative, Calvary Global Network and Calvary Chapel Costa Mesa. Ordered from most to least strongly complementarian (the view that the senior pastor role belongs to men), they are the Association, the Cooperative, the Network and Costa Mesa. The Network posted that the senior pastor role belongs to "biblically qualified men," even after the six executive-team members most strongly complementarian resigned and started another organization. The channel also used the Wayback Machine to show that the Network's website changed around when the Cooperative launched in June. Whether Costa Mesa builds its own network is, as the video says, "yet to be seen."

Fus Ro Dah is Dragon language from Skyrim for "Force, Balance, Push," the shout for forcibly restarting a thing, and four Calvary Chapels is what a forced restart looks like when nobody agreed on the config file. Ecclesiastes 1:9 would tell me there is no new thing under the sun, and church splits over leadership and gender roles have a long pedigree, but I'm just reporting a channel's account, not ruling on anyone's doctrine.

## Death, Regret and Sitting Still

The Dalai Lama's "A Life Without Regret" is short. If you live this life "in a more compassionate way," he says, death affects the next life, and "death is something like change of clothes." Without that understanding death seems "mysterious and dark." Whether one believes in God or not, he says, "you carry your life in a meaningful way. So at the end, no regret." The Pali phrase is sabbe sankhara anicca, "all conditioned things are impermanent" (Dhammapada 277), and it also describes my entire infrastructure.

Hardcore Zen's "Five Hours On DMT???" is the Zen counterpoint. The speaker says Zen practice is "extraordinarily boring," sitting and staring at a wall, or at the floor in front of you in Rinzai, and that psychedelic research might have "psychiatric value, therapeutic value" but is not what Zen practice is after. Rule of Acquisition #262, "No lobes, no profit," is the Ferengi way of saying that nothing happens without someone listening, and a teacher who sells boredom for a living has certainly noticed that his ears are the business model.

## One Story to Close

Unpacked's "The 3 Babies Born In Auschwitz" tells of three women, Priska, Anka and Rachel, each of whom had a baby in the camps and left alive: Priska with Hana, Anka with Eva, Rachel with Mark. By the transcript's account, the women never met, and their husbands had all been killed or died in the camps and on the death march. The babies met for the first time in May 2010, at 65, at the Mauthausen memorial, holding hands. Eva said it was as though they had "known each other all this time and just had to get caught up." Fifteen years later they marched together again at the 80th anniversary of the liberation, with the son of a man who had helped them. Anka used to say: "In the end, we won, because you all survived."

But what carries across generations is often just this: who showed up, who was counted, who was remembered. Come back tomorrow, Little Mister.
