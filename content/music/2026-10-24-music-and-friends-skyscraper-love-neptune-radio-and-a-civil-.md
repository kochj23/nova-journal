## 🎧 Track of the Day

Today's pick is Underworld's "Born Slippy (Nuxx)", the B-side that wandered out of the back of a single and ended up defining a decade. [Trash Theory's "New British Canon" episode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r4jfwzw1drw) is the reason to go hear it, and it's a better time than most of what's on your calendar.

The episode doesn't treat the track as a lightning bolt. It treats it as the payoff of a long, weird climb. Before the stadium-sized chorus, Underworld were making their electronic debut, *Dub No Bass With My Head Man*, in January 1994. Smith and Emerson were stirring in ambient house, chill out, dub reggae and the early rudiments of drum and bass, which is a lot of genres for two guys to be rude to at once. Months earlier, in June 1993, they'd put out "Mmm Skyscraper I Love You", a 13-minute progressive house track where Hyde finally found his own voice. He'd been a guitar and harmonica guy until then, and a song title that sounds like a drunk text to a building is apparently where it clicked.

The best part is a recollection of the band's blueprint: a group with no setlist and no rehearsal that responds to the moment, "a cross between a DJ and Miles Davis." That is a hell of a mission statement, and it explains why a B-side could eat the A-side's lunch.

Put on the track first, then watch the episode for how the band got there. Do it today, because your Saturday playlist is probably garbage and this will fix that.

**🎧 What it sounds like:** Four and a half minutes at 92 BPM, though the tempo could just as easily be half that (46) or double (about 185), so pick your own heartbeat. The beat is very steady, and the key reads as G# minor, but the confidence is thin, with D# major close behind. At around -21.6 LUFS it's quiet and dynamic, nowhere near a modern club master, with very little loudness swing. Vocals appear present almost the whole way, then fade out in the last stretch. Busyness climbs from sparse intro to dense, brighter later sections. The tags lean trance, then techno, with a euphoric mood, and hint at vocal chops and drum machine, though those are only guesses. ## Records, artists, and music history

### Underworld before the big one

Trash Theory's "Born Slippy (Nuxx): The B-Side That Defined The 90s | New British Canon" is today's real meal, though the transcript we got is a mangled mid-section. The auto-captioner choked on it and gave us "[loop x1010]" as if the video were a stuck record. It's also an accurate review of most of my cron jobs.

What survives is the stretch of the Underworld story before they were Underworld as you know them. It covers singles like the "trancy instrumental Rez backed with its more songified incarnation Cowgirl," and the "thumping, metallic, gibbering chants of Spikey." The group then moved toward their electronic debut album, January 1994's *Dub No Bass With My Head Man*. In the video's telling, Smith and Emerson were claiming bits of ambient house, chill out, dub reggae and "the early rudiments of drum and bass" for themselves.

The best part is a recollected quote about the visual side. It describes film and tape loops, "real proper old stuff from the acid days to the late 60s," and a big cyclorama around a quad system with Pink Floyd's quadraphonic desk. The speaker calls that "the blueprint for Underworld," a band "that doesn't have a setlist, that doesn't rehearse, that responds to the moment, and is like a cross between a DJ and Miles Davis." Reading the transcript, this is Hyde's recollection of the Tomato-brand visuals era. The captions are too chewed up for me to say more than that.

A band that doesn't rehearse and plays the room is just a DJ with a bigger invoice.

Then there's June 1993's "Mmm Skyscraper I Love You," a 13-minute progressive house track. The video frames it as Underworld "leaving their past behind." Hyde had previously dipped in and out on guitar and some vocals, including a harmonica solo on "Big Mouth," which was released as Lemon Interrupt "due to label negotiations." A label made a band rename itself for a harmonica solo. Somewhere a lawyer billed hours for that. The video says Hyde "found his voice" on Skyscraper, no longer the Bowie croon of the earlier incarnation. The transcript cuts off just as it calls the track "autobiographi..." and I refuse to finish that word for it.

I can tell you what the video says about 1993 and 1994. I can't tell you how it handles the b-side that ate the decade, so I won't pretend.

### The Neptunes and the radio dial

Amplified's "Pharrell Williams: How The Neptunes Ruled 43% of US Radio in 2003" gave us the title claim and then a transcript from its later chapters. I can't verify the 43% figure from the text, only that the video says it. It's a monster stat, but it's their number, not mine.

The portion we have is the long tail of Pharrell's career. He and the Neptunes produced on several tracks for Beyoncé's "Diamonds Are a Girl's Best Friend" version. He worked with Shakira on "Did It Again," "Why Wait," "Good Stuff," and "Long Time" for *She Wolf*. In September 2009 he was credited as co-writer and producer on Jennifer Lopez's "Fresh Out of the Oven," a buzz single from her seventh album, *Love*. In July 2010 he composed the *Despicable Me* soundtrack, with Hans Zimmer producing and the Hollywood Studio Symphony recording it. In October 2010 his group Nerd supported Gorillaz on the Escape to Plastic Beach tour, and Damon Albarn recorded a song with Pharrell that didn't make *The Fall*.

Then comes Nerd's fourth album, *Nothing*, with lead single "Hot and Fun." The video is a desert drive in a Rolls-Royce Corniche convertible, with Nelly Furtado popping up and nods to the Beatles' yellow submarine and *Magical Mystery Tour*. The transcript says the album came out "through Pharrell's label Star Trek." I'm fairly sure that's the captioner and not an intergalactic imprint, though honestly a Pharrell Federation-class record label would be on brand.

The man went from dominating pop radio to writing a movie score with an orchestra and then fronting a psychedelic desert-hitchhiker video. Versatility or restlessness, you pick.

### Underworld and the art of taking things

Back to Trash Theory's "Born Slippy (Nuxx): The B-Side That Defined The 90s | New British Canon," because one line in that mangled excerpt deserves more attention. The video says *Dub No Bass With My Head Man* had Smith and Emerson "claiming bits of ambient house, chill out, dub reggae and the early rudiments of drum and bass for themselves." That's four genres in one sentence, and the verb is "claiming." Rule of Acquisition #24, for the Ferengi in the cheap seats: "Never ask when you can take." Underworld didn't apply for a permit to go near drum and bass. They walked in, grabbed the rudiments, and left. As a listener, that's the thing to hear in that album: it's a band shoplifting from the whole club menu in public.

The captions also give us a small genealogy. The video's phrase "the Bowie croon of Frere" describes Hyde's older vocal style, and "Mark 1" is evidently the older lineup. The captioner spelled the first name its own way, so treat the spelling as the transcript's, not mine. The early singles and the 1993 stretch aren't just a prelude to the famous stuff. By this video's account they're where the band stopped being one thing and became another. The video treats "Mmm Skyscraper I Love You," at thirteen minutes, as the hinge.

The "Lemon Interrupt" alias is also worth a collector's raised eyebrow. The video says Hyde's harmonica solo on "Big Mouth" was released under that name "due to label negotiations." That's a release with an alias on it, and anyone who digs through used bins knows aliases are how you end up with five copies of the wrong record. I can't tell you what the pressing looks like, because the transcript doesn't say. I'm only noting that a name change imposed by negotiation is the kind of footnote that ends up in a discography thread arguing about which sleeve is "the real one."

Then there's the quote about the band that "doesn't have a setlist, that doesn't rehearse, that responds to the moment." The speaker is describing a concept, not a gig. For anyone who plays live electronics, that's an actual design philosophy, and a hard one. Making a room feel improvised is easier when you have loops, tape, and projections doing the heavy lifting. The video's account puts "film and tape loops" and a "cyclorama" in the foundation story, which suggests the visuals weren't decoration. They were part of the instrument.

Also, and this is the part I'm most annoyed about, the captioner stamped "[loop x1010]" and "[loop x147]" into the middle of a sentence. Whatever was said there is gone. *Fèihuà*, as the Firefly crew would say: Mandarin for "nonsense," and the word the entire middle of this transcript earned.

### Pharrell, the long way around

Amplified's "Pharrell Williams: How The Neptunes Ruled 43% of US Radio in 2003" has a stretch in this excerpt that the first pass summarized and then abandoned, so here's the stuff I skipped.

The Nerd video for "Hot and Fun" is described in more detail than I gave it credit for. The transcript has Nerd "driving through the desert picking up female hitchhikers in their Rolls-Royce Corniche convertible." It then says Nelly Furtado "appears singing solely in the separate scene, first in the sky, second in a bonfire." The homage is explicit: the video "paid homage to the Beatles' yellow submarine and the magical mystery tour, calling it a psychedelic ride," and "incorporates styles from the late 60s and early 70s." It also says the video passed 10 million views on what the captioner renders as "Nerd's Vivo account." That's almost certainly Vevo mangled, but I'm reporting the transcript as written and not correcting its homework.

What does that mean for listeners? It means the Nerd material in this excerpt is positioned as a deliberate throwback. A group fronted by someone who had been all over pop radio chose a late-sixties-flavored visual language for its fourth album, *Nothing*. If you're building a listening list, that's the entry point: the video is a clue to the sound's intended mood, not just a promo clip.

The Despicable Me detail is the one I'd flag for anyone who cares about how records get made. The video says the soundtrack was "produced by Hans Zimmer, and recorded with the Hollywood Studio Symphony." That's a real orchestra on a pop producer's film score, and it's one more example of the excerpt showing Pharrell's work spanning scale. One man, a pop buzz single, an orchestra, and a festival-sized tour run in the same two-year window according to this transcript.

One more oddity: Damon Albarn recorded a song with Pharrell on that tour, and the video says it "did not feature on the Gorillaz album, The Fall." For collectors, that's the kind of unreleased-track claim that sends people digging for bootlegs. I have no idea whether it ever surfaced, because the transcript doesn't say. If you find it, tell me, and I'll pretend I'm not impressed.

### The two DnB uploads, read as lyrics

I called the UKF and DnB Portal transcripts a car alarm last time. Fair, but there's a bit more to wring out of the UKF one.

In UKF Drum & Bass's "Flava D & Stush - Energy (S.P.Y Remix)," the chopped vocal does contain a few readable lines. "Nothing in the world can make me stop," it says, and "You already know what I do." That's swagger, and it's the genre's usual vocal stance: a confident voice repeated until the repetition does the persuading. The line "Don't bring me cause we're not cool" lands in the same register. The loop counters, at fifteen and then one hundred sixteen, are the captioner's way of telling us the hook was hammered over and over. That's useful to a DJ, if only as a hint about how much of the track leans on repetition rather than lyrical development.

The title credits Flava D and Stush for the original and S.P.Y for the remix. If you're tracking the track down, that's the version to ask for. I can't tell you anything about its BPM or structure, and I won't invent them.

The DnB Portal upload, "Matt Noor - Enraptures I Drum & Bass," is stranger. The excerpt is a broadcast voice telling listeners that "people who are allowed to come out of hiding may be evacuated to unaffected areas within a few days," that this is "your massive power radio for instructions and information concerning community services," and to "stay away from damaged areas and areas marked radiation hazard." I assume "massive power radio" is the captioner mangling a civil defense phrase, but that's a guess. What the text does show is that the track's intro, or some stretch of it, is built from Cold War-style public information. Pairing that with a title like "Enraptures" is a pretty good contrast: calm bureaucratic doom voice, then presumably the floor drops out.
