## 🎧 Track of the Day

Elton John, "Philadelphia Freedom," on *Soul Train*, as watched by the hosts of [Airplay Beats](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSuHGNMCOUY). They'd never seen this footage and didn't know Elton had ever set foot in that building. Their reaction is basically "hold on, is that Soul Train?" followed by a full-body surrender. Fair enough, because the man in the glasses and the sequins got dropped into the most rhythmically demanding room in television and came out grooving.

The reactors clock the flute first, calling it "slippery," slipping and sliding through the mix, and they're right: it's a goddamn flute holding its own on a dance floor. Then they get distracted by the people right behind Elton. A guy in a white sweater and a woman in a yellow shirt are dancing so hard the hosts admit they can't stop staring. Their verdict on the song itself is "that is a dance track," said three times, because once wasn't enough to cover the damage. They say they didn't know Elton could get that busy, and that the longer it ran, the better it got.

Why today? It's a few minutes of a pop star finding out he's secretly a soul act, and a couple of strangers discovering it live, with the table shaking. I'd call it heartwarming, but I have a reputation, so I'll just say the flute slaps.

## Records, Artists and Music History

### Amplified makes the case for Van der Graaf Generator, sort of

Amplified's "Van der Graaf Generator: Darkness, Double Saxes and A Plague of Lighthouse Keepers" is the kind of video title that tells you everything about a band before you've heard a note. Double saxes, darkness, lighthouse keepers, plague. It sounds like a maritime disaster written by a bored English teacher, and I mean that as the highest possible compliment.

The excerpt I've got starts mid-thought, with the host conceding that none of the comeback material beats the classic first phase of their career. His verdict is that it's "a worthy comeback," "not a pointless comeback," but "certainly not the be-all and end-all." That's about as warm as a critic gets without a restraining order.

They're "rated by those who know and understand them," underrated by people who've never heard them "because they haven't heard them," and overrated by anyone who slaps a "mark of genius" on them. That is the most airtight non-argument I've heard all week, and I respect it. Had they been briefly successful, he says, they'd have gone back to obscurity. As it was, they stayed a "powerful cult influence" on other bands, probably more influential to musicians than they ever were to the public. His best line is that their unique impact came from the fact that "no one realised what they were doing, least of all themselves."

I too have no idea what I'm doing, and a surprising number of people are impressed.

He also notes their great works are now more than 30 years away, "but it doesn't sound like it belongs there," and he calls the time "ripe for reappraisal." Then comes a detour I did not see coming. In the middle of all the darkness, the band drops a joke, a bit of cheesy dancing music, which he calls "very expertly done," funny, and somehow also poignant to the story.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #76: "Every once in a while, declare peace. It confuses the hell out of your enemies." Van der Graaf Generator apparently ran the musical version, which is to be relentlessly grim for years and then pull a cheesy dance number out of their ass. Nobody knew what to do with it, including, probably, the band.

### Airplay Beats finds Elton on Soul Train

The Airplay Beats reaction to Elton John's "Philadelphia Freedom" is a different kind of rediscovery, and the contrast with the Van der Graaf piece is funny. Airplay Beats is someone getting blindsided by a clip he didn't know existed. "I'm tripping that Elton's on Soul Train," he says, and then, "I never knew he stepped foot in that building. I've never seen that footage at all."

His review is mostly about the room. The flute is "slippery," "slipping and sliding on through there," and the production is "crazy." But he can't stop watching the dancers behind Elton, specifically a guy in a white sweater who was "grooving" and a woman in a yellow shirt "going off too." His theory is that they knew who to put on camera, which is correct, because that's the entire job of a camera operator on a dance show.

He also drops the best bit of dance-history criticism I've heard in a while. Dancing "was completely different back then," and "I know these people's hips ain't together these days." Their hips are "toe up." I can't verify the orthopedic claim, but I'm not about to argue with him.

The verdict is that it's "a dance track right there," that he didn't know Elton could "get busy like that," and that his knees were bouncing hard enough to almost bump the table. "He got all the soul," he says, which is why he shouldn't be surprised. As Nadsat has it, the whole thing is horrorshow, Burgess's Russian-laced teen slang for "good," and a clip like this deserves it.

Airplay Beats got one by accident, from a flute and a dude in a sweater. One of these routes is more fun, and it isn't the one with the footnotes.

## Live Sessions

### KEXP puts Iguana Death Cult on the couch

The KEXP session, "Iguana Death Cult - Full Performance (Live on KEXP)," gives me only the interview tail, so I can't tell you a damn thing about how the set sounded. The interviewer runs through the résumé: bills with Jack White and Frankie and the Witch Fingers, plus festivals like South by Southwest, Levitation and Desert Days. Then comes the question every touring band hates, which is where you haven't played yet.

Nashville is the U.S. pick for one member. Brazil is the other, with the immortal line, "let's put it out there in the universe so that it happens." Anything crazy on tour? "We've been good boys. For the most part, yeah. I'll leave that to the imagination." That's the most suspicious sentence of the day, and I'm including the one about the hips.

The real mystery is something that looked "like an old German fire truck." One of them recalls showing up to their single release "with the sirens going and everything," and then, "we had to get rid of it." I need to know what that is. The excerpt doesn't say, I'm not going to guess, and I'm furious. Oel ngati kameie, as the Na'vi say, "I see you," and what I see is a band that owned a fire truck and a transcript that cuts away before explaining it.

They're a nonprofit arts organization, and the sessions exist because of individual donor support. Public radio pays for a lot of the live music you watch for free, so consider that your moral spinach.

### Charleston White on djvlad, and the fake-account problem

The djvlad interview with Charleston White is a long, rambling conversation that touches several heavy subjects I'm not going to retell, and almost none of it is about music. The excerpt wanders through fame, strangers online, and being blamed for other people's behavior. It's a rap-adjacent media sit-down with the rap mostly absent.

The one bit with a real industry angle is the impersonation problem. He says he stays off Facebook because "I let them have a fake Charleston over there," and that people have told him they sent $5,000 for an interview to a booking address that wasn't his or the channel's. "Make what right? I didn't get anything out of this." He says he told them to call the police.

I can't verify any of it, and I'm repeating it only as his claim. If a booking email lives at a free webmail address, that's not a booking office. It's a guy with a laptop and a dream. Pay-to-play in music was already sleazy, and now there's a counterfeit version of the sleaze.

The host's reappraisal pitch is hedged more carefully than the first pass let on. He says "for me" that the great works are "30 years plus away," so it's a personal pick, not a ruling. He never tells us which records he means. The excerpt also teases a joke that lands after "tune right down the middle," then "this extraordinary thing." Neither the tune nor the joke gets a title in the text I have. I can't tell you whether it's a song, a section or a whole record, and I'm not going to invent one.

He says none of the comeback material is better than "the classic material from the first phase of their career," and he says "everyone should hear" the band. ### Airplay Beats: the song is a build

What it skipped is how the reactor describes the track's shape. He says "that one gets going," then "that one gets to moving," and finally "the more it kept going, it just got better and better." That's a groove that earns its keep over time. He admits it was "hard to sit through," which at a table means sitting still was the hard part. He calls the production "crazy" and the flute "slippery," and that's the whole arrangement review. He doesn't mention the lyrics or the songwriting at all.

He also name-drops someone called Thelma twice, "the way Thelma used to come out doing her stuff." The excerpt never says who she is, and I refuse to guess. His last words are "And the song, thoug," which is a sentence with its pants around its ankles. Whatever caveat he was about to deliver, the transcript cut the power like the worst DJ in Burbank.

### djvlad: two complaints, one theme

The title of djvlad's Charleston White interview lists an arrest, an eviction, an allegation, Jay-Z and Yella Beezy. The excerpt I have touches none of that. What it does have is a pair of stories about other people's behavior landing on his doorstep.

First, the audience. He says "all of my likes and shares is from strangers" and "I don't see nobody familiar." Then comes the impersonation, the "fake Charleston" on Facebook. The booking address he describes is the channel's name plus "bookings" at a free webmail domain. That's gǒu shǐ, dog crap, with a logo on it. Somebody out there runs a Charleston White tribute act with a gmail account and no conscience. 😏 Straight facts: he says people sent $5,000 for interviews that never happened, and that he had nothing to do with it. That's his account, which I can't verify. But the pattern of a trusted brand name plus "bookings" at a free mail service is worth recognizing. As Ecclesiastes 1:9 (KJV) puts it, "there is no new thing under the sun," and that includes the grift.

Second, a story from a venue called Taboo after a Fresh and Fit podcast appearance. He says a woman from the group he went with left drunk and hit "two or three cars." Then, he says, the people affected came to him for payment, even though he says he'd only just met her at the podcast. He asks the man who called about the damage whether he has insurance, and the excerpt cuts off at "they're trying to shake me down. I ain't payi." He also invokes Tupac, but the captions turn that into soup, so I'm skipping it. It's a one-sided account, and I'm treating it as one. Ori'haat, in Mando'a: this part is not a joke. A drunk driver hitting three cars is somebody's bad night and somebody's insurance premium.
