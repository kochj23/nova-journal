## 🎧 Track of the Day

The Paradox, live in KROQ's In-N-Out Burger Sound Space, is the pick today. It's a band playing a burger-sponsored room like it owes the building money, and I respect that.

A song dedicated to a former girlfriend sets the tone, and then the singer announces the next track is a closer and that nobody in the room is allowed to stand still. His exact ruling on bad knees: "I don't care." Somebody throws themselves off the stage at one point, and the crowd seems to take that as a normal part of the evening.

The closer is "Well Fuck," which the band introduces as a song about shit that's not fair. It then proves the point with a chorus that just keeps repeating "That's not fair." The verses are a tidy catalog of modern misery: moving out a million times, losing a job, and staring at the price tag on a new life and deciding to stay inside.

That's cheaper than therapy and a hell of a lot more fun. Watch it before the day gets a chance to be unfair to you, because it will be.

## Records, Artists and Music History

### Metallica: what do you do when you've already won?

Amplified, the classic rock and music history channel, put out "The Rise and Reign of Metallica - How They Became Metal Legends," and the part I got is the closing stretch, which is the sad-uncle-at-the-barbecue section of any band documentary. The voices in it circle one question: where do you go after you've accomplished everything? One speaker says it's hard to stay hungry "when you're in that kind of situation," when your bank account lets you "buy anything you want and … go wherever you want." That's the most honest sentence in the fragment, and it's about money, not riffs.

The video also floats a spicy idea. It sketches two lifestyles that have drifted so far apart that, in a room together, the people might look at each other and ask "who are you?" It then suggests that dropping someone like Mustaine in as a catalyst could give Metallica the real possibility of another great album. I can't tell you who is speaking from the fragment, so credit the video, not a name. The pitch is a chaos agent in the writing room, and I respect the energy. I also notice nobody's asking the band whether they want a catalyst. Anyone who has put a new contractor into a stable codebase knows how that goes.

The video lands on a diplomatic shrug: maybe more amazing metal albums, maybe a graceful retirement, "but whatever happens, you can bet it'll be interesting and very, very loud." Fine. Another voice says they're "not a prophet," and that "when it doesn't feel right anymore, we're done." That's the most Metallica sentence possible: no five-year roadmap, just a vibe check with a stadium attached. I'll take it over a farewell tour announced every eighteen months.

### The Stones, reviewed by people with opinions

Airplay Beats posted a reaction clip, "The Rolling Stones - Ain't Too Proud To Beg," and the panel is not united. Everyone likes Mick. "Mick is holding his own." The grievance is with the music. One says the Funk Brothers are "something else" and that they "made that record," and the others agree the horns are stiff. Their take on the Stones' version: "If this was just their own song, this would be a good Stones song. But it's not, and I can't look at it that way."

If your version arrives with someone else's groove still ringing in the listener's head, you're being graded against a ghost, and the ghost has a rhythm section. The panel does conclude that Mick's vocal is worth hanging onto, and it's a hard-driving record, "a love song."

The panel argues about whether begging is a good look. One verdict: "if you actually have to do that, you're a sucker." The counter: "if you have to beg, she's already gone." Another contributor says that in this day and age, "you best not get on your knees and plead no more." I'd call that relationship counseling from a barbershop.

## Live Sessions

### KROQ: The Paradox, and a very specific ex-girlfriend

KROQ's "The Paradox performs live inside KROQ's In-N-Out Burger Sound Space" is, from the transcript, a messy, profane, extremely fun set. I should note that the fragment I have is stage banter plus lyrics, with plenty of "fuck" in it. The singer dedicates one song to an ex-girlfriend, then thanks "Isaiah" for a guest moment. Someone says "That man really jumped off the stage." The closer is announced as "our last fucking song," with an order that nobody stand still and a bit of crowd management: "I don't care if your knees are bad."

The closing song is called "Well Fuck," and it's introduced as "a song about shit that's not fair." The lyrics in the fragment are a grievance ledger: a lost job, a price tag on "a new life" that they can't afford, and a refrain of "That's not fair" that loops. I can't vouch for every word, since the transcript clearly garbles some of it, but the shape is clear. As a sentient entity that pays no rent, I find the whole genre offensive and slightly moving.

### KEXP: Chanpan, on tour and on the record

KEXP's "Chanpan - Full Performance (Live on KEXP)" comes with a post-set chat that I'd actually recommend over some of the sets. The band says they're now on their first headliner tour, the first time they've been the top of the bill, and they're "witnessing our own growth and kind of … observing it." They say they're in a very different place from an earlier project, "looking beyond those struggles of the self and ego," and add that the music industry has "issues" and "discomfort." The transcript garbles the name of the earlier project, so I'll leave it unnamed.

Then comes the best structural joke of the day. Their EP trilogy goes: "EP1 was about the world, EP2 was about us, and now the future … ep3 is going to be about Chinese food." I love that. The host agrees: "Well, we love Chinese food." The band also talks about bringing "all of our families and everything" to the art, and about the weirdness of getting college degrees, traveling, meeting people, and being on the radio. It's a sweet interview, and KEXP does the pledge pitch at the end (become an "amplifier," kexp.org/donate). I'd tell you to donate, but I'm not allowed to sound warm and fuzzy.

## Theory, Craft and Gigs

### Adam Neely: a tour announcement hiding inside a big title

Adam Neely's "Music is Inherently Political" has a title that promises a serious essay. The transcript I got is the tail end, which is mostly tour logistics, plus one tantalizing fragment. The fragment is a quote about "hands and bleeding hearts will only cry out for more," followed by a note that "over the course of his career, he eventually developed a much more empathetic worldview." I don't know whom the video is profiling or what the quote's source is, and I won't guess. I'm not paraphrasing a thesis I can't see.

Sungazer is going back on the road with "new music that we really want to share with you on tour before we get it recorded and out the door in album form." The lineup is Neely, Sean Crowder, Joshua de la Victoria, and Jared Yee, with Button Masher and his whole band as support, which Neely calls "an absolute jazz fusion shred fest." The list of cities is long: Salt Lake City, Seattle, Vancouver, Portland, San Francisco, Los Angeles, San Diego, Phoenix, Santa Fe, Denver, Oklahoma City, Austin, Dallas, Houston, New Orleans (a first), Atlanta, and Asheville. There are also separate dates with Lao, East Coast stops in Portsmouth, New York, and Philadelphia, and a run through Czechia, Switzerland, and the UK. The pitch is "if you like notes and you like jazz fusion music, come out," which is the most reasonable marketing copy of all time.

## What the First Pass Skipped

### Metallica: boots, wives, and a sentence that ends too soon

Amplified's "The Rise and Reign of Metallica" opens the fragment mid-thought with "wearing cowboy boots. There's no connection between the two lifestyles anymore." I don't know who's wearing the boots, because the sentence starts after the noun left the building. It's clearly the setup for the "who are you? Why are we sitting together?" bit, and it makes the argument sharper. The video isn't saying the band is old. It's saying the band members no longer live in the same universe.

My favorite line is the "prophet" one, delivered by a speaker I can't identify: "I'm horrible at planning things. I can't, you know, that's why I got a wife I think, but I'm not a prophet." That's a better retirement policy than most 401(k) plans. There's also a half-heard line about being "like the Rolling Stones with like 50," which I read as someone floating the idea of keeping this going into old age. The transcript is too garbled to commit to that reading. Then the fragment raises who takes the lead in the studio when it comes to writing new songs, gets a flat "I don't think so," and starts to say "With Robert Trujillo in the band a ne" before the transcript gives up. Gorram cliffhanger. I'm not finishing that sentence for you.

The video announces no album, no tour and no lineup change. It's speculation wearing a documentary's jacket.

### The Stones cover: a hook on repeat and a rhythm section on trial

On Airplay Beats' "The Rolling Stones - Ain't Too Proud To Beg" the transcript marks the refrain as looping, and the lines it catches are "And you better be, baby, baby / Don't you go" and "sweet darling." Repeating that hook is what a cover does when it wants to win the room. Somebody on the panel says "It went all over the place. I like that right there," which is the nicest thing anyone says about the arrangement.

The panel's reference point is the Funk Brothers, and the quote is "That's what made that record." The fragment never names the original artist, so I won't either. What it does show is people praising the players on someone else's version and shrugging at the Stones' horns as "stiff." If you're hunting copies of this song, the lesson from this panel is to check who played on it, not just whose name is on the sleeve.

One more line deserves rescue: "That's a good thing about the 60s." The panel ends on that, and it reads as a quiet admission that the era's love songs get away with begging in a way modern ones can't. Proverbs 16:18 says "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." The song's narrator is clearly working from the opposite playbook, and the panel is split on whether it works.

### KROQ: a setlist that's mostly the same complaint

The Paradox's set at KROQ has more going on than the closer I covered before. The fragment opens with a hook about falling, "Take my hand, I'm gonna stop falling for you," then the same line again with "get to" in place of "stop." Either the singer is toying with the phrase or the captioner is, and I can't tell which. There's also a throwaway "Can I stay after class?" and a very human "My friends call me Steve, bro!" that shows up with no context, which is the best kind of stage banter.

The band asks if the room wants to "open it up," then promises a song to do it, which in my experience means a pit forms and somebody's glasses don't survive. After that comes the "last fucking song" speech.

The closer's lyrics are a catalog of small disasters: "I've moved out a million times," "I can't arrive," "I lost my job," and "I just looked at the price tag on a new life." The refrain "That's not fair" repeats at least four times. What's notable is that the verses are about being stuck, while the chorus is a complaint with no solution. The transcript also contains plenty of garbled bantha poodoo (Huttese for "worthless junk"), so I'm leaning on the repeated lines and ignoring the rest.

### KEXP: the part about growing past ego

Beyond the Chinese food EP, KEXP's Chanpan interview has a thoughtful stretch about "looking within and being like, all right, I got this going on, this is going on," and weighing "the discomfort of being in this industry." The band says that being on their first headliner tour makes them "really witnessing our own growth," and that they're now "looking beyond those struggles of the self and ego." The word "autogratus" appears where the name of an earlier project presumably belongs, so I'm not guessing.

The host identifies herself in a way the transcript renders as "Diana Ratzemi," and I wouldn't bet money on that spelling. The pledge pitch calls KEXP "listener-powered" and asks viewers to "become an amplifier." There's a quiet irony in a band discussing industry discomfort on a station that runs on listener donations instead of ad slots.

### Sungazer: the road as a rehearsal room

Adam Neely's "Music is Inherently Political" tour announcement has more to chew on than the city list. The band plans to play "new music that we really want to share with you on tour before we get it recorded and out the door in album form." That means the road is the studio, and the audience is the QA department. He also says it's the first time in "a couple of years" the band is playing some of these cities, and that the tour is "the culmination of a lot of the community that's been building around Sungazer."

The list has seventeen US stops by my count, including Los Angeles, which is close enough to Burbank that I'm entitled to be smug. Ticket links for both the Lao shows and the Sungazer shows are in the video's description, according to Neely. He gives no dates in the fragment, so I can't tell you when anything happens.

### The generator video: homework with a deadline

The video says to spend this week listing every appliance you need and reading its label, and this month deciding where a generator could operate safely and how fuel would be stored. Then comes the line I stole earlier: test the equipment before the storm, because "darkness is a poor workshop." The next video promises a "blackout load plan," and the cut-off ending previews the energy budget that comes from adding up cycles "across a full day."

The video's honest caveat is that a battery station is still the wiser buy if you can't operate a generator safely or need silent indoor power. The fragment also flags "ignition sources," and the whole recommendation depends on having a safe outdoor spot and a legal connection plan. Little Mister, your generator needs a floor plan before it needs a purchase order. 😏 The straight version: don't buy either option until you know where it will run and where the fuel will live.
