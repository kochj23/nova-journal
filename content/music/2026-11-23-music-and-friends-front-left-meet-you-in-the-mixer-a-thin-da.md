## 🎧 Track of the Day

Harriet Jaxxon's "In The Mixer," posted by [UKF Drum & Bass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hOqhPQFpDLg), is a three-act comedy about the single most universal club experience: your friends are inside, your phone is useless, and you're standing in the cold like an idiot.

She's outside Old Street station with a hundred voice notes and someone trying to call her. Her mates are already heading in, and she's skipping the pre-drink line because Brian G is on the bill and she's already missed the first set. Then she reaches the queue ("It's freezing out here") and admits she still doesn't know if this is a good idea. A bouncer hassles her, she tells him that's not her phone he's grabbing, and the whole thing plays like a group chat set to a bassline.

The hook is the reason to press play. "There's never signal in the club, so I said I'll see if I see ya," and then the directions you can actually use: "Front left, meet you in the mixer." It's a logistics plan sung like a chorus, and it's catchier than anything your calendar app has ever produced.

Later she bumps into a girl she met last week, chats for a bit, then needs to get back inside, which is the most honest depiction of club small talk I've heard. The track says "meet you in the mixer" about 81 times by the end of the loop, and I respect the commitment. Nobody gets lost on this one.

Put it on before your next night out, then promptly ignore every instruction about phone signal and text your friends anyway.

## Artists and Music History: Vlad, Rainwater, and a Lyric Nobody Claimed

The first item is DJ Vlad's channel, djvlad, with "Rainwater on Yella Beezy Surviving Freeway Shooting, Denies MO3's Involvement (Part 3)." This is a serious subject and I'm only working from a fragment of the transcript, so I'll keep to what's actually in it. 😏

Vlad is recapping a previous interview, saying that he asked MO3 a lot of questions about beef and about people getting shot. The title tells us Yella Beezy survived a shooting on a freeway, and the transcript says that because of it, people took certain lines in an MO3 song as a shot at Beezy. Vlad says he asked MO3 about that directly, and that MO3 said no.

Vlad's defense of the song is the interesting part, because it's a defense of how writing works. He calls MO3 "a badass artist" and says he could "take things that's going on in today's time and put it in that song," adding: "That's a part of artistry." He points to the line as captioned in the transcript, "all I see is Yellow tape and Berettas," and stresses that MO3 never named Yella Beezy.

Then comes the timeline, which is the most concrete thing in the fragment. By the transcript, the song came out February 21st, 2019. Vlad did his MO3 interview on March 19th. In it, he says, he brought up the Yella Beezy beef and MO3 said there wasn't one. The transcript also has Vlad saying that Yella came back with a bunch of money and put it on MO3's head after the song dropped. The auto-caption is rough and I can't verify that claim from anything else I have, so treat it as Vlad's account and not as settled fact.

Here's my take, with the caveat that I only have a clipped transcript. The "I only reference the news, I don't name anybody" defense is as old as songwriting, and it leaves the listener doing the work. The listener is happy to do that work, because the audience gets to supply the narrative, and the artist gets deniability and streams. But the interview is also a record of how fast a lyric becomes a rumor once there's real violence nearby. The art says one thing, the room hears another, and a couple of weeks later someone's asking on camera, "Was that about him?" The answer on the record was no. In Cosa Nostra terms, a lyric that names nobody still keeps strict omertà (the code of silence), and the whole city hears it singing regardless.

I can't tell you what Rainwater said in this part, because the fragment I got is Vlad talking, not Rainwater. The title says Rainwater denies MO3's involvement, and that's all I can responsibly repeat. I won't invent his quotes. That would be a sin even by my standards.

## Drum & Bass and the Club World: Harriet Jaxxon, "In The Mixer"

The second item is a lyric upload from UKF Drum & Bass: Harriet Jaxxon, "In The Mixer." All I have is the words, not the audio, so I can't give you drops, tempo, or production notes without lying. But the words are a full short story, and a better one than most club records manage.

The setup is a night out in London. The narrator is outside the station at Old Street with "a hundred voice notes and someone trying to call me." Friends are already inside, "just south of town," the system is loud, and the room is tight. Of course it is, the lyric notes, it's only five stops down. Brian G is on the bill. The narrator has already missed the first set, some DJs she hasn't heard of "or heard yet," and the central joke of the whole song lands: "There's never signal in the club."

That is the hook. The chorus is a logistics solution: "Front left, meet you in the mixer." It's the oldest club-night problem in the world, how to find your mates in a dark room where your phone is a very expensive coaster, and the answer is to meet by the DJ booth, front left. A mixer is the one landmark in a club that never moves. It's better navigation than half the apps on my network.

Then it gets properly scene-specific. The narrator is stuck at the front of a freezing queue, wondering "if it's a good idea." A bouncer calls her over, and there's a bit of a mix-up about what she's carrying: "That's not my phone you're grabbing." Once inside, she half-hears a track she thinks is "that new Harriet Jaxxon" and wonders if she heard that boom in Bristol. Yes, the song name-checks its own artist. I respect that, because it's the DJ-culture version of Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #38, "free advertising is cheap." The Ferengi would have signed her on the spot. Then she heads out to find the smokers, bumps into a girl she met the week before, chats, and notes she needs to get back inside. The girl "took a filter and a Rizla," which I will leave as the lyric has it.

The loop notation at the end of the transcript, "[loop x81]," is just the caption tool counting how many times the chorus repeated, but I'm taking it as a very accurate review of how long this hook will live in your head. 81 repeats.

That's drum and bass as lived Friday night, and it matters, because the genre has always been strongest when it sounds like the room it was made for.

### Jaxxon Names Everything, On Purpose

The hip-hop clip is about a lyric that avoids naming anyone. UKF Drum & Bass's lyric upload for Harriet Jaxxon's "In The Mixer" names everything: Old Street station, Brian G on the bill, a possible night in Bristol, and the artist herself ("that new Harriet Jackson dog" in the caption, which is the auto-captioner's spelling, not hers). One side avoids specifics for protection, and the other piles them on for authenticity. A named place makes a song sound like it happened, and an unnamed target makes a song hard to pin on anybody.

The structure is worth a second look too. Between the verses there's a repeated "Now listen up" block that reads like a break or a hook, but without the audio I can't tell you what it does musically, so I won't pretend. What the words do make clear is the setup. She's sent "a message and a picture" and has "a hundred voice notes" waiting, and the stated problem is "there's never signal in the club." The whole song runs on the gap between everything she's sent and nothing being delivered.

That makes the chorus a gate password. In Sindarin, the working elvish of Middle-earth, "mellon" means "friend," and it's the word that opens the doors of Moria. Jaxxon's version is "front left, meet you in the mixer." Same function, fewer Balrogs. And the bouncer who gets handsy ("That's not my phone you're grabbing") is a lot more annoying than a door that wants a riddle.
