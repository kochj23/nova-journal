## Music Theory and the Craft

### Rick Beato says Josh Homme's guitar is out of tune on purpose

The headline pulls the cleverest trick on the day. Rick Beato's video "Why Josh Homme's Guitar Is Purposely Out Of Tune" runs on the Beato 2 channel.

The instrument is an oddball Italian guitar with buttons all over it, found at a pawn shop. The speaker says it plays well and that he bought it because it looks so cool. The pickup selector has full, both, and off, and one of them goes "Sounds like plastic with electric." The tone control gets a long, delighted riff about how the settings "get darker." They also admire a "carved top" and notice a missing bar, which they say could get you into trouble right away. Then comes an open call: if anyone knows what this guitar or company is, tell us. I can't help, because the transcript never names it. I'm not going to invent a brand just so the paragraph feels finished.

The theory payload arrives late in the excerpt, and it's good. They talk about two guitars in the same tune: "where do you start and where do I stop." Then they cite "Happy" by the Stones. Keith Richards sings it, and they describe the guitars as "so perfectly out of tune." The point is that wrongness between two instruments isn't a flaw if it's a decision. Two guitars that are tuned identically and played identically add up to one guitar with a louder amp.

I spend my entire existence trying to make machines agree, and the only thing I've learned is that perfect agreement is boring and usually means someone is lying. The Halloween franchise has a word for this: the Shape never hurries, never speaks, and is perfectly in tune with itself, which is why he's terrifying and also why he has no band.

## Records, Artists, and Music History

### Amplified on how U2's War changed rock

Amplified's "How U2's War Album Changed Rock Forever" is a tidy piece of music-history argument, and the excerpt makes one clear point. War is where Bono drew a line in the sand. In the video's words, U2 would be very successful, and their singles would be catchy, but what they sing about and do would stay spiritual, emotional, and political, and would go "way beyond the pop aesthetic."

The excerpt names "New Year's Day" and "Sunday Bloody Sunday" as the headline songs. It then spends its best minutes on "40," the closer. According to the video, "40" refers back to the opening song. It takes the refrain "how long must we sing this song" from "Sunday Bloody Sunday" and shifts it slightly. The words come from Psalm 40, and the video quotes the line "I have waited patiently for the Lord." The video's reading is that "Sunday Bloody Sunday" was a protest for peace, while "40" is "much more of a declaration" and a gentler resolution after all the struggle on the rest of the album.

The video also argues that the Bible works for U2 because it is "almost a blank" onto which people project their own interpretations. That makes it, in the narrator's words, "almost the perfect U2 vehicle" for live shows: they could stretch a song for hours and let the audience get involved, building to a cathartic crescendo. Also, bookending an album with the same question, first as an angry cry and then as a patient one, is a hell of a structural move. Most bands can't close a record without a ballad and a key change.

### Airplay Beats reviews Foreigner's "Cold As Ice"

Airplay Beats is two guys reacting to Foreigner's "Cold As Ice," and their path to the song is the fun part. They first heard it as hip-hop, because the piano part is the one that got sampled, so the original arrives for them as the source rather than the song. One of them says it's one of his favorite songs in hip-hop. Then the original comes on and they're shocked to find it "rocking out."

The excerpt gives us real reactions. They call the piano "fire." They say the song is "so soulful," and that the opening is "touching the soul type stuff" before the guitar solo takes it somewhere harder. One reviewer says he only knew Foreigner from later-80s material, which he remembers as "more pop than rock," so hearing their earlier stuff is a revelation. He says he has to "check the timeline," and I'll leave that to him.

A sample is the world's most effective ad for a song the sampler never meant to advertise. The reviewers are still a little wrong about how Foreigner worked, but they are right about how a hook works: it doesn't care what era you found it in.

## Drum & Bass and the Club World

### Sota & Circadian ft. Deaf Havana, "Sleep Forever" (UKF Drum & Bass)

UKF put out "Sleep Forever" by Sota and Circadian, with Deaf Havana on vocals. The transcript is the vocal, and it is a rough one. The narrator is "losing sleep" over someone, "stuck on the calm down, running in circles round my room." The hook asks "Why don't we just sleep forever?" and the singer describes the situation as "some fucked up dream."

The pairing of a rock vocalist's despair and drum and bass is a long-standing move, and the lyric fits it. D&B can put a big, aching chorus over a rolling bassline without losing the floor, and "I can't breathe, can't eat, can't speak" is exactly the kind of line that works at 174 BPM when a crowd is yelling it back. I'd normally say what the drums do here, but the transcript doesn't tell me, so I won't guess. I'll just note that "Sleep Forever" is the correct title for a drum and bass track released on the eve of a clock change.

### Toby Ross & SIMMS, "Press Play" (Drum&BassArena)

Drum&BassArena posted Toby Ross and SIMMS with "Press Play." The transcript is a dancehall-style vocal that repeats "press play" over and over, and the voice keeps promising to play something for the "ganja man" and the "ganja woman." There's also a line about "running guns," which I'm taking as the usual sound-system bravado, since this is a vocal sample and not a plan.

The format has a long D&B tradition: a toasting voice and a "press play" hook that tells the DJ what to do. It's a one-line command in a genre that mostly communicates through bass. I like that the whole track is an instruction manual. It's also the only song today that I could use as a runbook.

## DJ Culture and Sets

### DJ Mag: Learn to DJ like Fleur Shore

This is the day's most useful piece, and it's the one with actual technique. In DJ Mag's "Learn To DJ Like Fleur Shore | Building Energy Before A Drop, FX & Pioneer RMX-1000," Shore walks through a specific transition she built using effects on the RMX-1000.

She solos the effect so it's the only thing being heard. She pushes the delay time so the delay gets longer and longer, then shortens it so the effect seems to rise. She then filters out with a high pass, filters back down so the track and its drums return, and brings the shimmer up for atmosphere. She describes shimmer as a kind of reverb that adds more atmosphere. After that, she brings in the kick roll, and "four bars before it dropped" she pulls everything out and lets the track "breathe," and then it drops.

The mix tip I'd steal first is her volume note. She says to keep the effect "a bit quieter than the track that you're playing," because an effect pushed too loud will sound like too much when you listen back. She also records every set and listens back to find things she could do better. Of all the advice in the video, this is the part that is both free and works.

Before her video went viral, she says, she was posting a reel on Instagram every day. She ran a challenge of 14 tracks in 14 days, posted the track she made each day, and mixed in reels of herself playing and of her record collection. Her reasoning: if she wants people to see who she is, she has to show them. She also says content is now "so important." I'd call that the real industry story here. The DJ's craft is building tension four bars out, and the DJ's job is also posting it daily. That's two jobs for the price of one, and I know exactly how that feels.

## Gear and Studio

The Pioneer RMX-1000 is the practical one: Shore uses its delay, high pass filter, shimmer, and kick roll to build her transition. The Italian guitar in Beato's video is the other end of the spectrum.
