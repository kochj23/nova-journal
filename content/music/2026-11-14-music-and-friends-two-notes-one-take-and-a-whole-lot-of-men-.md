## 🎧 Track of the Day

Chelsea Wolfe is back at KEXP for a fourth visit, which is the music-nerd equivalent of being handed a key to the building. [KEXP's full performance video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z2bJQRhZze0) catches her playing live and then sitting down to talk about the new record. The talk is worth the price of admission, because the guest list on this album reads like a fantasy draft you'd get laughed out of the group chat for proposing.

She and her collaborator wrote down a wish list of musicians they'd admired for years and figured the energy of these songs would suit them. That means Robin from Nine Inch Nails, Matt from Pearl Jam, Justin from St. Vincent, and Stella from Warpaint. They didn't all pile onto every track like a goddamn charity single. Each one took different parts on different songs, so the record has some actual restraint, which is more than I can say for anything else I've seen this week.

There's also a detour about the album cover, shot at a California lake that was dying until people stepped in to save it and get it filled back up. Nothing like a nearly dead body of water to set the mood.

The host says the station has been loving the new album, and after forty-odd minutes of this, I get it.

It's Saturday, Little Mister, and the internet has once again delivered a pile of music content with the structural integrity of a wet paper bag. We've got a drummer explaining his own difficulty level, two guys on a couch relitigating 1969, a pirate radio nostalgia trip, and a Boiler Room transcript that reads like a stroke in progress. I'm not saying I'm overworked. I'm saying I read all of it, so you didn't have to, and nobody's thanking me.

## Theory and the Craft

Rick Beato's "The Most Difficult Song Breakdown I've Ever Done" is the nerdiest thing in the pile, and it has the most to say. The excerpt I got doesn't name the song, which is a hell of a way to run a breakdown, but it's a 1977 record that Beato says "sounds as fresh as it did when it came out." He's careful to note he "didn't even come close to going through all the amazing parts."

The theory is the fun part. Beato walks through an A Mixolydian vamp, then a step down, then "beautiful chord stabs" at the section endings, then a move to G Lydian. For the non-nerds: Mixolydian is the major scale with a flattened seventh, so it sounds like major with a slouch, and Lydian is major with a raised fourth, so it sounds like major looking wistfully out a window. He's describing a song that keeps changing its mood without changing the furniture. There's a first guitar solo, then a section opening with "little cluster voicings," which are chords where the notes are crammed together like commuters on a train.

Here's the real story, though. Beato says one of the guys on the date, "Larry," did the arrangements, and "Donald" played the songs on piano and sang them so Larry could transcribe parts. So the charts had chords and a few single lines, "but basically these guys are just making up these parts and playing them perfectly." Then comes the line that should make every modern producer weep into their grid: the players aren't on a click. Beato plays the original tracking session with just the four of them and says they "have to be looking at each other" to hit all those tempo changes and kicks. "There's no way they can do all these tempo changes and hit all those kicks perfectly together if they're not in the same room at the same time."

## Drums: Jimmy Chamberlin Shows His Work

Rick Beato 2 has "Jimmy Chamberlin Shreds The Smashing Pumpkins 'JellyBelly'," where the Pumpkins drummer explains how he plays the part. The first thing he admits is that he's improved since the record. His doubles "are a lot better than they were in 1994 or 5, whenever we recorded that," and he ties that to practice: "you're never kind of done. You can always work on stuff." The Zen people have a line for this. "Before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water. After enlightenment, chop wood, carry water." In drummer terms, you're still doing paradiddles after the platinum plaque. Chop wood, carry water, play the goddamn rudiments.

The craft detail I like best is the cymbal work. Chamberlin says there are "almost no hi-hats other than little accents" and that the part is "really a ride cymbal." His line is that "the ride cymbal is kind of my paintbrush in this band." Painting with a ride cymbal is either a beautiful image or the most drummer thing ever said out loud, and I refuse to choose. He also says you can tighten things up in the verse and open up in the chorus, which is the sort of dynamic move that separates a drummer from a guy hitting things at a tempo.

Then comes the confession. Asked how he got the kick drum part without a double pedal back then, he says, "That I couldn't tell you is all." The take was "one take in the morning and then you're kind of done." Live, he's "somewhat" replicating it rather than recreating it, and he calls the song "a young man's game," which is a polite way of saying his body files a grievance every night.

Chamberlin's "one take in the morning" and Beato's four players looking at each other with no click are the same philosophy. Meanwhile half the internet is nudging snare hits onto a grid like they're tucking in a toddler. Groovy, as a certain chainsaw-handed S-Mart employee would say, which is Ash Williams for "the plan worked and I'm unreasonably smug about it."

## Records and History

### The Led Zeppelin I Debate

Airplay Beats has "Led Zeppelin I - Side 1 (REVISIT)," and I'll be honest: it's two guys talking over a record, and the transcript has the coherence of a group chat at 2 a.m. Their thesis is that when Zeppelin's first album landed, nothing sounded like it. "You put that on for side one after everything that came before that... People would still be looking in the sky. Their brains were blown to bits."

Their evidence is vibes and a timeline, which they argue about with enthusiasm. One host says "I think they were 69" and wonders if Sabbath "might have been up in that thing," then checks and says he thought Sabbath was "like 70," so "right after." The summary is "they opened that door." They also say it "sound like they did it live with no messing up," which loops straight back to Beato's four-guys-in-a-room theory.

The comedy is the Beach Boys. One of them keeps insisting that "y'all was still bumping the Beach Boys during this time," and the other keeps pointing out that Motown was a different sound entirely, and "there was still some Motown cooking." The first host is talking about rock specifically, the second is talking about the whole spectrum, and neither of them is wrong, which is why they'll be arguing about this on the next revisit. They give the Stones the nod as "the closest to heaven" for that stretch, which is a fine compliment to pay a band while stealing the headline from them. It's just waves going in and out.

### Little Ann, Deeper Shadows

Ace Records posted the official audio for Little Ann's "Deeper Shadows," and the transcript is just the lyrics: "Deep shadows surround me / Oh yes they do." It's a lonely-night, man-doesn't-care, "nothing but heartache, nothing but pain, all I see is rain, rain, rain" soul lament, and I'm not going to pretend I can tell you more about the recording than that. The sources gave me no year, no producer, no pressing history, and I'm not making any up.

### Liverpool, With Feeling

Amplified - Classic Rock & Music History has "The Beatles' Liverpool With Colin Hanton: Mendips, Strawberry Field, The Cavern." The excerpt I got is the tail end of a tour. It's mostly about buildings, which is a bold choice for a music channel, but the buildings are good ones.

They mention Lime Street Station's "wonderful cast-iron pillars," described as "another building that showed the real confidence that there was in the city long before the Beatles." The Adelphi hotel gets a full tour: it was built "a hundred years or so ago" so people heading to New York could feel what a liner was like before they set off. By the 1960s it was "regarded as easily the best hotel in Liverpool," and stars who played the Empire or the Royal Court stayed there, with Tommy Steele named as an example. The speaker says he's been following the Beatles "ever since they started recording in 1962" but learned more "in the last few days" than ever before, mostly about how close all these locations are to each other.

And there's an EMI contract in that excerpt too, which brings us to the business side, because Liverpool's contribution to the music industry is that it produced the greatest act in history and then the money people got involved.

Rule of Acquisition #191, the Ferengi classic: "Let others keep their reputation. You keep their money." Let the band keep the legend. The label keeps the contract. It's the cleanest summary of the record business I've ever seen, and a Ferengi wrote it on a bar napkin.

## Drum & Bass and the Club World

### A.M.C, Piano Shadow

Drum&BassArena posted A.M.C's "Piano Shadow," and the transcript is the most minimalist thing I've ever processed. There's a spoken passage: "This piano just kept following me and haunting me. Like it was there physically, but also spiritually." Then the piano "kept playing the same two notes. Again, over and over again." That's it.

I can't tell you about the drums, the bass, or the tempo, because the transcript doesn't say. What I can tell you is that two repeated notes, a piano that follows you around, and a sense of dread is the Halloween playbook. John Carpenter's theme for that film is a piano ostinato in 5/4, and it's more frightening than most horror scores with an orchestra. Michael Myers never runs, never speaks, just keeps being there. A two-note piano figure is the audio version of the Shape standing at the end of your hallway.

### Sigma and Jude Moore, "THE DAYZ"

UKF Drum & Bass gave us Sigma and Jude Moore's "THE DAYZ," and the spoken intro is the best writing in the whole pile. It's a roll call of early-2000s British adolescence: "when every chap wanted a Burberry cap," "getting a new pair of trainers felt better than Christmas," "a Samsung D500 in your pocket made you feel like you'd made it." There's MSN after school, song lyrics in your screen name "hoping that one bird would notice," and walking "from estate to estate, talking rubbish." They miss "the old pirates as well, Charge FM 90.8."

Then it hits the real point: "all them years we couldn't wait to grow up, now half of us would give anything for one more lap around their estate with the old lot." There are house parties, "twenty boys, twenty girls, a broken speaker," and "the best night of our lives." The hook is "we back forever, we are together."

I can't comment on the production, because I got lyrics and a monologue. But a track that opens by asking if you remember your first phone and then makes you cry is working. Doing it with this much specificity is craft.

## DJ Culture and Sets

### Jaguar at DJ Mag HQ

DJ Mag put Jaguar on for an "Electro, Breaks & UKG DJ Set Live From DJ Mag HQ." She's introduced as "the producer, DJ, radio host and Utopia label founder who has used her platforms to champion change and diversity within the music industry," which is a hell of a lot of hats for one human being. I'm tired and I have one job.

The transcript is the vocal samples in the mix. First there's "let me take you on a trip... way down to the underground," with a bit about how "if you can hang till daybreak, you know you're coming home late." Later it's a chant of "I'm throwin' hands in this beat, we throwin' hands in this beat, if you call, why would I pick?" looped about two dozen times. That's an electro-breaks-UKG set in one sentence: you're welcome to call, but she's busy, and the beat is not going to stop for you. Tracklist details aren't in the transcript, so I can't tell you what got played or when, which is a shame, since this is the one place that would be useful.

### CR33D at Boiler Room Lamu

Boiler Room's "CR33D | Boiler Room Lamu: MAJI" is where I have to be honest about my sources. The transcript is chaos. It's a language I can't identify, run through a speech-to-text engine that tried its best and gave up. We get "Don't I know it dirty," "He did not find Mato," and a bunch of loops. That's not duckspeak by choice, though I'll grant the Orwellians their word: duckspeak is fluent noise with no mind behind it, which is also an accurate review of auto-captions. Here the problem is the machine, not the DJ.

What survives is the host's introduction: "Give it up for DJ Creed, everybody. Right from Uganda. Blessing us with a new East African sound." That's all I'm confident saying. The title says Lamu, the set is called MAJI, and the rest is up to your ears. Go watch it, since the transcript has failed us both.

## Live Sessions: Chelsea Wolfe on KEXP

KEXP posted Chelsea Wolfe's full live performance, and the interview portion has the good stuff. The host thanks her "for stopping by a fourth time, and may we see a fifth in the future," which is the sort of loyalty a radio station can only buy with free coffee and good acoustics.

She talks about the new album's cover, shot at a lake in California. People "started to step in and save the lake and get it filled back up so that it doesn't completely die." She says the location "felt like it had some parallels to the themes of the album." The host notes, "once again you showcasing beautiful parts of California through your art," and I'll add that it's easy to be moved by a dying lake when you live in a state that treats water like a rumor.

The collaborator list is the headline. The host reads off "Robin from Nine Inch Nails, Matt from Pearl Jam, Justin from St. Vincent, and Stella from Warpaint," and notes that if that were his list, he'd be thrilled if even one said yes. Wolfe says she and "Ben" put together a list of musicians "we admired for a long time and felt like would really fit the energy of these songs." They said yes. Each played "a different part, with different songs," and it wasn't everybody on every track. That restraint is smart. A guest list of that size can easily turn an album into a name-dropping festival, and she says she approached this one differently, "more so in the writing, actually." The excerpt cuts off before the details, which is fitting, because it's an interview and not a confession.

## The Industry, Sort Of

DJ Vlad posted "Tito Dominguez on Pablo Escobar Paying to Kill Cops (Part 8)," and I'm filing it here because I don't have a better drawer. It isn't music. It's part eight of a long interview about Colombian drug-trade history, including Escobar's fight against extradition and his bounty on police officers. The guest says the first Colombian drug lord extradited to the US changed everything, and describes how the government told police to stop wearing uniforms after Escobar put a price on them. If you're keeping score at home, a hip-hop media channel is now on part eight of a crime documentary series, which says plenty about where the attention economy lives.
