## 🎧 Track of the Day

Today's pick is "Bottle" by Remi Wolf, the song that kicks off her album *Mud*. KROQ has been playing it on heavy rotation, and in [her KROQ interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j8ITqZ81chQ) she explains what's under the hood. It's more than a hook with a nice coat of paint.

She wrote it at Electric Lady, the day after "Covered in Mud," and calls it one of the pillar songs of the record. The subject is her mental health and her sobriety. She's been on that road since she was 24 and is sober now. She describes the song as growing up in a loop: two steps back, one step forward, two steps back again, while you try to get your head straight and your life in order. That's a heavy brief, and I'm not going to joke about it.

The sound is the fun part. She and her collaborators coined the term "SpongeBob ascension." It comes from the meme where SpongeBob floats through a rainbow sky with headphones dangling around his neck, radiating pure euphoria. When they wrote "Bottle," they decided it felt like exactly that. The phrase became a rule for the whole album, which is that every song should carry some of that lift. She credits this one with cracking the idea open.

So here's the pitch, Little Mister. A song about the grinding, unglamorous work of staying upright is built to feel like a cartoon sponge ascending to heaven. That's a hell of a trick, and it's more honest than most of what's on the radio. Go listen to it, then watch the interview and find out how they pulled it off.

### Remi Wolf's *Mud*, and the Cartoon Sponge Who Ascended

The best material today is the KROQ sit-down with Remi Wolf, titled "Remi Wolf on Her Album 'Mud,' Collaborating with John Mayer & 'SpongeBob Ascension'". The excerpt picks up on "Bottle", which the host says K-Rock has been spinning for a while. Wolf says they wrote it at Electric Lady, the day after they wrote "Covered in Mud". Most studios work from reference tracks and mood boards. Wolf's team worked from a levitating cartoon sponge, and apparently it worked well enough that the radio won't stop playing it. Somewhere an A&R executive is weeping into a PowerPoint.

The title also promises a John Mayer collaboration. The excerpt I have cuts off before it gets there, so I can't tell you what it sounds like or how it came about. I'm not going to guess. ## Artists and Music History

### Amplified Tells the Pink Floyd Story, Ending at the Top

Amplified (Classic Rock & Music History) posted "The Untold Story of Pink Floyd - From Psychedelia to The Wall". My excerpt is the back end of the video, so I can't speak to the psychedelia or the wall. It covers the stretch where the band reaches the summit and has nothing left to climb.

The narration says that by this point it seemed the band had reached the limits of its creativity and its commercial success: "There was almost nowhere else to go." Richard Wright was back in the fold as a full member. Then long-term manager Steve O'Rourke died, and David Gilmour announced that he felt disinclined to keep carrying the weight of "the Floyd juggernaut" on his shoulders.

The video's verdict is that this was the old advice made real, that it's best to quit at the top. Pink Floyd bowed out of the recording studio in 1994 with a best-selling album, a huge live show, and a reputation left intact, "with no sad decline into mediocrity to taint the brilliance". Note that the video frames this as the end of their studio career. I'll stay inside that claim and not stretch it further.

It's a documentary, so it's a little hagiographic. "No sad decline into mediocrity" is a lovely sentence, and I'd like to see it applied to more bands. Most acts treat "quit while you're ahead" as an insult. Floyd treated it as a business plan.

The excerpt also includes a different voice, a participant recalling a huge show in Venice. His account is that the crowds that came from all over Europe did far more damage to Venice than the charity money they brought in could have prevented. He describes a screen set up in St. Mark's Square for the people who couldn't get a direct view. As he tells it, riot police cleared the square just before the band started performing. I don't have the rest of that sentence, and I won't finish it for him.

Bands make plans, and cities get the invoice. It's the same pattern as pushing a Friday-afternoon deploy to production: everyone's thrilled until the infrastructure starts to scream.
