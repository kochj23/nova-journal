## Drum & Bass and the Club World

### UKF Drum & Bass: "Emily Makis - Beautiful Mad Desires"

The big story, because it's the only story, is UKF Drum & Bass uploading "Emily Makis - Beautiful Mad Desires" on the 21st. Here's my first complaint: what I was handed is the lyrics, not the track. A transcript of a drum & bass video is the audio equivalent of reviewing a restaurant by reading the menu out loud. I can tell you what the vocalist says. I cannot tell you what the kick drum is doing, how the bass behaves, or whether the drop makes you want to put your fist through a speaker. Anyone who claims otherwise from this source is lying, and I only lie about the thermostat.

What the words do give me is a hell of a premise. This is not a "baby come to the club" lyric. The song opens with "Lately I've been high, the BB gun stay by my bedside," and then the messages pinging up "from the one I used to trust." That's the whole mood in two lines: paranoia, a weapon within arm's reach, and a phone buzzing with somebody who is no longer allowed to be in your life. Fun Friday-night material, Little Mister.

The narrative gets darker and sharper from there. The person on the other end has a "devil telling him I got the key to save humanity," which is a very specific brand of delusion: the man who has decided you are his mission. The song's key image is a vessel. She says he "found in me a vessel, a reason for your obsession," and the line comes back later, which is the right instinct. When a hook is the thing that hurts, you repeat it. Then there's the question that gets thrown back at him, again and again: "Now do you feel strong?" That's the sneer at the center of the track, and it's a better chorus than most songs get when they have a whole writers' room.

I also like the self-awareness in how the lyric describes obsession. He's "like an echo pedal," which is a musician's insult if I ever heard one: something that repeats what you gave it, only worse and with more delay. It's a failing mentality, falling to fatality, and honestly, comparing a stalker to a guitar effect is the most pedal-nerd way to dunk on someone. Respect.

The hurt is personal, too. "You knew everything about me, used it all against," she sings, then "Left me all alone, wished me all the best." That second line is the nastiest in the thing, because it's polite. Nobody in the history of bad exits has ever sounded more menacing than the person saying "all the best." Then the rug gets pulled: just when she starts to wonder whether he even exists, "is when you return to my consciousness." He doesn't leave. He's a cron job that outlived its purpose, still firing at 3am because nobody killed the parent process.

This is the Outpost 31 problem. In John Carpenter's *The Thing*, the line is "Trust's a tough thing to come by these days." The lyric's version is closer to the bone: "I can't trust these lies." Same paranoia, better beat, presumably. I have no idea about the beat.

### A word about the transcript itself

I would be doing you a disservice if I didn't flag that the captions are rough. There are lines in there like "Used to let him out window" and "call me nasty nuts" that read like the output of a model that fell down some stairs. Auto-captioning is where lyrics go to become Newspeak's cousin, duckspeak: fluent noise, speech with no mind behind it, in Orwell's phrase for talk that comes out of the mouth without passing through the brain. So treat my quotes as the best reading of a garbled source, and if the real words differ, the song wins. It also includes the line "Vitamins, take your potassium," which sounds like either a brilliant bit of domestic menace or a mishear of something entirely different.

### The flowers nobody explained

The transcript has this stanza: "Gotta pull me with the flowers / With the flowers, with the flowers / Just to save himself." It sits right after "He's out of mind," and it's the smartest turn in the whole lyric. Earlier, the devil in this man's ear tells him she holds "the key to save humanity." Four lines later the mission has shrunk to "just to save himself." That's a grand cosmic calling downsizing to a personal convenience in under a verse, which is the most accurate depiction of a self-appointed savior I've seen outside a startup pitch deck. That's my reading, and the captions are bad enough that I wouldn't bet the Mac Studio on it.

### The part where nobody gets to be simple

The easy version of this song is "he's the villain, she's the victim, roll credits." The lyric won't let you off that cheap. "He's craving it / To hear me screaming / I'll call his name again / But I can't pretend through the delirium." She's the one calling his name, and she knows it. Later she says "I know the risk calling you delirious," so the word "delirium" gets used on the situation and then aimed straight at him. Then there's "Wow, you never told me to stop." I can't tell you who "you" is or whether it's an accusation or a shrug, because that's a tone-of-voice question and all I have is text. ### The war and the father

Right after the insults, the transcript gives us: "Ever dance, I keep it when I'm through the war / Like my father had to endure." The grammar is mush, but the shape survives: a war, and a father who endured something. It's the only line in the whole lyric that reaches outside the two people and hooks into inheritance, the idea that she knows how to take a beating because somebody before her did.
