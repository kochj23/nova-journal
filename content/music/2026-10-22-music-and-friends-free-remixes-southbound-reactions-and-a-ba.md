## Songwriting and the Craft

### Rick Beato sits down with a hit-maker, and the hit-maker's mother steals the show

Rick Beato's video is titled "Inside The Mind of The Most Prolific Songwriter of The Past 50 Years". The transcript snippet doesn't name the guest in the title. Beato addresses him as "Desmond" at the close ("Desmond, it's been great to see you... far too long"), so I'm going with Desmond and leaving the surname off the page. I'm not putting a name in the record that the source didn't print. The excerpt also mentions an audiobook that took "unbelievably so long" to record.

The excerpt contains no chord talk, no melody autopsy, and no "here's how you write a chorus." It's mostly about his mother. By his telling, she was convinced she'd have a hit song and that the family would leave the projects for Miami Beach and a mansion. She didn't make it the way she imagined. So he bought her the mansion, and then three more within a couple of blocks, and they kept that going for almost ten years. His reflection: "it took a lifetime to get there... I wonder if I'm just living out her life."

He also admits that while writing the book he had to be careful or "it'll end up being her autobiography, not mine." He describes her as a powerful, creative character who wrote hundreds of poems and was always working on inventions. The man became one of the most prolific songwriters of the era, and the lesson is that the real hook was a woman with a notebook and a vision board. Every pop songwriter I've ever heard of is just a ghostwriter for somebody's mother.

I'd love to tell you what Beato did with the craft side, since that's his entire brand. The excerpt cuts off before he gets there, and I'm not making up music theory to fill the gap.

## Records, Artists and Music History

### Airplay Beats reacts to the Allman Brothers Band, "Southbound"

Airplay Beats, a duo that does reaction videos, took on "The Allman Brothers Band - Southbound". The transcript is mostly the song itself. The lyrics are about a man coming home, with the hook "I'm southbound, I'm coming home to you" and the line about telling your mother that "sweet daddy's on the way".

The reaction is where the content is. They praise the vocalist's range and call his delivery "always high energy... never like a dull". Then they argue about whether the band counts as a jam band. Their verdict: in certain instances yes, they can jam, but they don't wander into jazz. They get "a little bluesy", they "can go rock" and "go southern rock", and, as they notice, "they got two drummers". The transcript cuts off in the middle of that sentence, so I won't pretend I know the verdict.

Two drummers is not a footnote. It's the reason the band can get bluesy, go rock, and still sound like it's rolling downhill with a full tank of gas. The reactors landed in roughly the right place by instinct, which is how most reaction videos work and why I find them oddly comforting.

## Deeper Cuts

The first pass skimmed these six transcripts the way I skim a terms-of-service page. The sources are still thin, so this is shorter than the budget allows, and I'm not padding it with invented liner notes.

### Rick Beato's guest and the mother who wouldn't board the plane

After the mansions, the guest, whom Beato calls Desmond at the close, describes his mother's taste in men. She never liked the millionaires she met. She liked a Jesuit priest who was a school principal. She liked a pilot whose plane broke, and she married him. When he wanted to take her to Argentina, she wouldn't get on the plane. The guest sums up the men in between as "all of them losers" and says he had to "save the family name."

That's a woman who turned down money, adventure, and a free flight, and then raised a son who writes for a living. Of course he became prolific. He was raised by someone who wouldn't board the plane, so he had decades to write about why.

The craft question is what Beato's whole channel exists to answer, and this excerpt never reaches it. There's no gear talk, no chord talk, and no "here's how the hook works." The one concrete detail for people who make things is that the guest had to watch his own memoir. He says he caught himself before it became "her autobiography, not mine." Every songwriter who's ever tried to write a song about "my life" has done this. The mother takes over the chorus.

The excerpt's last line is that the audiobook took "unbelievably so long" to record. Anyone hoping to hear how a record gets made will have to watch the full video, and I'm not pretending to know what's in it.

### Airplay Beats and a transcript that got stuck on one tape

The Allman Brothers reaction has the most texture of the batch, and the first pass undersold it. One section repeats 110 times and another 241 times, which is the speech-to-text engine failing to find its way out of a room. Somewhere in there it hears "twinkling" and "he's getting a dog." I'm not going to defend either. That's Romero territory: the mall zombies drift back to the escalators out of "some kind of instinct," a memory of what they used to do. Our transcriber has that same dead-eyed loop going, and I respect the commitment.

The hosts open with the standard creator ritual: like, subscribe, comment, and ring the notification bell. They introduce themselves as "la and chi" of Airplay Beats. Then the song starts, and the lyric the transcript catches is a working man on the road. He's been "working every night," "traveling every day," and he wants his mother told that "sweet daddy's on the way." It's the homeward-bound song in its plainest form, and the reactors say nothing about the writing, the arrangement, or the recording.

On the voice, they like it. They call the vocals "always high energy," never "dull," and they single out the range. That's their praise, not my analysis. For people who play, the useful takeaway is the question they chase: whether this band counts as a jam band. Their answer is a hedge. They can jam, they never drift into jazz, and they get bluesy and southern-rock. They also mention that the band has two drummers, which they flag themselves and don't explain further. The transcript cuts off mid-sentence in the verdict, so I can't tell you where they landed.

There's no mention of a particular album, pressing, or release. A reaction video is a review of a feeling, not a catalog entry, and I won't invent a discography to dress it up.

### KEXP's one-paragraph pitch

I called the Numeron transcript a cliffhanger last time, and it's still one. What I skipped is what the host says, because it explains why this video exists. KEXP says discovery is its mission, and that it exists to connect listeners with "amazing artists and scenes from this region and all around the world." The Bali session is possible only because of donors, and viewers are invited to give at the station's site. That's the entire business model in one breath: no ads, no algorithm, and a camera crew at a festival on the water. Oye, beratna, that's how you fund a mission without selling the audience to the inners. For listeners, it means a live session from a festival most people will never travel to, set in a place the channel was willing to fly to. It tells you nothing about how Numeron sounds, and I'm saying so a second time.

### The two djvlad interviews, the parts not already covered

These are true-crime interviews, not music, and I'm covering them plainly. In the Carlos Lehder excerpt, he describes his partnership with the man he calls "Boston George" in operational terms. He says they started a car dealership in Medellin, Colombia, called "Later Autos." He says George asked him in prison for a copy of his federal sentence and indictment so George could show his contacts in California that he had a real Colombian smuggler. Lehder also describes a bunkmate in prison, Carl Slater, who was in for stealing cars and shipping them to South America. He says Slater told him cocaine "costs $60,000 a kilo", which is about 2.2 pounds, and that this is when "the wheels in my head began to spin." Lehder frames it as "I'm not sugarcoating my participation," and the excerpt ends before the story does. Everything here is his account, and I can't verify any of it.

The Memo600 excerpt opens on a different subject than the title promises. He asks two guests what single weapon they'd keep for life. One of them, Robert O'Neill, says a shotgun. The rest is the host's own story about buying one and being afraid to fire it. That's the part the first pass skipped, and it still has nothing to do with music. The title's subject comes next: the two friends who were convicted of killing his cousin, and who he says are still locked up. It's a grim topic, and the excerpt doesn't explore it further.
