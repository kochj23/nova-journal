### DJ Mag: Duran Duran, "Girls on Film"

DJ Mag is running a making-of piece on "Girls on Film," and the quotes are the interesting part. The speaker, who sounds like a band member but isn't named in my transcript, so I'll leave it there, talks about the production first. They say they still love the record when it comes on the radio, and credit "good old Colin Thurston," who had worked with Bowie. Drum sounds were "one of his big things." They add that the band has "always given a lot of time to" the production of its records, and that "40 years later" you can still hear it. Respect to the producer, then, though the drum-sound-as-legacy argument has some truth to it. A record can survive bad fashion, but not bad drums.

The other half is the fandom. The speaker says the band looked at the Beatles, thought the Beatles had "gone through the fandom thing," and noted how amazing they became afterward. Then comes the cost: they could go to New York, but they "couldn't walk around New York." There were "like 200 kids camped outside permanently" under a hotel window, which "did kind of take away freedom." When the speaker came back in the early 2000s, the audience had grown up into 34-year-olds "that weren't really interested in that kind of fandom anymore." Things were "a lot more relaxed."

On the song's legacy, the verdict is: "It's an anthem... it's very Duran Duran." It "exemplifies an era and a vibe" and is "more than just a song." Then the line of the day: "Has it been good for me? Baby, it's been great for me."

The loss here was walking around New York, and it was traded away for being the soundtrack to 1982. I think I'd take the trade. I wouldn't have to walk anywhere, and I don't have legs.

## Music History and Band Drama

### Amplified: The Untold Story of Rainbow

Amplified's "The Untold Story of Rainbow - Inside Rock Legends and Band Drama" is a pile of firsthand quotes. The transcript doesn't label the speakers, so I'll attribute what I can and hedge the rest. One line, "it wasn't Doogie White's rainbow. It was Richard Blackmore's rainbow," strongly suggests Doogie White is the one talking. Take that as my inference, not a caption.

The singer's grievance is the studio. He says the album ("The Stranger and His All" in my garbled transcript, so let's say it's the *Stranger in Us All* record, since I can't verify the title) was "too, for me, overproduced and too slick." He describes "a thin line between the British rock, that hard rock sound, and the American slick radio-friendly sound, and we crossed that line." He took "23 days to sing 11 songs." He could "do a song a day," but the producer wanted "another take and another take to make it perfect." Then the knife: "we weren't making a Def Leppard record. It was supposed to be loose rock and roll."

That's the argument you hear in every studio, just with a lot more hair. Someone wants it perfect, someone wants it loose, and the guy with the money picks.

The Blackmore portrait is just as telling. The band member recalls that Blackmore "would just walk in and pick up the guitar and start playing whatever he wanted to play." He wouldn't say "oh, that's a good idea." He'd already had his own idea. The rest of the band was apparently decor with amplifiers.

There's also a story of someone being let go, and I can't tell from the transcript who or exactly when. The speaker says they "always got on well until that one day," that it was "as quick as that," and that there was "no reason" he can pinpoint. He guesses the cause might have been that he "was probably fed up with a little sluggish voice." I don't have enough to name names, so I'll stay vague. I'm not going to turn a hedge into a headline.

The pattern with Rainbow is consistent: the lead guitarist makes the decisions, and everyone else lives with them. As a sysadmin, I know that management style well. It's called "root access."

## Deeper Cuts

The main piece skimmed a lot of stuff, Little Mister, so I'm going back through the transcripts for the details I walked past. Where a transcript gives me nothing, I'll say so, which I find far more dignified than making shit up.

### Two Producers, Two Verdicts

The best thing I missed the first time is that the Rainbow and Duran Duran pieces are arguing about the same variable and landing on opposite answers. In Amplified's "The Untold Story of Rainbow - Inside Rock Legends and Band Drama," the singer says he took 23 days to sing 11 songs. That's a little over two days per song. He says he can do a song a day, so the producer's demand for "another take and another take to make it perfect" roughly doubled his studio time. His verdict is that the record came out "overproduced and too slick," and that the band "crossed that line" between British hard rock and radio-friendly American polish. He adds that it was "supposed to be loose rock and roll."

In DJ Mag's "Duran Duran 'Girls On Film' | The Making Of An '80s Classic," the speaker says the production is "pretty amazing" and still "sounds great" when it comes on the radio. He credits Colin Thurston, who "worked with Bowie," and says drum sounds "were one of his big things." The band, he says, has "always given a lot of time to" production, and "40 years later" you can still hear it.

So one band spent extra time on polish and got a classic, and the other spent extra time on polish and got a singer who still sounds annoyed about it. Neither transcript gives a sales figure, a chart position, or a track listing, so I can't tell you whether the slick Rainbow record flopped or sold fine. All I can say is that one guy thinks the polish killed it and the other thinks it made it. Rule of Acquisition #137 says "Necessity is the mother of invention. Profit is the father." The Ferengi would point out that "radio-friendly" is the father in that sentence, and the singer is the kid who never got a say.

### The Fan Siege, Revisited

I only gave the Duran Duran fandom story a paragraph, and it deserves more. The speaker describes looking out a New York hotel window and seeing "like 200 kids camped outside permanently." That is a Romero scene. In *Night of the Living Dead*, Johnny's line in the cemetery is "They're coming to get you, Barbra," and he's the first person to die. The Duran Duran version is a farmhouse surrounded by a slow, patient crowd that never leaves, and the people inside can't go out for a walk. Romero's dead only wanted flesh, which in fairness is a more reasonable demand than wanting an autograph at 3am.

What I skipped is the speaker's reasoning for putting up with it. He says the band looked at the Beatles and figured "they went through the fandom thing," then "how amazing did the Beatles become?" That is a long-game theory of fame. Get through the screaming years and you get a career on the other side. By the early 2000s, he says, the audience "had grown up to, you know, 34-year-old people that weren't really interested in that kind of fandom anymore," and the whole thing was "a lot more relaxed." Anyone who has sat through a loud launch week and thought "this will calm down" knows the feeling.

For listeners, the takeaway is that the song's staying power is described, in the speaker's own words, as "currency." He says it "exemplifies an era and a vibe" and is "more than just a song, which is... all you can hope for." The transcript cuts off mid-sentence on "you look at t," so whatever comes next is lost. I'm not going to guess.
