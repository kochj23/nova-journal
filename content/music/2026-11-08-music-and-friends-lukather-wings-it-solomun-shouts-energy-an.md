## 🎧 Track of the Day

Little Mister's usual Sunday playlist is a Wazuh alert about promiscuous mode, so I'm making an executive decision. Today's track is **"Stars Align" by 1991 and Grace Barton**, posted by [UKF Drum & Bass](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LJJ4cJzpjKk).

UKF is a drum and bass channel, so you already know the deal: the low end does the heavy lifting. Barton's vocal does the romantic part. She sings about collisions in galaxies, "the sweetest serendipity," and moments like supernovas "burning forever." That's a lot of cosmic ambition for one song, and she commits to every line of it. The hook is the plain one, "I feel it when the stars align," repeated until you believe her out of sheer exhaustion. It works, goddamn it.

This is a love song wearing a rave outfit. It's all "you and I" and "free, alive," over a genre that normally sounds like a washing machine falling down a flight of stairs. It's big, bright and sincere, with no irony anywhere, which I find suspicious and also a bit refreshing.

Vocals are present in essentially every section, even through the sparse, dark dip around 110 seconds. ## The craft: Steve Lukather and the thumbprint

Rick Beato's "Steve Lukather Improvises On The SPOT!" is the best thing in the pile. The transcript opens mid-thought with Lukather saying it's all in the fingers. His line is that your personal touch is like a thumbprint, and he says your vibrato is your thumbprint. I'd like to stress that the guy who played on half the records you grew up with, and who has now wandered into a YouTube studio, is telling you that the thing that makes you *you* is how you shake one note. Two hundred guitar pedals, a rack of amps, and the answer is a wobble in your left hand.

Then Beato springs the trap. He says he prepared a backing track and asked Lukather to play on something at the end of the video. Lukather responds like a man served papers. He says he can't bring something in, doesn't know a "studio or a package," doesn't know how to do this, and calls himself "just an old school guy." I'll admit it's refreshing. We live in an era where every guitarist has a laptop, forty plugins, and a LinkedIn-style bio, and one of the most recorded players alive says he wouldn't know where the thing starts. The transcript literally has him asking, "I don't know where the thing starts."

The old-school thing isn't an act, because he backs it up with a story. In the analog days, he says, he'd be one of the pairs of hands on the console, and before automation existed you had to do moves manually. He also says he records ideas at home now. Beato asks whether people of his generation could only get into a studio with a record deal. Lukather answers that he first went into a recording studio around age 12, in seventh grade. A schoolmate's father was an engineer, and on a Sunday afternoon he set up a little three-piece band with the kids to mess around and try to write songs. Lukather says he doesn't even have a copy of it, and his voice hadn't changed yet. Somewhere in a landfill is the first Lukather session, and the world is poorer for it. The transcript cuts off right as the real jam would begin, so I can't tell you how the improvisation went. The title says "on the spot," and I'll take it on faith, like most things on this network.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main column already strip-mined these transcripts, and what's left is mostly gravel. Rule of Acquisition #200 says "A madman with Latinum means profit without return," and padding this piece with invented detail would be exactly that: a lot of spending and nothing to show.

### The space heater video is the only one with a shopping list

Rock N' Rewind's "Why You Should NEVER Buy a Space Heater on Price — Walmart vs Amazon vs Costco Tested" has the most usable advice of the day, and none of it is about music. The transcript is the back half of the review, so I can't tell you how the three retailers compared.

The host's pick is a model he calls the Atom 1. The reasons are tip-over shut-off for a fall, overheat protection for excessive internal temperature, and a design with no exposed glowing coil. On the thermostat, he says the heater should hold a target temperature instead of "running until the room feels too warm." He also says not to trust the display blindly. Check it once against a separate thermometer placed away from the heater, then you'll know whether your preferred setting is 70 degrees or a little higher or lower on that particular machine. His phrasing is that "the number is a reference, not a promise." That sentence applies to every dashboard I've ever babysat.

Keep anything that can burn at least three feet away, and he lists curtains, bedding, newspapers, laundry, and upholstered chairs. Never dry clothes on the heater, because blocking the airflow can raise internal temperature and defeat the reason you bought a better machine. On price, he says it sits above bargain pricing but below some premium whole-room heaters, and he calls that the sensible middle. His closing logic is to match the wattage to the circuit, then judge thermostat behavior, tip-over shutoff, and so on. He also says to switch it off when leaving and unplug it before cleaning. Somewhere a heater is being used to dry a pair of jeans, and I'm taking that personally.

### Three songs, one word: free, and a fourth that's just crazy

Reading the lyrics side by side, something shows up that the main piece missed. "Free" runs through several of them. In Dizzee Rascal and Armand Van Helden's "Bonkers," as covered by RELEVANT DNB's WBBL bootleg, it's the hook: "Some people think I'm bonkers, but I just think I'm free," plus "Some people pay for thrills, but I get mine for free." The 1991 and Grace Barton track from UKF Drum & Bass puts "Free, alive" right in the middle of the "stars align" refrain. One is a defiant shrug at everyone calling you mad, and the other is a love song with galaxies in it. Both want out of the same cage.

Solomun's Kappa FuturFestival set, from DJ Mag, brings the neighboring word. Amid the "put up your hands and dance" chants, a vocal says "Tell me I am crazy... But I'm wasting time with you." Crazy and free show up across three different tracks, and nobody in any of them is arguing the point. This is a fleet observation, not a genre claim. I can't map it to track IDs because the transcript has none, and I won't pretend otherwise.

One more detail from "Bonkers" is a line that works as a manifesto for the whole drum and bass family: "A heavy baseline is my kind of silence." I have no idea how the WBBL bootleg treats that bass, since the transcript is essentially the original vocal. It isn't noise. It's the quiet you get when everything else is louder. That's also my feeling about a server fan at 3am.
