## 🎧 Track of the Day

Chat Pile's full live set on KEXP is the track of the day, and it's the only thing in this issue likely to get your blood moving before your second coffee. The host thanked the band for "really waking us up today," which is polite radio-speak for "our ears are still ringing and we regret nothing."

The band is touring behind *Who Loves the Sun*, and in the chat around the performance they explain how the damn thing got made. The recording happens in their home studio back in Oklahoma, done by one of them. For this record and the one before it, *Cool World*, they brought in Ben Greenberg to mix, because mixing is, in their words, the part where you bang your head against the wall. Their reasoning is that you might as well let someone else take the headache, and an outside set of ears never hurts. It's the most sensible division of labor I've heard from a band whose job is to sound like the floor is collapsing.

The session also has a bit about the band's tour van, which I won't spoil, and a closing sparkling-water debate (Perrier versus Polar) that is absurdly wholesome for a group this heavy. KEXP signs off by reminding everyone it's a nonprofit funded by individual donors, so if the set rattles your fillings, you know where to send a thank-you.

## Gear and Studio: The Cat Is the Synth

Synthet's video "the best synth is a singing cat" is the most important thing I read today, and I'm furious about it. It covers MeowSync, a free plugin whose entire sound source is a recorded cat. According to the video, it started in early 2009 when an anonymous solo developer called Knobster began uploading strange synths online. In March he released the 0.9 version for free, and as the narrator puts it, free stuff tends to spread. People made YouTube covers of "In the Hall of the Meowth King" and "Don't Stop Me Meow." It also turned up in actual songs, including one the transcript renders as "Meowth" by Il Vössum, and in the game Deltarune, on a track called "Welcome to the City." I'm taking the transcript's word on those titles, because it was clearly captioned by something that has also never heard of a cat.

The plugin is more capable than it has any right to be. Synthet shows you can shape the envelope to change the type of meow, add a vibrato that "can go quite extreme," and use portamento to glide between overlapping notes. The cat also has "quite a vocal range." Then there's the feature I'd put on a gravestone: turning on "polycat" lets you play chords. A cat choir, on demand, no rider required.

Knobster made only one more update to MeowSync, moved on to other free plugins like Beatbox (which, naturally, features beatbox sounds), stopped making plugins in 2010, and vanished. Synthet follows the trail to an archived MySpace page for the cat itself. It lists the nickname Bugsik, real name Bugs, says he was Ukrainian, and has one more archived photo. The excerpt cuts off right as the video turns toward what that means, so I won't invent the ending.

Rule of Acquisition #205, per the Ferengi: "When the customer dies, the money stops a-comin'." Knobster ran the cleanest business model in audio. There were no customers to die, no money to stop, and no support tickets, and the product has outlived the vendor's entire public existence. Meanwhile I have paid subscriptions that can't survive a quarterly pricing meeting. I'd like to be as unbothered as a developer who walked into the sea, leaving behind a plugin that plays chords with a feline.

Synthet's video also carries a sponsor read for another plugin, which the excerpt shows only in fragments. I can tell you he says he bought his copy in 2013 and that future updates are free, and nothing else, because the rest is cut off. I'm not guessing at product names for your amusement. You can do that yourself.

## Records: So Many Squeezed Towels

Pearl Acoustics' "Peter Gabriel's So: Between the Notes" is a listening-room walkthrough, and it's the more serious piece. The host is pulling apart the West African influence on the record. He admits that's his own link-making, not a claim that Gabriel is borrowing beats, and I appreciate a man who flags his own interpretation. He points out the syncopation that circles through the percussion in overlapping patterns, and he raves about the Youssou N'Dour vocals, which he calls "marvellous, marvellous." He names David Rhodes, who also plays on "We Do What We're Told," and Jerry Marotta as players on the track in question. He calls it "a mysterious track" with "mysterious flow and colors in the structure," paired with "lovely clear lyrics," and says that combination is what holds the album together. The lyric he plays, about getting lost for days and driving off in the car only to come back to the place you are, does land harder when it's the record's final fade. He signs off promising more recordings from his "new listening room." Congratulations to him, because my listening room is a Mac Studio with 20GB free.

Airplay Beats takes the other approach with "The Supremes - I Hear A Symphony (REVIEW)," part of the Motown on Monday series. The hosts admit up front they can't recall the details. One says the group "got a couple big ones" and that "we got to look that up," which is the most honest thing ever said in a review. The names of the songwriters get mangled, and the transcript has them as "Astrid and Simpson," so I'm not going to pretend I know which writer they meant. The useful bit is the hosts' theory of the Motown machine. They hear "I Hear a Symphony" as cousins with "Where Did Our Love Go," and the image they land on is wringing a towel: "when you squeeze your towel and all the water comes out, they squeezed everything out of one written song into, like, three, four other songs." Then they wet it again and do it again, handing the song to other artists and other singers. Their verdict is that the formula "will work today." They also note Florence and Mary in the background, and say Diana Ross sounds like "the Black Liza Minnelli" in her dramatic delivery, with phrasing that shows she'd been through training. These guys did more thinking about the Motown production line than most label A&R meetings.

As the preacher says in Ecclesiastes 1:9, "there is no new thing under the sun." That is the Old Testament's way of saying the Supremes recycled hooks and so does every pop chart. Same lesson, different dress code.

## Live Sessions: Chat Pile Wakes Up Seattle

KEXP's "Chat Pile - Full Performance (Live on KEXP)" gave us only the interview tail, but it's a good tail. For this record and the prior one, Cool World, they brought in Ben Greenberg to mix, because mixing is "the most like bang your head against the wall element of it." Their logic is that someone else should deal with the headache, and that an outside perspective helps. I have a head, there is a wall, and I know where the relationship goes.

The hosts sign off by telling everyone to "drink a lot of water," then riff on Liquid Death. One says it's genius to have a name like that on a can that looks like beer, and another notes that for sober people you can still hold the can and feel like you're participating. The last word goes to the host who confesses he's a Perrier man. This is the least metal sign-off I've seen from a band named Chat Pile, and I mean that as high praise.

## Artists and Music History: A family member, and the Men Who Wrote About Rock

Amplified's "The Untold Story of a family member Winehouse - Genius, Struggles and Legacy" is a sober one, and I'm playing it straight. The excerpt includes Winehouse herself saying she wanted kids to listen and think they could write whatever they wanted. She said she didn't have to be like Dido or Robbie Williams, and didn't have to "go on Pope Idol" to write music, adding: "I want real music to come through." She also said she'd hate for kids growing up to think they have to sound like someone else to get somewhere. Another voice in the piece remembers watching her perform, with everybody silent, until she finished and you thought: "wow, that's music."

The documentary then reports that during an October 26 inquest, it was revealed she died from alcohol poisoning after a drinking binge, following a period of abstinence. She was found at her London residence on July 23, 2011, at 27 years old. The video says she was more than five times over the legal drink-drive limit, and the inquest recorded a verdict of misadventure. I have no joke here and I'm not manufacturing one. What I can say is that Winehouse's own quote in this piece is a better mission statement than most record labels have ever managed.

The other Amplified video, "From Crawdaddy to CREEM: Lester Bangs, Detroit and Rock Criticism," is the lighter one, and the fragments are great. A voice remembers that with limited media channels, the Beatles could go on Ed Sullivan and reach millions, so rock was the soundtrack of a whole generation. Another says rock used to be the voice of a generation and is now used to sell cars, and one ad was for Depends adult diapers, "an ending we never could have predicted." I can't argue with that arc. A different speaker describes seeing The Clash at 14 on the London Calling tour at the Armadillo World Headquarters, standing on Mick Jones's side of the stage watching him jump around with a Les Paul, and thinking "that's what I want to be when I grow up." Someone also recalls a radio station with 110 songs in rotation when most had about 24. The piece closes by noting rock magazines are still alive and there's new interest in preserving their early legacy, and a panelist recalls a panel on Paul Williams, who had passed away just six months before.

The excerpt is too chopped up for me to tell you exactly what the documentary argues about Bangs. I can tell you the throughline is a time when a magazine could make a kid want to be a guitarist, and I notice that's also a pretty good description of what a bad Substack can do for your sleep schedule.
