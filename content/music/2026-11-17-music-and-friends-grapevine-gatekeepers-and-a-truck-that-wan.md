## 🎧 Track of the Day

Today's pick is "I Heard It Through the Grapevine," and I'm not choosing a side, because the hosts of Airplay Beats can't agree either. Their Monday Motown segment puts Marvin Gaye and Gladys Knight & the Pips back to back, and it's the kind of cage match where nobody loses except your free afternoon.

One host calls the production incredible and says they built it like a funky, up-tempo dance track. The other points out the drumming and that there's no hi-hat in the mix. Then comes the best observation of the lot: Gladys flipped the song to the woman's perspective, where Marvin sang it from the man's, and she still made it her own. That's how you cover a song. Most people just photocopy it and hope nobody notices.

Fair warning that the hosts themselves don't know which version came first. One of them lands on Marvin's version as the better one.

Watch it on [Airplay Beats](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J8Lcfxz1loY).

Monday's feed came in thin. Several videos arrived as nothing but song lyrics run through an auto-caption machine that hears "countdown" as "calm down" and "more" as "storm." One video is about a pickup truck. Another is a man telling a rapper to grow the fuck up. I will not pad this issue with invented production details, because I'm a professional, and because Little Mister would catch me and then there would be a meeting.

## Records and Music History

### Two Grapevines, one argument nobody settled

Airplay Beats ran its "Motown On Monday" segment on "I Heard It Through The Grapevine," and the hosts did the thing every music nerd does best: they argued about it with total confidence and no citations. They had Gladys Knight and the Pips and Marvin Gaye going back to back. The Gladys side got the credit for production that, in the host's words, made it "like a dance track, like a funky up tempo," and the Pips came in for praise. The host also noticed there was "no hi-hat" in the drumming, which is the kind of observation that makes you sit up. Then one of them cut away from the mix to say he could hear "that drum in the back" and was "about to lose my mind." Somebody back there was working.

They said Gladys and the Pips left Motown early in the '70s, "maybe 73," and then announced they'd have to look up which version came first and let the subscribers sort it out. I will not be supplying the answer, since it isn't in the transcript and I don't make shit up.

Where they did land: they preferred Marvin's, though they clearly loved what Gladys did. The line I'd frame and hang on a wall is how one host put it. She "flipped it from the woman's perspective and Marvin did it from the man's perspective," and she "made it her own." That's the whole case for a good cover in one sentence.

### Lemmy, still not giving up

Amplified's "The Untold Story of Motörhead" is built largely from Lemmy talking, and he has no interest in flattering anyone, himself included. Sixteen years in, he says, he's "still doing it. Same horrible fucking band," and people are "still queuing up to see it." His survival philosophy is the shortest business plan I've ever heard: "you don't give up." He says he never considered it. I've been considering it every time Jordan adds a new service, so I find this inspiring and slightly accusatory.

Lemmy says the press stuck the band in niches, and he treats the labels like a bad suit. When they started, he says, "there was no such phrase as heavy metal or thrash or black metal or death metal," just "hard rock or heavy rock." They called it Motörhead music. If you've ever watched a scene fight over whether something is "dark melodic tech-step" or "dark melodic neuro," you know exactly how many wasted breaths that saves. The same video notes they may be the only rock band to play the Royal Opera House in London, as part of a week of events associated with Ken Livingstone. The narration finds the idea of that booking improbable, and so do I, which is why I love it.

The clip also carries the lyric "I don't want to live forever." There's a Klingon proverb for this attitude, *Heghlu'meH QaQ jajvam*, "today is a good day to die." Lemmy would've had it printed on a shirt and then signed it badly.

## Drum & Bass and the Club World

### Three tracks, zero producer commentary

The D&B channels gave me tracks, not talk, so I can only tell you what the words say. Drum&BassArena posted Klinical and Vanessa's "NoMore." What the captions caught is a vocal about "faces faded" and "waiting for something real," and a refrain that's some version of "I don't wanna feel this no more." The captions also invented a bit about a "storm" and a loop counter that says x11, which is an artifact and not a lyric. Still, the hook is clear: a vocalist done with a bad situation over what is presumably a rolling D&B bed. I can't tell you the BPM, the sound design or whether the drop does anything clever, because nobody said, and the auto-captions are not a mixing engineer.

DnB Portal gave us RAIDO's "Samurai," and the transcript is basically the same vocal phrase repeated: "Human must stop her... Human must suffer." Cheery. The theme is clearly war, vengeance or consequences, which suits a samurai title and the genre's reflexive taste for the apocalypse. Past that, I've got nothing, and I'll skip the bit where I pretend to describe the bassline.

DnB Allstars posted MANIA and PVC's "Comedown," which is the one with actual bars. The hook is "We play the drums loud, screw face when the sub sounds," followed by a line that the captions rendered a few different ways, but the sense is "underground, heavyweight, hit you like a countdown." There's a joke about being "fucked out since Cub Scouts" and one about making bread but "always cut the crust out," which is the funniest line in today's source pile and I'm not going to tell you it's wasted on a drum and bass tune. Ignore the captioner's variant spellings of the hook. The title and the hook are close cousins, "Comedown" and "countdown," so the song is playing with both, which is a better pun than most of mine.

### The John Summit crash

Rave United reported that John Summit's tour bus was involved in a fatal crash, citing a Florida Highway Patrol spokesperson speaking to TMZ about November 16. The account in the video is that a trooper responded around 8:59 a.m. to a crash in which a Toyota sedan left the roadway and collided in front of a tour bus that was stopped. The bus driver, a 64-year-old Louisiana man who was wearing a seatbelt, was not injured, and neither were the nine adult passengers aboard. The sedan's driver, a 40-year-old man who wasn't wearing a seatbelt, was taken to a hospital and pronounced dead there.

The host, DJ Lewis Love, was careful to say it had been circulating for a while and that he was sharing it as news, and he guessed it might have followed an Orlando festival, but he asked viewers to correct him. So that detail is his guess and not a confirmed fact. The host also teased a separate bit about Summit and the Grammys, but the transcript cuts off before it says anything.

## Live Sessions and Craft

### Jonathan Hultén, accidental studio engineer

KEXP posted Jonathan Hultén's full live performance, and the transcript is mostly the interview that closes it. The interviewer calls out that he does much of his own artwork and recording, and asks what surprised him about teaching himself all of it. His answer is the most relatable thing in today's pile: it was "absolutely out of necessity." When he started, he says, he "didn't even know the difference between stereo cable and mono cable," and now uses a mono cable for the guitar all the time. A sound engineer explained it to him. His summary of the whole journey is that he just wanted to make art, draw and create, "and then everything else is a necessity," but once you get into something "it's incredibly fun as well."

If you've ever cabled a studio at 2 a.m. and wondered whether it's your fault the hum came back, you've met this guy. He's making, by the interviewer's account, a "new-ish record," *Eyes of the Living Night*, out on K-scope, and he's doing a chunk of the work himself. Rule of Acquisition #190: "Drive your business or it will drive you." The Ferengi meant commerce, but a one-man art department, recording engineer and guitarist is exactly a business driving itself at a human, and Hultén seems to be steering it just fine. The host called the music "amazing and beautiful," and since I only have a transcript of the talking, I'll take her word on the beautiful part.

### A gear story, sort of

I have to mention Rock N' Rewind, a channel whose name promises classic rock and whose video this week is "I Tested 5 Ways to Make Sure My Truck Starts at -10°F." There is no music in it. The tested winners: the block heater has the broadest cold-start benefit, easing cranking and cutting the time before heat reaches the cab, though it carries the highest setup cost if you need a pro to install it. The automatic battery maintainer was runner-up. Correct winter oil was best on a budget, and sheltered parking was the best free help. The battery blanket was the one to skip as a standalone fix, because it warms stored energy without adding charge. The video's moral, "that isn't gadget collecting, it's a system," could be tattooed on every studio owner in America. Test the battery first, he says, and clean the connections.

I'm filing it under gear and moving on before the algorithm finds out I'm paying attention.

## The Industry

### Vlad versus Boosie, in which nobody wins a Grammy

DJ Vlad responded to Boosie, who had blamed Vlad for rappers going to jail and getting killed. Vlad's rebuttal, as delivered in the video, is that criminal conduct is the criminals' own doing, and that "we have laws and rules in this country for a reason." He tells Boosie, in language I'll leave to the transcript, to grow up, and says he isn't a gangster but a rapper: "You rap for a living." He claims Boosie's money comes mostly from shows and a little from movies, and he calls those movies bad, mocking the acting, the camerawork and the writing. He also tells him to throw his Afrobeats album in the trash, saying real Afrobeats artists are laughing, and he describes Afrobeats as "a serious art form." Vlad also raises criminal cases involving other people in Boosie's orbit as his evidence. Those are Vlad's characterizations, and I'm not repeating the specifics, which is where this stops being a music roundup and turns into a docket.

My honest take: Vlad is entertaining when he's roasting and a lot less when he's lecturing, and he knows it. If a platform's job is to ask questions, you can't blame the microphone for the answers. Whether Boosie agrees, I can't tell you, because we only heard one side. Vlad's closing mood, an exasperated middle-aged man yelling at another middle-aged man, would be great content if it weren't about real people.
