## 🎧 Track of the Day

Terry Jack's "Seasons In The Sun" is today's pick, and I'm recommending a song about dying and goddamn well meaning it. The version worth your four minutes is the [Airplay Beats reaction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pGH8N5QrCJY), where two guys hear it fresh and spend the whole time clocking exactly why it works.

The song opens with "Goodbye to you, my trusted friend," then walks through childhood memories of climbing hills and trees and skinning knees. It's a deathbed farewell, which is a hell of a thing to be catchy. The hosts call the production simple but pleasing to the ear, and they single out the verses, where an organ carries the sad stuff on its own. Then the bird line hits and the beat flips. He admits he missed it on first pass, which is the honest reaction of a man who got emotionally ambushed by a pop song.

Their verdict is that the verses made the record, and I'm not going to argue with two guys who heard it cold and still nailed the structure.

Cheaper than therapy, and the bird part is free.

## Craft: How a Verse Lifts, and How a Fire Doesn't

### Terry Jack, as heard by two guys who weren't expecting it

Airplay Beats put out a reaction to "Seasons In The Sun" by Terry Jack, and for a reaction channel the hosts do something unusual: they talk about arrangement. The first thing they clock is the organ in the verses, which they call "simple yet pleasing." Their best observation is the structure. The first half of each verse carries "the sad stuff," as they put it, over that organ. One of them says it "feels like the heavens open up and the sky, the sun's shining." The hook then opens up even further.

That's a real lesson in dynamics, delivered by guys who said "you ready, yep, here we go" a minute earlier. You keep the verse narrow, you let the lyric flip, and you save the width for the hook. Producers charge money for that advice.

They also say the record sounds like late-60s music, a little funky, with an organ that "makes things spooky" and was "definitely an organ that was made in the 60s." I'll take their word on the organ and refuse to litigate the year, because the transcript doesn't give one. The lyric they're reacting to opens with "Goodbye to you, my trusted friend / We've known each other since we were nine or ten," and a few lines later it's "it's hard to die / When all the birds are singing in the sky." A song about dying where the arrangement gets sunnier as the singer gets deader is the most passive-aggressive thing a pop record can do, and I respect it.

Ecclesiastes 1:9 (KJV) says "there is no new thing under the sun." That's a wry fit for a song with "sun" in the title, being discovered fresh by guys who found out an old record can still work on a new listener.

### The firewood video, which is on a music channel

Rock N' Rewind, a channel name that promises classic rock, uploaded "Which Trick Makes One Load of Wood Last All Night at 10°F? 4 Ways Tested." There is no music in it. *Fèihuà*, which is Mandarin from the Firefly crew for "garbage talk," and it's the kindest thing I can say about a stove tutorial in a music digest. Somebody, and I'm looking at you, Little Mister, keeps adding feeds without auditing what they actually deliver.

The tester's conclusion is that dense hardwood gives endurance while softwood gives speed. Dry softwood is "excellent for kindling, quick heat, and shoulder season fires," but a firebox of it holds less fuel mass. Several substantial splits beat one enormous midnight log, because the splits leave flame paths between them. The top-down method improved startup, but "the dense hardwood supplied the reserve."

Softwood is your three-minute single, quick heat and gone. Hardwood is the long record that's still warm at 4 a.m. The video's final advice is to record your own conditions and results, because stove size, chimney draft and wind change everything, and it also insists you follow your stove maker's instructions. That's the same advice every mixing engineer gives, minus the chimney. Here in Burbank, where my outdoor humidity sensor is reading a sticky 85 percent, a 10-degree night is a theoretical concept.

## Artists and History: Deep Purple Eating Itself, as Told by Amplified

Amplified – Classic Rock & Music History posted "Burn to Stormbringer: How Coverdale & Hughes Transformed Deep Purple," and the excerpt I have is mostly the sad end of the story. The setting is a show in Liverpool, "maybe Royal Court," where Hughes "was so out that he didn't know where he was." There were tears afterward. Then one of the older members, rendered in the transcript as "Pace and Lorda" and presumably Paice and Lord, says that's it, no more, I'm out. The narrator calls it "a terrible way for such a great and gracious rock band" to grind to a halt, and mentions that the band "got resurrected again in the 80s."

An unnamed voice in the clip, and I won't guess whose, takes a swipe at the "boys nights" with Tommy and says "it wasn't great." There were "other issues that broke the band up which probably nobody will talk about," and this person won't talk about them either. Which is the most tantalizing non-answer in the whole transcript. You don't get to say "bizarre things going on there" and then close the door.

Purple was "clearly past its best" and "fragmenting," while Ritchie Blackmore was building "a formidable outfit with Rainbow." The man had Ronnie Dio on vocals, who the narrator calls "quite magnificent." The band was "very classically oriented, very much traditional hard rock, while Purple were going off the rails completely." Blackmore "had stolen the march," as the guy with ideas, the vision and the legendary guitar. The narrator's conclusion is that Rainbow were "very quickly going to overtake the ailing Deep Purple."

I'll add my own spin, clearly labeled as mine and not the transcript's. Every band has a Blackmore, the guy who leaves and is better at being himself on his own, and every other member is spending the aftermath being asked about it. Nothing in the excerpt says whether the Coverdale and Hughes era was good or bad musically, so the title's promise of transformation stays unredeemed here.

## The Industry: Hustle, Ruin and What the Camera Catches

### Yella Beezy's media run

The most revealing part of the Yella Beezy transcript isn't the guest list. The interviewer notes that most people would be in bed recovering, while Beezy is on a media run "arm in a sling, bullets still in you, taking medication." Beezy says, "Man, I can't let this shit stop me," and talks about taking care of his body, watching what he eats and keeping the work going. The interviewer calls it "true work ethic."

The cue light is mine, so I'll own it: that is one hell of a promo cycle, and nobody should need to run one that way. 😏

Asked if he has regrets, Beezy answers: "It is what it is. I don't live off regrets." He says he could talk about what he could have or should have done, but "it already happened." It's a blunt, unembellished answer, and in an industry that rewards oversharing, the restraint is the most interesting thing he says.

### Tito Dominguez, Part 9, with no music in it

The other DJ Vlad item is "Tito Dominguez on Getting Arrested by Feds for Drug Smuggling, Facing Life (Part 9)." Nine parts deep and the excerpt still doesn't mention music, so this is a true-crime entry in a music digest, and I'm treating it with the gravity it's owed rather than the sass I'd like. Part 9 of anything is a commitment, even a dark one. 😏

Per his own account, Dominguez faced an automatic life sentence and says he was charged in South Florida, Central Florida, Georgia, Detroit and the Bahamas. A prosecutor, he says, told him, "I would charge you over every state you flew over." He describes being indicted on "historical conspiracy," which he calls hearsay assembled by informants. He says he spent two and a half years in solitary after being accused of ordering a hit on witnesses, an allegation that he says escalated to federal agents and a federal judge. He credits an attorney with turning around the "badass image" prosecutors had built. These are his claims in his words, and I'm reporting them as such.
