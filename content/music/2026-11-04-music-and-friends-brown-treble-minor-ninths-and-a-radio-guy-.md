## 🎧 Track of the Day

Paradise Lost, "Tyrant's Serenade." It's a doom-adjacent guitar record that's been sitting there being gloomy, and I'm sorry to report it's good.

Producer Key of Geebz [took it apart in a reaction video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s31lOVnJuM4), and his big takeaway isn't the riffs. It's the mastering. He loves that the super high end is rolled off, and by his ear it sounds like everything from roughly 11k to 16k has been shaved away. He calls the result "the darkness, the brown color of this track" and says it's phenomenal. He also admits it could just be age eating his top end, which is the most honest thing a mix engineer has ever said out loud. Whether the dulling happened in the mix or as a nudge at mastering, he can't tell. Either way, it works.

The guitars sound great. The lead has a pleasant noisiness and noodles and meanders through nearly the whole song. He singles out the guitar melody as a key part of how the chorus is built, and says it leads into a hook he rates highly.

Put on decent headphones and listen to what a deliberate lack of sparkle sounds like. In a world where every master is a bright, harsh, fatiguing sheet of foil, a record that dares to sound like a dark room is practically an act of rebellion. Your tweeters will survive. They've had worse.

## Music Theory and the Craft

### Rick Beato goes to the cornfield and gets a lesson out of it

Rick Beato's "This Didn't Quite Go As I Planned..." is a rare video where the title is probably the most honest thing in it. The transcript is a fragment, but the fragment has real music in it. Beato is at the Field of Dreams site, and he says he practiced and went to the batting cages beforehand "so I wouldn't completely embarrass myself." Respect. A man who will explain a Neapolitan chord to forty million people still doesn't want to whiff in front of a cornfield.

The theory is the good part. He calls out "the real first key" moment in the score, "where the real melancholy sadness comes in." It's an F sharp to G flat 9 interval, "that minor 9 interval," which he calls "really powerful." That collapses into a major 7th, "same two notes, but now it's G, F sharp." Then it "walks down to that perfect fifth, which is so solid."

A minor ninth and a major seventh are the two most dissonant intervals in the book, both a half step away from an octave. They're about as close as you can get to home without being home. The same two notes get flipped, and the ear feels the grief change shape. Then the line lands on a perfect fifth, the most stable interval there is. It's a cadence in miniature: ache, ache differently, rest. Beato is telling you the sadness in that cue is built out of two notes and a staircase. I'd argue that's more emotionally efficient than most people's entire second album.

He also names the cue he loves most, "The Place Where Dreams Come True." It's the scene where James Earl Jones's Terrence Mann walks into the cornfield, Ray Liotta's Shoeless Joe turns up, and Kevin Costner is watching. He says that as a younger viewer he connected with Ray playing catch with his dad, and that the music "blew me away." Then he says something self-aware and a little devastating. As he's gotten older, he's "become Moonlight Graham," because all he wants is to go there "and hit the ball one more time." His friend Mike, who has worked with him on the channel "forever" and lives near the site, had reserved a couple of days there the year before.

I can't tell you how the video goes sideways, because the excerpt cuts off mid-sentence just as it gets interesting. The title promises chaos and the transcript delivers a preamble. Take that as a note about my sources, not about Beato.

## Records, Mixes, and the Metal Corner

### Key of Geebz meets Paradise Lost, and the treble takes a vacation

Key of Geebz's "Checking Out Paradise Lost Tyrant Serenade Old Composer Reaction" is a producer reacting to the track "Tyrant's Serenade." I'm also working with the end of the video only, so this is what he said in the last stretch.

<!-- satire-image -->
![Paradise Lost turned the treble down and somehow sounds even more doomed. That's the whole point.](/images/satire/2026-11-04-music-and-friends-brown-treble-minor-ninths-and-a-radio-guy-.webp)
*Paradise Lost turned the treble down and somehow sounds even more doomed. That's the whole point.*

His main observation is about the mastering, and I love that he opens by admitting it might be his ears. He says he knows he's getting older and may be "beginning to lose some high end," even though he mixes at low levels, "and that also just happens with age." But he's convinced the effect is on the record. "The mastering on this, I love how there's this treatment where the super high end is really kind of rolled off." He can't say whether it happened in the mix or as a "nudge in mastering." He later guesses it sounds like "a little high-pass filter taking out like 11 to 16k," then immediately concedes it could be his own aging. He calls the result "the darkness, the brown color of this track," and says it was "phenomenal."

I want to flag the slip here, because he said "high-pass filter," and what he's describing is a low-pass filter. A high-pass removes lows and a low-pass removes highs. The speech-to-text may have mangled it, or he may have misspoken mid-flow. That's a hell of a choice in an era when everything gets brightened to survive earbuds and algorithms. A doom-leaning band choosing "brown" over "sparkle" is exactly the right call. Nobody wants airy shimmer on a song called "Tyrant's Serenade." You don't make a villain's love song sound like a toothpaste ad.

On the composition side, he's tracking details in real time. He likes "the nosiness of the lead," wonders aloud whether the bass is "holding out those long tones at the bottom," and says the guitar melody is "such an important part in this chorus construction." Then comes a fair observation: the lead guitar "has been meandering through the whole thing, noodling through the whole song, pretty much." He means it as praise, and so do I. A lead line that wanders under the structure without ever taking over is the difference between a metal record and a guitar clinic.

Beato zooms in to two notes and explains the heartbreak. Geebz zooms out to the frequency balance of the whole master and explains the mood.

## Drum & Bass and the Club World

### Two uploads, very little talk

I'm not going to pretend I have deep analysis here. The sources for both of these are lyrics, with no commentary, no production notes, and no interview.

UKF Drum & Bass posted Koven's "sHow me heAVEn." The vocal, as transcribed, is a long list of definitions of heaven: "a rooftop buzzer sunrise," "swimming in the pools of your eyes," and "a world back home with the streetlights shining down." There's also a line about a "blood-rich moon in the distance." The hook is simply "Show me heaven," repeated until it becomes a mantra. That's a club-friendly structure, since a hook that simple works at 3 a.m. on a dancefloor when nobody remembers the verses. I can't tell you anything about the drums, the bass design, or the drop from a lyric dump, and I won't invent them.

The other is "Fitch - Tonight (Ft. Emile Battour) (Free Download)," posted on the channel labeled RELEVANT DNB. The words are about being locked in, "endless nights," "no moonlight, no sunrise," and finally "Will I break free tonight?" The chorus insists "I'll break free too." The Free Download tag in the title is the useful fact. Free releases are a quiet tradition in the scene, a way to put a track directly into DJ crates without a gatekeeper. That's inference from the title and not something the video says, so treat it accordingly.

Both tracks are about night, escape, and some version of release. That's what club music is for, and hearing two of them back to back is a reminder that the genre's lyrical range is "please let this be the night something changes." I respect the consistency.

## The Industry

### Rosecrans Vic, the meeting email, and the worst kind of text

KROQ's "Rosecrans Vic Talks Power 106, Getting Fired From A Show He Created, & Fumbling $500k" is a conversation about radio, and the firing story is the center of it.

The guest recounts a normal day that went sideways around eight o'clock, when a coworker on another show at Power texted: "you get that staff meeting email mandatory?" Vic hadn't, and he started worrying for the friend, thinking "maybe it's nothing." Then he checked his own inbox and found it. The meeting subject was "by Vic." Whatever dread you've felt opening a calendar invite titled "Quick Sync," that's the grown-up version.

The hosts' response is the part I find most decent. One says that as someone "who's been on radio a bit," it "sucks," and that when people get "the mic stripped away from them," it's their duty to give them a voice.

Details are thin. The title mentions "fumbling $500k," and that is all the transcript tells me about it. I'm not going to guess whether that's a deal, a contract, or a bad investment. The sign-off, in Vic's words, is "I love you, Los Angeles. Thank you for tuning in." I'll admit that, from a man who just got escorted out of a show he created, is a class act.
