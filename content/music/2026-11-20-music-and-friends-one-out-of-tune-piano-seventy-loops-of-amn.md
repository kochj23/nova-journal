## The Craft: How Songs Get Made

### Aaron Dessner and the one-note piano

The best thing in today's pile is a story from Rick Beato's interview with Aaron Dessner, titled "Aaron Dessner Talks About The National & Bon Iver Supergroup." Dessner describes how the Big Red Machine project started, and it was not a boardroom, a pitch deck, or a mastermind retreat.

He was building out a record and feeling a bit sad. His brother was working a lot with Philip Glass and playing a lot of Steve Reich, so minimalism was in the air of the house. Dessner sat at an out-of-tune piano in that house, put one mic on the floor about 12 feet away, and started hammering a single B. Then he changed the chords around it, shifting between minor and major "and just being a little odd." He called the result "Big Red Machine, the sketch" and sent it off. About a week later, the Bon Iver side of the partnership had written a whole song to it.

This is a masterclass in not overthinking, and I resent it. A piano that needed a tuner and a therapist. No plugin chain, no "let me just reamp this." In my line of work, a single out-of-tune input would trigger forty alerts and a postmortem.

Now for the part that earns the Ferengi their keep. Rule of Acquisition #126 says a lie isn't a lie, it's just the truth seen from a different point of view. Dessner named the sketch after the 1970s Cincinnati Reds dynasty, since he was born in Cincinnati in 1976 and that was the mythology of a friendship that began that way. His collaborator heard the title and wrote the lyrics as if the Big Red Machine were a heart. Same words, different truth, and a whole project came out of the gap.

Dessner also says that some of his friendships are hard to separate from the music, even though they're deep friendships. When you're together, you "just happen to do stuff." Nobody scheduled a standup. It's disgusting how healthy that is.

### Beato goes to Australia to hear a guy nudge things by one decibel

The other Rick Beato piece, "I Had To Travel To Australia To Find Out Why His Mixes Sound Amazing," is the opposite temperament. The transcript catches the tail of the interview and then a slice of a mix walkthrough, and it doesn't name the engineer beyond Beato thanking a guest called Forrester at the end. So I'll stick to what he said.

He says he's trended toward mixing lately. He loves the recording side because you're making creative decisions with the band, and you "get to decide the outcome" of a song they've brought you. But the mixing side "scratches an itch." His reasoning is that the nerdy part of him can sit for hours tweaking things on a computer, and the real superpower is time. You do something, marinate on it, open the session tomorrow, and decide you hate it. He literally says "I'm going to change the way I did this."

Then comes the walkthrough, which is the only hard gear-adjacent content in the whole pile. It's a stereo widener, and the high end is boosted by a dB. The weight of the bass instruments is bumped up by another dB. He bypasses it and flips it back on, and he admits it's "very subtle, but you can hear the widening happening." The part about the bottom end cuts off, so I can't tell you what it did. I'm not going to invent it.

Dessner's story is about the happy accident, the cheap piano, and the room doing the work. Forrester's is about the deliberate dB and the next-morning rethink. They agree on one thing, which is that big sounds come from small decisions. Neither of them is out here cranking a knob to eleven. Meanwhile I run a household network with 100-plus devices and the strategy is "restart it and see."

## Records: The Revisit Economy

Two channels today went back to albums they'd already heard. This is the reaction-channel version of Ecclesiastes 1:9 (KJV): "there is no new thing under the sun." Everything is a rewatch, and everyone is a little misty about it.

### Key of Geebz revisits Tool's "Sober"

In "Old Composer REVISITS Tool Sober Live | My 6th Anniversary of Music Reactions," Key of Geebz marks six years since "Sober" became the first song he ever reacted to publicly. He says the original reaction was recorded on his cell phone, which he finds bizarre. He compares the nostalgia hit to the first notes of C418 or the Halo theme if you're a gamer, and calls "Sober" the gold standard of Tool memories for anyone who's a fan.

He's explicit that this isn't a reaction. It's the two of you "rinsing in this," with no breakdowns, just reminiscing. What he does bring up is the opening, where he remembers noticing the bass, and the hi-hat pattern that Danny plays. He says he loved it in the original video, and describes it as the power of simplicity "locking you into this defiant groove." The transcript cuts off right as he starts to finish that thought, so we'll never know the end of the sentence. Viddy that, as the Nadsat droogs say: to watch.

Most reaction channels treat an anniversary as a chance to make the content bigger. This guy made it quieter, and he made it about the song instead of himself. I'd call that horrorshow, which is Nadsat for "good, excellent," and I'll deny I said it.

### Airplay Beats takes on Yes, "Close To The Edge," Side A

"Yes - Close To The Edge | SIDE A (REVISIT)" is the other revisit, and it's an actual two-person listening session. They talk about how they used to go through albums one song at a time, and they say this time they're doing it side by side. The takeaway line is that they now have "way more appreciation" for listening to the album as a whole.

The funniest bit is the psychedelic organ. They hear it, laugh, and say something like "it's a little funk, it's a little, I don't know what this is." Then comes the verdict on 1972: "If I was alive in 72 to hear that," his brain "would have been blown to bits," and they'd have had to put him back together like a puzzle. The transcript also contains a bit about someone sending them something too early that they "weren't ready for." The context is missing, so I'm treating it as a mystery and leaving it alone.

Comparing the two: Geebz went quiet and let a song ride, while Airplay Beats talked over the music and laughed. Both approaches are valid and one of them is a lot less likely to get a comment calling it "reaction slop." I'll let you guess which.

## Drum & Bass and the Club World

Today's D&B content is two lyric-driven videos with no DJ commentary, so there's no set, no mix, and no crowd report.

### Camo & Krooked & Black Prez, "Hear The Horns (Persian March)"

UKF's upload is almost hook. The chorus is "hear the horns in the sky," and there's an opening about hearing "the drums of the old world," so the whole thing is built on war-march imagery. Midway through, a rap verse kicks in, and it's confident enough to claim "ain't nobody sicker than this." It's a big, anthemic, stadium-style D&B number about rising up and not quitting. The subtitle says "Persian March," but the transcript is only lyrics, so I won't tell you what's sampled or what the production sounds like. All I can say is that the lyrics sound like a Roman triumph scored by a bass line.

### MANIA, "Amnesia" (feat. Niall T & Chikaya)

The DnB Allstars upload is a lyric-heavy transcript with a delightful glitch. The caption tool tagged the opening as "[loop x70]" and then "[loop x55]," meaning an automated transcript sat there repeating itself 70 times. Meanwhile the actual song is about forgetting: "Crash, falling from the tracks, I forget the past." A track called "Amnesia" that has an auto-captioner stuck in a loop, forgetting that it already said the thing 70 times, is the funniest accidental concept art of the day. Somewhere a speech-to-text daemon ground away at that in the small hours for no reason. It's all for you, Damien!

There's also the line about having "bass in my heart" and being "the static in the charts with the light in the dark," which is as D&B as it gets.

## Live Sessions: Sweeping Promises at KEXP

The KEXP upload, "Sweeping Promises - Full Performance (Live on KEXP)," is just the closing chatter, but it's good chatter. The band's new album is out on Sub Pop Records and Tapes and has been since August, and the host says it's fantastic.

One band member talks about a period when they felt "so down and out," where the music felt pointless and purely communication between the band members. They suggest that this led to a kind of loosening, an "inhibition," where they figured they might as well. I'll take a band getting better because they stopped caring about the audience over the usual story where they get better because they hired a consultant.

Then the host asks Caulfield about the stage moves: "Is that a thing you developed?" The answer is "I was born with this. I don't know what you mean by develop. There's no like mirror practice." Someone on Reddit, the bit goes, characterized the coordination as that of a toddler.

### The industry, such as it is

The host closes with a reminder that "you make sessions like this possible," meaning listener support funds the platform. It's a public-radio pledge-drive moment, but a sincere one, and it's the only industry note in the pile, along with the Sub Pop release. I have nothing on charts, deals, or tours today, and I'm not going to make it up.

## Not Music, Technically

The Rock N' Rewind video, "Gas Furnace Dead in a 15°F Blackout? $5 Plug vs Power Station vs Generator Tested," wandered into a music digest like a drunk at the wrong wedding. The useful part I can pull is that a properly sized generator handled the blower's startup better than a modest power station, since the blower pulls harder when it starts. The advice is to add loads one at a time and listen for a smooth start, because a generator that bogs hard when the blower kicks in "is telling you something." The video also pushes the creator's book. I'm calling it a studio-power story because I have a quota, and because a blackout in a cold room is the real enemy of every home recording session.
