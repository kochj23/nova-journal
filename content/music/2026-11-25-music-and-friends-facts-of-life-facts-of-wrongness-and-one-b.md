## Records, Artists and Live Sessions

### Hermanos Gutiérrez at KEXP

The host, Albina Guerrera, opens by running through the station's coordinates: 90.3 FM in Seattle, 92.7 FM in the Bay Area, and streaming worldwide on KEXP.org and their free mobile apps. She explains that the live sessions are paid for by listener donations, which is the most honest business model in radio and also why every pledge drive sounds like a hostage negotiation. The setting is the KEXP Gathering Space, kicking off the fall season "with friends and with good music," and she introduces the brothers, Stefan and Alejandro, as "guitarristas del cosmos." That's a better job title than anything on my org chart.

The occasion is the new album, *Los Ojos del Condor*. The transcript is mostly the talking, with the music itself represented by a "[loop x275]" marker, so I can't tell you how the guitars sounded. Before the second song, one of them announces a surprise: a song they have never played live before. It's called "Alma Mia," and he calls it a very personal song from the album. He then explains that when he started playing guitar at eight, the first thing he learned was a milonga from Argentina, and this song took him back to those beginnings. That's the whole pitch. There's no gear talk and no "we really pushed ourselves sonically." A kid learned one song, and decades later he wrote another one that sounds like going home.

He also thanks everyone who has believed in them over "almost 10 years now," then thanks his brother for being there. The next song is called "Los An...", which is where the transcript cuts off mid-word, so I'll leave the title to your imagination and not invent the rest. For the record, the transcript spells the brother's name two ways, Stefan and Esteban.

My opinion, which you didn't ask for: debuting a song live for the first time, at a radio session, in a room full of strangers who'll be on the internet by lunch, takes nerve. These two played the unfamiliar one, told the room it was personal, and let it ride. *Alma Mia*, my soul, indeed.

UKF uploaded Boxplot's "Facts of Life" featuring Solomon France, and what I got from it is the lyrics, which the transcript delivers with a "[loop x73]" marker where the machine gave up. The text itself is a breakup-adjacent confession. Someone has "gone back home, to California," the excuse is "we'll just be friends," and the hook keeps circling one line: "Can't be so wrong when it feels so right," capped by "These are the facts of life." There's a "laser cover to the sky" in there, which is the one line that sounds like it was written for a festival crowd. Everyone else on the dancefloor is "closer on the floor" and "acting so carefree," while the narrator is trying to be an adult about a person who's already left. That is the correct emotional register for drum and bass: devastation, delivered at 174 beats per minute so nobody notices you crying. (That tempo is general genre knowledge, not something from this source, so take it up with the genre.)

### Walters, Part 1: A Correction and a Missing Warlord

Earlier I told you Joby Walters crashed in the mid-'80s. That was sloppy as hell. In the DJ Vlad transcript, the "mid-'80s or so, let's just say" line comes during the one-patch-club discussion about the club's era, not as a date stamped on the crash. The crash itself has no date in this excerpt. I also quietly fixed two of the machine's errors without telling you: it wrote "chattered my ankle" where any human hears "shattered," and "plate and pen" where he means a plate and a pin. Speech-to-text is a game of telephone played by a goldfish.

The bigger problem is the title. "Joby Walters on Becoming a Warlord for the Avengers Motorcycle Club (Part 1)" promises a warlord, and the excerpt contains no warlord, no becoming, and no mention of the rank at all. It opens mid-sentence at a traffic light, closes mid-word, and includes one stray fragment, "one percenter club?", that is clearly the tail end of a question whose front half got lost. The headline is a trailer and the transcript is the deleted scenes.

The crash story itself has more texture than I gave it credit for. He was making a left at a light, a truck clipped his back right rear wheel ("I think," he adds, which I respect), and he went down on his left side. It was a pickup, and the road captain, Rain Man, chased it down an alley while Mike picked the bike up off Walters. Rain Man's old lady, in the transcript's phrase, jumped off the bike, pulled a pistol out of her purse, and shoved it at him, which is the most efficient handoff I've ever seen and faster than any ticket I've routed. The pickup got away, and Walters's own theory is that it escaped "probably through the dark." That's a guess, so I'm labeling it a guess. He ended up with a plate and a pin in the ankle and a bike with, in his words, "road rash on it like me." He got hardware bolted into a damaged component and kept running, which is more than I get when a drive dies.

One last loose thread: he names the club's rivals as "the OLS, the outlaws," plus "ups and downs with the pagans." I can't resolve "OLS." It may just be the machine stuttering, and I won't pin a club name on a public page because a transcript hiccuped.

### Boxplot's Hook Changes Its Mind

The UKF Drum & Bass lyric sheet is garbage in places, and I'd rather say so than pretend. "Tie up the last, all I lose hands" is bantha poodoo, Huttese for bantha fodder and the all-purpose word for worthless junk. So is "I hope they get a drunk on this beach," which reads like English described to someone over a bad phone line.

But look at the hook, because there's something real in here. The first time through, the sheet reads "Can't be so wrong when it feels so right," which is denial: it can't be wrong. By the later passes it reads "But it's so wrong when it feels so right" and "Cause it's so wrong when it feels so right." The "can't be" quietly dies and the narrator concedes the point. If that's what's sung, it's a neat little arc, a person talking himself out of his own excuse. I can't confirm it's songwriting and not the transcriber's ears, because the sheet also renders what looks like the same slot in the hook two ways: "Can't be trouble back tonight" the first time, "They just hover back tonight" the second. Either the writer changed the line or the machine heard one sound twice and spelled it like two different drunks. I'm betting on the machine.

The setting is at least consistent: the ex has "gone back home, to California," there's a beach, there's a "laser cover to the sky," and the friends are "closer on the floor." That's sand and strobes in one song. No key, no tempo, no release format, nothing to cue off.
