## Records, Artists and Music History

### The Stranglers, or How to Be Hated Professionally

The Trash Theory episode "Golden Brown & The Most Hated Band In UK Punk," part of the channel's New British Canon series, is the meatiest thing today. Its thesis is that "Golden Brown" is the strangest thing The Stranglers ever made and also their biggest hit. The video calls it an atypical song, the one most people could hum, their TikTok song. Then it calls it exactly as awkward and thematically ugly as their earlier work. It has a pretty melody and an irregular time signature, and the song is about "a grotty man trapped in a narcotic swirl," observing his own helplessness while smacked out of his gourd. The video's point is that the band made that presentable enough for radio. A heroin song disguised as a waltz-adjacent lullaby that grandmothers hum in supermarkets is the best prank in British pop.

In May 2020, as a tribute after Greenfield's passing, the YouTuber Lawrence Mason built a version of "Golden Brown" in the style of Dave Brubeck's "Take 5." It has racked up 17 million views and led plenty of people to believe the Stranglers' song had been a cover all along. The answer is 17 million, it's suspiciously precise, and it explains nothing about why the internet needed a jazz band to tell it a punk band wrote something clever.

The Green Fender Bass the first two albums were recorded on is now owned by Clayton, according to the video. Simple Minds, The Damned, Manic Street Preachers, Inspiral Carpets and Die Toten Hosen are all named as bands who've declared their fandom. Alaska had to settle out of court because their 1994 song "Waking Up" sounded too much like "No More Heroes." The Suda51 game series No More Heroes was named after that same song. So the most hated band in UK punk got a lawsuit, a video game franchise, and a jazz tribute out of it. The video's list of the people they made enemies of is a work of art: punks, the press, women, record labels, and various local authorities. I was going to say that's an impressive range, but that's everybody.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #185 says there are three known ways to ruin yourself: gambling is the fastest, women are the sweetest, and banks are the most reliable. The Stranglers, per this episode, found a fourth, which was being The Stranglers. It worked fantastically, creatively speaking.

### Pink Floyd, Before the Moon Was Dark

Amplified's "The Untold Story of Pink Floyd's Psychedelic Beginnings" comes at a different kind of ruin. The clip we have opens in the middle of the band's post-Syd scramble. They were on the verge of splitting up soon after parting company with Barrett, and the video argues that getting Gilmour in went some way to saving their own bacon. It also says Roger Waters found his feet as a songwriter, and that *A Saucerful of Secrets* was a significant success that gave Floyd a launch pad for far greater things. The record is the pivot the whole career turns on.

The voices in the video describe him as sparkly-eyed, witty, funny and charming, and then they say what honest people say about Syd: as to why he went how he did, nobody knows. I like that the video doesn't pretend otherwise. Its case for his genius rests on originality. Rolling a ball bearing down a guitar string, one speaker says, is probably not the most technically difficult thing, since anybody could do it, but doing it first and wanting to do it is "Syd Barrett's innovation." That line is a good compact argument about what invention really is.

Syd is "not under the ground, the cold, hard ground." He's out there doing the painting he trained for at Cambridge, living through his own legend. The transcript cuts off just as it gets to the royalty cheques, which is the most Pink Floyd place to cut off.

## The Craft, and the Sample Desk

### The Stranglers Video Is Also a Hardware Story

Trash Theory's "Golden Brown & The Most Hated Band In UK Punk | New British Canon" ends on a legacy ledger, and the ledger has an actual object in it. According to the video, a man the captions call Clayton now owns the Green Fender Bass on which "Bunnell" (the captions' spelling, and they've earned our distrust) recorded The Stranglers' first two albums. For record collectors, that's the interesting line in the whole video. A specific bass tied to two specific albums, with a named owner, is the kind of ghost story the vintage world runs on. The video gives no price, no provenance and no history of how it changed hands, so don't ask me, because I don't invent that shit.

The influence list also works as a listening map. The video names Simple Minds, The Damned, Manic Street Preachers, Inspiral Carpets and Die Toten Hosen as fans, and says the band has been covered by "a huge variety of artists." Two of its tangents point back at the same record. Alaska settled out of court because their 1994 song "Waking Up" resembled "No More Heroes," and the Suda51 game series No More Heroes borrowed the title. If "Golden Brown" got you in the door, the video's own evidence says to go down the hall to "No More Heroes," where a lawsuit and a video game franchise both went looking.

The video says he made it in May 2020 "as a tribute to Greenfield's passing." The 17 million views came from a memorial that turned into a phenomenon, which is an uncomfortably good outcome for a grief project. The video's explanation for why people assumed the original was a cover is that "Golden Brown" is "an oddity" in the catalogue, "a completely atypical song" and "their TikTok song." For listeners, the takeaway is that the hit is the exception and the rest of the catalogue is the grotty bit. Also, the transcript logs the band's music playing in repeated stretches, 14 loops in one and 27 in another. Either the video lets the records talk or my transcription software got stuck on a skipping CD.

### Syd and the Ecclesiastes Problem

Amplified's "The Untold Story of Pink Floyd's Psychedelic Beginnings" makes an argument for players that the main piece mentioned but didn't unpack. The speaker admits that rolling a ball bearing down a guitar string "is not probably the most technical and difficult thing to do," and that "probably anybody could do it." The genius, the video says, is "to do it first and to want to do it." The video also says Syd "didn't really have a template to follow," and that the same goes for his guitar playing, which was "all incredibly original."

That's a direct fight with Ecclesiastes 1:9 (KJV): "there is no new thing under the sun," meaning everything has been done before and will be again. The speaker's rebuttal is a ball bearing. For anyone holding a guitar, the lesson is that the video isn't praising chops. It's praising nerve, the willingness to do the dumb-sounding thing before anyone has told you it's allowed. The Stranglers video made a cousin of that argument about "Golden Brown." The two videos agree that "nobody else would have done this" is a stronger compliment than "nobody else could have played this."

The video's yardstick for genius is generous too: "someone who makes a profound effect on the lives of others." By that definition, the guy who invented the snooze button is a genius, and so is whoever greenlit my hardware budget. The video also calls Syd one of the few geniuses the English pop scene produced, and says he "came from nowhere" and "produced these blazing incidents, these songs that are still so fondly regarded." Nothing in the clip names those songs, so I can't either.

The video says Floyd were "splitting up soon after they'd parted company with" Syd, that bringing in Gilmour "went some way to saving their own bacon," and that Waters "found his feet as a songwriter." It names *A Saucerful of Secrets* as "a significant success" that gave the band "a launch pad from which they went on to far greater things." Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #6 is "Never let family stand in the way of opportunity." The Ferengi meant relatives and ledgers. A band is also a family, and the video says this one grew bigger after the lineup change. The video is gentle about the human cost, and the rule is not, which is why it's a Ferengi rule and not a Floyd lyric.

### Eyedress Is a Rescue Song in a Nice Coat

The main piece called "Vivienne Westwood Tears" a tender luxury love song. Look closer at the lyric sheet and it's also an extraction plan. The song opens on "Wake up, filthy" and closes on "Wake up, Sophie, I see the pain in your eyes," so the whole thing is framed as a wake-up call. In between, the narrator promises "I still got your back," "I'll always protect you," and "It's not like before." Then he tells her, "You getting tired of it now, I think that's enough, I think it's time to split ways," with a line about her guy not being around. It's a comfort song with an exit strategy tucked in the verse, and the sunrise chorus is the getaway car: "Ride to sunrise. Everything will be alright. Just come with me."

A designer shirt becomes a tissue, a Mercedes becomes a place to cry, and the song treats both as proof of safety. The devotion vows also escalate past wardrobe, with claims of being "killed for you" and "done for you" that sit awkwardly beside a shirt that costs more than my power bill. That's the song's trick, softness wrapped around something intense. The sources are only lyrics, so I can't tell you what the beat does, how it's mixed, or whether it sounds like the sunrise it keeps promising.

### The Cosmic Thread, and a Band I Can't Describe

Read the day's lyrics side by side and a pattern pops out that nobody planned. The TRACKLIB "Sample Breakdown: King Geedorah - Fazers" scraps give us "intelligent and well trained astronauts," "King Ghidorah, take me to your leader," and a line about someone "rhyming longer than Sigma." Floyd's record is called *A Saucerful of Secrets*. The Eyedress song rides to sunrise. The Slift set talks about "a banker from the distant star," "a theater by the stars," and "Earth rises" from the ocean. Four unrelated sources and every one of them is staring at the sky. The sources don't say why, and I won't blame the moon.

It's a map with only the legend attached.

The KEXP session is the opposite problem. What I can trust is the imagery. The narrator is an outsider throughout: "the stranger," a "banker from the distant star," someone they "call Nile." He lives "in the village by the sea" and "light[s] fires in the valley." There's a mayor, "heresy," "the evil can't go," and "I'm falling for the blood." Read cold, it sounds like a passage from the Necronomicon, the flesh-bound book from Evil Dead that you absolutely should not read aloud unless you want demons. I viddied this transcript. That's Nadsat, the teen slang in *A Clockwork Orange*, for "saw," and what I saw was mostly cal, Nadsat for crap, though the crap belongs to the auto-captions and not to the band.

### Rainwater, Handled Carefully

Rainwater says Roy Lee told him that Yella Beezy and his people shot him. When he's asked whether this has come out in court, his answer, per the transcript, is that it "just came out in a big interview." Nothing in the pile mentions a charge, a ruling, or any finding.

The details the main piece skipped are about how confusing the aftermath was. Rainwater describes calling Roy Lee's pastor, asking him to come pick him up and Mo3, and asking him to "go pray up for Roy Lee." The pastor called back about 20 minutes later saying Roy Lee was gone. Then, per Rainwater, Roy Lee himself picked up the phone and said "what's up?" Rainwater told him, "hey, they said he dead." In a single call, a man went from being mourned to answering his own phone. Rainwater says he never talked to Mo3 that day again.

The medical thread is just as hard to follow. Roy Lee was headed for surgery, then rehab, with a hopeful line that he'd "be back walking in no time." Later there's a comedy show, a hotel, someone struggling to breathe, an ambulance, and a blood clot that "went to his lungs." The captions blur whose hotel it was, but it reads as the sequel to the shooting. When Rainwater is asked how he felt hearing the allegation, the answer is hesitant and fragmented: "I didn't know what to think then." The clip cuts off mid-sentence, right as he gets to whether Roy Lee saw the shooter.
