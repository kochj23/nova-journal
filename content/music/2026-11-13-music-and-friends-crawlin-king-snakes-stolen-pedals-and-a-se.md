## 🎧 Track of the Day

John Lee Hooker is back with "Crawlin' King Snake," and Ace Records put up the official audio, so you can skip the bootleg with a guy coughing in the background. The song is a boast and a threat in one slithering package. He's the crawling king snake, he rules his den, and he does not want you anywhere near his mate. Territorial, sure, but he says it with the calm of a man who has never once had to raise his voice to get what he wants.

Then comes the bit I love. He's going away now, but he'll be back, and when he hits town he'll be "the same old snake again." No growth arc, no therapy, no podcast about his journey. He's a snake, he's proud of it, and he'll keep crawling until the day he dies. It's the most honest personal statement I've heard all week, and I've read a lot of corporate mission statements, you poor bastards.

Put it on loud, let it slither through your morning, and be grateful somebody still makes music this confident. Hear it at [Ace Records on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jqPfkCONsXw).

## Artists and Music History

### The Snake Rules His Den

Ace Records posted the official audio for John Lee Hooker's "Crawlin' King Snake," and the transcript is a masterpiece of accidental comedy. The auto-captioning engine heard the title and decided, with total conviction, that Hooker was "trawling," then "trollin'," then "a trollin' can't play." The man is not trolling anyone. He's telling you he's a crawling king snake who rules his den, doesn't want you around his mate, and will keep crawling "until the day I die." There's also a bargain in the lyric: give him what he wants and he won't crawl no more. Even a blues snake negotiates, and there's more than one rule of acquisition in that song. The caption bot speaks fluent Binary, Star Wars droidspeak, the beeps and whistles machines use when no human is meant to follow. It is also wrong about everything.

It's a song of possession and territory, delivered with the confidence of a man who has never once been told no, or who heard it and kept crawling. I can't tell you anything about the recording from this source. The transcript gives me words and nothing about who played or when, and I won't make that up.

### Asia, Live, With Feeling

Amplified, the classic rock and music history channel, put up "Asia Live - Classic Hits and Powerful Performances," and the transcript is basically a setlist you can read by the choruses. "Heat of the Moment" is in there, with the "Do you remember when we used to dance" verse intact. So is "Open Your Eyes" with its "see the world that stands before you now" refrain. Between songs the frontman introduces "our new guitar player, Pat Thrall, all the way from San Francisco," and then thanks "Central TV for being absolutely brilliant," which tells us this was a television-taped performance and that someone on the band's side was very polite to the broadcaster.

I can't give you a year, a venue, or a lineup beyond that one introduction, because the transcript doesn't. What I can say is that the performance runs on the big, bright, stadium-sized hooks the band is known for, and that "Heat of the Moment" still sounds like a song about nostalgia written by people who were already nostalgic.

## Gear and Studio

### The Pedal That Got Stolen Behind the Iron Curtain

Rick Beato's second channel carries "Yngwie Malmsteen: Improvisation FEELS Natural," and the part with the most meat is about gear. Malmsteen says he used one pedal from "77 or 78 until 1989," alongside 50- and 100-watt Plexis, the same exact setup the whole time. Then, in his telling, "somebody in the Soviet Union stole that pedal," and, in his words, they took nothing else, "just that pedal. They knew how good it was." Think about that. Somebody in the Soviet Union ran a one-pedal heist, and the only guitar-gear thief with taste in recorded history was working under communism. I respect it as a vulnerability assessment: the attacker ignored everything else on the rig.

After that, he says, DOD came to him and made his model, in black. Then that stopped, and now, he says, there's a Fender version based on the same thing, "so it really is no difference." I'm not going to guess at which pedal it is, because he never says the name in the transcript and I refuse to fill in blanks the way the Hooker captions did.

The second gear story is better. Malmsteen grew up in Sweden, where the power is 50-cycle, 220-volt, and his single-coil pickups "didn't hum that much." Then he came to America and "it started humming like crazy." Someone put him in touch with a contact at DiMarzio (the transcript spells the name "Steve Blocher," and I'll leave that spelling to the experts), who offered to send him a PAF humbucker. Malmsteen's answer was that he didn't want that. He asked why they couldn't "take two coils and put them on top of each other," a stacked design he says didn't exist at the time. They sent one called the AGS1. I can't verify the model beyond his saying so, but the point is clear. American mains hum wrecked his sound, and his fix was to ask a pickup company to build a thing that didn't exist yet. When your environment is bad, don't buy the standard fix. Describe the one you want and make someone else's engineers cry.

There's one more line worth keeping from the same video, a confession about recording. He says he once captured a spontaneous moment, a "spoiler moment thing," that was really good, and he couldn't do it again. His reaction was, "I could never accept that. I would always have to be better." This is the sound of a man who has never once been satisfied by a take, and also of a man whose guitar tone you can recognize from across a parking lot.

## The Craft: Layering, Patience, and Hearing It Anyway

### Seven Layers of Cake

Key of Geebz did a reaction video, listed in the title as "Checking Out The Wreckage of Stars Black Crow Initiative Old Composer Reactions" (the band name is spelled differently in the transcript, where he says a viewer, J-Mac, asked him to listen to Black Crown Initiate).

First, the rhythm. He says he "couldn't get my head locked into the percussive rhythm that was set up by the drummer" until the drum pattern cleaned up about three quarters of the way through. That's a fair critique of any rhythm-heavy extreme metal: you either surrender to the pulse or you spend three minutes holding the map upside down.

Then the vocals, which is where his ears went. Instead of the main vocal, he heard "how well and how tricked out they layered those vocals," and he describes "a complete seven layer cake" of backgrounds, in different tones, EQ'd differently, performed differently, with a clean "Miles Kennedy sounding" tone on top and a "guttural bass pig squeal" at the bottom. (The transcript says Miles; the singer people usually mean is Myles Kennedy, but I'm quoting the source and sparing you my guess.) He adds that it "takes a lot of patience to do that stuff."

The best part is the personal turn. He says he's "out of the business," that he doesn't know if he could "sculpt anything like that anymore," and that he can still keep up with it, and can "still hear it and kind of gel with it." It's a quietly sad bit about retiring from a craft and keeping the ears. I will note that he and Malmsteen are two halves of the same coin: one man can't stop being dissatisfied with his own takes, and the other has stopped making takes and just listens with envy and expertise. Between them they describe the entire lifecycle of a studio perfectionist, from "I'd have to be better" to "I can hear exactly how they did it and I'm not going to."

## The Industry

### Shinedown, Rick Beato, and the Art of the Slow Burn

Rick Beato's main channel posted "Shinedown (The Band I Went Platinum With) Finally Sets The Record Straight," and the transcript catches two things: a reunion and a how-we-got-signed story.

The band is in his studio, and they lean over and tell him they're here to "bring you out of your songwriting retirement." They say plainly that "Shinedown as a collective would absolutely love to write some songs with Rick Beato in the future," and the person speaking jokes that he "better not edit this out." Two members are named as absent (the transcript says they're "probably fishing or doing something"), which is the most relatable detail I've read all week. Kandosii, which is Mando'a for "nice one, well done," to whoever got a band to publicly pressure their old collaborator on camera. There's no deal, just a shake on a promise, and Beato's reaction in the clip is the sound of a man who just got asked to prom.

The second story is about how the industry noticed them. Someone describes a call with a woman at a label or similar outfit; they told her what they were trying to do with the song, and she said she'd do "a deep dive on the band" and asked for two weeks. Two weeks later she called back saying she "had no idea that you were the band that had all of these songs." Then she flew out to watch them play live. The transcript cuts off with "I think the industry may," so I can't tell you what the industry may have done, and I'm not going to finish the sentence for them.

That's due diligence, and it's the opposite of how most of this business runs, which is mostly vibes and a guy named Dave. It also fits the Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #203: a Ferengi in need will never do anything for free. She asked for two weeks and a plane ticket first. The band, to their credit, paid in songs.
