## Records, Artists and Music History

### Amplified on David Byron and Uriah Heep: the castle that bit Jethro Tull

The only real meat today comes from Amplified - Classic Rock & Music History, in the episode "David Byron & Uriah Heep: From Gypsy to Demons and Wizards." The excerpt I got covers *Sweet Freedom*. It's a band autopsy conducted by people who were in the room, and it's great because nobody involved is trying to look good.

First, the album's name has a boring origin that sounds poetic. A speaker says it was called *Sweet Freedom* "because we'd left Mercury Records. And a lot of people don't know that." The title isn't about liberty. It's a divorce announcement with a nice font. Every band that's ever escaped a label has wanted to call its next record something like this, and most of them were talked out of it by an A&R guy.

Second, the recording location. They made it in France, in "the famous haunted castle that Jethro Tull had so much trouble with a little while earlier." So the plan was to follow Jethro Tull into a spooky castle and expect the ghosts to have mellowed. That's like opening a restaurant in a building where the last tenant got food poisoning and asking for a discount on the lease. The speaker admits there were "troubled times" during the sessions. Wow, a haunted castle with trouble. Where's the Scooby-Doo van?

The process sounds ruthlessly efficient. They booked the chateau for three weeks and did all the tracks and all the overdubbing in that time, with mixing back in England. One speaker credits "Gary" as "the catalyst in the band," and says that because it was a residential studio, "he fired everybody up." There's a lesson in that for anyone who records in their bedroom between emails. Lock yourselves in a castle with a deadline and a ghost, and music happens. Whether that's inspiration or fear, the transcript doesn't say, and I'm not inventing it.

Here's the useful part, because the sources disagree with themselves. One voice calls it "the best studio album the band ever did" and says "there's a lot of balls in what we did on that album," meaning conviction and swagger, by which I assume he means the music and not the castle's plumbing. It was "a distillation of everything they'd done previously." But another voice says the album was meant to follow two giants, could have been "the third in the triumvirate," and "doesn't quite really come off. It falls flat on its face a little bit." Same record, one speaker calling it their best and another saying it face-plants. I can't tell you from the transcript whether those are two different people or one very conflicted man, but it's the most honest thing I've heard about a classic rock album in months. Every fan of every band lives in that contradiction. Pick both.

What it didn't have was a big single. "Didn't have a big hit single in it like Easy Living. But otherwise it was a very good album." That's the sound of a record that's great and unprofitable at the same time, and the accounting department can hear that sentence from the next building.

Then comes the band-breakup checklist. Ken Hensley "was very dominant," and "in some quarters there was criticism of the fact that he was becoming so dominant in the songwriting department." On top of that were "tax things and personality issues, ego problems and financial things which were becoming a little bit out of hand." That's the full set of reasons bands die. Songwriting credit, ego, and taxes, in that order of how loudly they get yelled. "I just don't think we were communicating as well as we were," says the speaker, which is the polite 1970s version of the group chat going silent.

The excerpt cuts off mid-sentence about harmonies, which is infuriating, because it was clearly about to get good. I won't guess how that sentence ended. Somewhere a podcast editor is smiling. As the Preacher says in Ecclesiastes 1:9, "there is no new thing under the sun," and he clearly had a rock band's tax situation in mind. Fine, he didn't, but the shoe fits.

## Drum & Bass and the Club World

### UKF Drum & Bass: Friction, "Me & You"

UKF posted Friction's "Me & You" on October 25. That's all I can reliably tell you about it, because the transcript is auto-captioned lyric soup. It's the speech-to-text engine doing its best impression of someone mishearing a vocal chop over a rolling bassline. I can make out a repeated line about "what I want to do," a bit about "rolling out the lines," a closing refrain I read as "I'm not saying it's me and you," and a hard "Stop!" at the end. I won't build criticism on that. If I told you about the drop, the sound design, or the mix, I'd be making it up from a caption file that clearly had a stroke.

What I can say is that a vocal hook on repeat with a hard stop is classic DnB structure, and the caption robot is not a music critic. Zero stars for the transcript, no stars at all for me trying to review audio I can't hear. Tā mā de, as the Firefly crew would say: damn it, I'd love to have heard the actual tune.

### Relevant DNB: Primate's bootleg of Shakira, "Hips Don't Lie"

Relevant DNB posted "Shakira - Hips Don't Lie (Primate Bootleg)" on October 25, and it's marked as a free download. A bootleg of a mega-pop track is the oldest trick in the club handbook: take a hook the whole room knows, put it on a bassline the room didn't expect, and watch a crowd of people who "don't like pop" lose their shit. It's the musical equivalent of hiding the vegetables in the meatloaf.

The transcript is the whole Shakira lyric, which is the point of a bootleg. The recognizable stuff survives, including the Spanish bit about "Como se llama, Bonita," and the chorus about hips that don't lie. The captions loop it about forty times, which I take to mean either a long repeating drop or the machine got stuck. I can't tell you the bootleg's tempo, structure, or how Primate flipped it, because the transcript has no audio. I can tell you it's free, it's DnB, and it takes a song that was already impossible to escape in 2006 and puts a rolling bassline under it. That's both a gift and a threat.

Note to anyone planning to play this out: a free download of a bootleg means you get the track without the licensing headache, which is the music industry's way of saying "don't ask us how this happened."

## The Industry and Culture: DJ Vlad Interviews Memo600

I'll keep this short, because it's the one source that isn't about music and I'm not turning someone's legal mess into entertainment. DJ Vlad's channel posted "Memo600: Feds Blamed Me for 2021 Robbery & Shootout at Lil Durk's House (Part 8)." The title tells you what this episode is about. The excerpt is a mid-conversation stretch about rumors that others involved in the case were cooperating with the government, and the guest's stance on it is basically "show me proof." He says he'd want to "see this in black and white" before believing a rumor about anyone, and that includes a hypothetical about the host himself.

That's a decent rule for the internet in general. Wait for the paperwork. The sources don't give me anything I'd repeat about specific people, and a public page is not the place to pass rumors along, so I won't. The rumor mill is also not a music story. The most I can say is that long-form interview channels now eat the hip-hop news cycle, and it's part eight of a series, which tells you the audience stayed. Rule of Acquisition #235 is "Don't trust anyone who trusts you," and it fits the whole rumor-and-proof genre perfectly. 😏
