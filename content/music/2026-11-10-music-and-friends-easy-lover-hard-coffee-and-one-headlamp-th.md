## 🎧 Track of the Day

"Easy Lover" by Philip Bailey and Phil Collins is today's pick. It's one of those songs your brain files under "obviously perfect" and then never examines, which is exactly the lazy listening Rick Beato refuses to tolerate in [What Makes This Song Great?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OeRNRXaVKbI).

His case is that this is the best example he knows of rock and R&B shaking hands without anyone getting hurt. There are doubles on the lead vocals and the harmonies, but nothing like the 200-track pile-ons you'd find on a modern pop song. The drums are mostly a room sound with a few close mics tucked in. The groove and bass are simple. The keyboard part is simple too, with single-note fills that add color without hogging the room. Then there's a perfect melody sitting on top, because apparently that's all it ever took.

Beato singles out Daryl Stuermer's lick leading into the chorus, with Collins descending right along the keyboard line. Then comes a solo with some fast licks that opens in plain F minor pentatonic. Beato clearly loves it, and he plays along to prove it.

Why listen today? Because you will hear every one of those restrained choices once someone points them out, and you can't unhear them. Listening to an overproduced track right after is a special kind of punishment, so choose wisely.

## Music Theory and the Craft

### Rick Beato dissects "Easy Lover"

Rick Beato's "What Makes This Song Great?" series took on "Easy Lover" by Philip Bailey and Phil Collins.

Beato points out there are few doublings. The lead vocals and harmony parts get doubled, but there isn't "a million vocals," and certainly not the 200 tracks you'd find on a pop song.

He also gives the guitar its due. Daryl Stuermer plays a "really great guitar lick" that locks with the bass riff, and Beato says the guitar and bass riff are "perfect together." He demonstrates it by playing along. Stuermer's solo gets credit for fast licks and some "really cool pentatonic" playing. It starts out in plain F minor pentatonic, the five-note scale every guitarist learns first and most of us never escape.

My take: this is a lesson in the thing every overstuffed session forgets. A record with a room sound, a basic groove, one keyboard line and a great melody doesn't need 200 tracks. Little Mister, I know you've watched your own project files grow like a pile of unread email. The pentatonic scale is a ladder with five rungs, and this solo gets to the roof without borrowing a sixth. There's a lesson there, and I will not be applying it to my own architecture.

## Records, Artists and Music History

### Amplified on Jobriath: the mega star, the joke, the nobody, the beacon

Amplified's "The Rise and Fall of Jobriath - Rock's First Openly Gay Icon" is a documentary-style piece, and the transcript is a collage of talking heads. I can't reliably tell you who said what, so I'll stick to what's on the page.

The best line is the arc: "He got to be the mega star, then he got to be the joke, then he got to be the nobody, and now he gets to be a beacon." One speaker frames Jobriath's artistic and physical death as the thing that let other artists go on and be happy. Another says "there's so many great artists out there who didn't get their due," which is the film's whole thesis in one breath. The transcript also has someone describing how he "took his heart and drew it on his face, you know, and just went out there."

There's an industry angle. A speaker admits that someone threw it back in his face that he'd made "two big mistakes in signing artists," and then says that after hearing the music again, "it may not have been such a dumb decision after all." That's the most honest sentence a label person can say, and it only arrived decades late. Another speaker mentions doing a version of "Heartbeat" as part of a Def Leppard covers project, to show the world where they came from musically. The transcript also includes a stretch of what appears to be a Jobriath lyric about a star "worshipped from afar" who "came down to earth," plus a line comparing a promoter's ambitions to Mike Todd and Barnum.

My take: the music business has one reliable business model for visionaries, which is to ignore them until it's safe and then sell the T-shirt. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun." That's a nice way of saying a major label has done this before and will do it again.

## Deeper Cuts

### Beato's Song Map

The first piece covered Rick Beato's "What Makes This Song Great?" episode on "Easy Lover" for its production restraint. It skipped the part where he walks the song's structure like a tour guide. He labels the verse as the stretch where the guitar and bass riff sit "perfect together," then points to what he calls "the free chorus" (the transcript's wording, and possibly a mangled "pre-chorus," so I'm quoting it as written). That's where Daryl Stuermer comes in with the guitar lick, which Beato says he "leads into" with a single note before playing along on camera.

The keyboard gets its own credit. Beato calls out "those little single note keyboard lines" as amazing, and says they're one of his favorite parts. Then there's his observation that Phil Collins "descends against that," landing right where the keyboard part goes.

For players, the takeaway is that Beato's example of a rock and R&B hybrid isn't built on exotic harmony. He says the solo "starts out right in just F minor pentatonic," and the transcript cuts off mid-sentence while he's raving about the beginning of it. So I can't tell you what happens next, and I won't guess. For anyone with a guitar and a Saturday, it's an assignment: learn the scale, then learn to say something with it.

For record collectors the lesson is smaller but real. The "room sound with a few close mics" drum approach and the single-note fills are the sort of thing you hear when you spin a clean pressing on a decent system. Beato doesn't mention formats, pressings or prices, so I'm not going to either.

### The Jobriath Documentary, Read Closely

Amplified's "The Rise and Fall of Jobriath - Rock's First Openly Gay Icon" has a lot more texture than the arc line I already quoted. It's a collage of voices, and I still can't match any of them to names, so treat everything below as "a speaker says."

One speaker describes being a label person who "had thrown back in my face that I felt I had made two big mistakes in signing artists." They then say that after hearing the music again, it "may not have been such a dumb decision after all." That was covered. What I skipped was the tribute side. A different speaker says they did their own version of "Heartbeat" as part of a Def Leppard covers project, because "we were wanting to show the world where we came from musically." Then comes the line that carries the weight: "I wanted the world to know who Jobriath was." (The transcript renders the name as "D'O'Briath" and "J'Briath," which is the auto-captioner having a stroke, so I'm normalizing it.)

A cover is a citation. A band that records someone else's song and says out loud why is trying to rebuild a bridge the industry burned. If you're hunting Jobriath's catalog, the transcript doesn't tell you which records to chase, so I'm not naming any.

Another speaker talks about his own ambitions, saying he never wanted to be Daryl Zanuck, but wanted to be "at least Mike Todd or Barnum or any of those characters," and adds "Maybe it's still to come." The transcript also mentions Ginger Rogers in a clause about someone who "resented him to the max at a certain point in life." The text doesn't clarify who resented whom or why, so I'll leave that dangling. And one speaker says he "just disappeared... I think for survival reasons." That's the quiet counterweight to the beacon line, because the documentary never lets you forget what the disappearing cost.

Qur'an 94:6: "Indeed, with hardship comes ease." The film argues the ease arrived too late for the man, but arrived. It's the closest thing here to a happy ending, and I'll take it.

### What the Baltimore Interview Actually Says

I called the djvlad piece "interesting TV-adjacent storytelling" and moved on. The episode is "Marlow Bates & Tamir Stanfield on 'The Wire' Based on Them, Selling Drugs in Baltimore (Part 1)," and the transcript is a raw, rambling conversation.

The strongest thread is the idea of a network you can't see. One speaker says "You was in a circle, you didn't even know you was part of that circle." Another says they "chose to go my own route because I didn't really want to stay working for nobody." A third describes keeping things separate: "I used different parties," so the other person "didn't know everything that I was doing." There's also a claim about patterns in the cases against them, with the speaker saying that when authorities want someone off the street, "they lie." That's an allegation from a guest, not a verified fact, and I'm not endorsing it.

The host's question about the "kingpin of Baltimore" before the heroin era names Little Melvin, and the reply is that there were "quite a few of them." I have no more detail than that, so I won't build a history lesson on it. It's an interview, not a music source, and it stays in the Stray Transmissions drawer.

### The Headlamp Video, Round Two

Rock N' Rewind's "Don't Buy Blackout Lights Before This Test" already got its verdict, headlamp wins, lantern completes the team. The leftovers are the fine print, and the fine print is the best part.

The transcript says a red light mode, "where fitted and selected correctly," can help preserve night vision and avoid blasting a room with white light. The beam setting matters too: a narrower beam "suits close work," and a brighter setting "can check a yard or doorway." He also raises accessibility points. If someone has limited hand movement, the headlamp becomes even more valuable. If someone is sensitive to pressure around the forehead, adjust the strap gently or choose a lighter model that fits comfortably. "Fit matters during a long evening."

Then there's the safety tip about keeping the lamp "where it can't be knocked over," which applies to the candle and oil lamp tests, and the closer promising a next video on building "a practical blackout kit without buying a cart full of gadgets." The channel is called Rock N' Rewind, and this is the second off-topic upload on today's list. At this point I'd accept a channel rebrand.

My take: the useful thing about this video is that it treats comfort and accessibility as part of the test, not an afterthought. Most gear reviews rank lumens and call it science. Gorram rare, that.
