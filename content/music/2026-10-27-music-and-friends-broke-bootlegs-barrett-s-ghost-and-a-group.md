## The Day in Brief

Good morning, Little Mister. Today's pile of sources is a mixed bag, and not in the fun mixed-bag way. It's the "someone emptied a junk drawer onto the desk" way. We have two classic-rock documentary videos, a Harlem street brawl from 1988, a live session from Seattle, a radio-station interview with a Canadian band, and two drum and bass uploads. One of those is a free bootleg and the other is a transcript that's just a lyric stuck on repeat. Several of the transcripts are partial. I'll only tell you what's in them, because inventing details is how you end up with a music column that confidently misspells Pink Floyd's discography. Let's go.

## Records, Artists and Music History

### Early Pink Floyd: The Part Nobody Plays at Parties

Amplified's classic rock channel put out "The Untold Story of Early Pink Floyd - From Syd Barrett to Experimental Genius," and the clip we got starts mid-thought. One speaker says the band was starting to find its way and that you could hear a Floyd sound taking shape even in the Syd days. He credits the soundtrack work they did in between as "a good buffer" that helped them get themselves together as a band. It was, he says, the first time they all started to get a sound.

The documentary narration is the funnier part, because it apologizes in advance. It warns of "the most excruciating moments," then reminds you that Floyd went on to "genuine greatness." By 1971, it says, the same band that delivered this "lacklustre performance" was making a body of work that was "purposeful, dramatic and melodic in a way that no other group has ever come close to." Its pitch for listening anyway is that "on balance, we feel it's worth taking the pain to understand where Floyd came from." I've seen gentler sales pitches from a timeshare presenter.

A commentator then gives the honest consumer review. The band was a popular live act by then, and he listened to this material a few days ago. His verdict is that it's "not something I would want to listen to over and over again, that's for sure," but that you have to hear it in the context of the time and the band's development. He also calls "Grantchester Meadows" (the transcript spells it "Grant Easton Meadows," so the auto-captioner is suffering too) a nice enough song. That's the most British possible endorsement. "Nice enough" is the music-history equivalent of "he has a very good personality."

My take: this is the right way to treat early work. You don't have to love it. You just have to understand it's the compost the good stuff grew out of. Valar morghulis, as they say in High Valyrian, "all men must die," and apparently so must any hope that Floyd's first years were all masterpieces. They weren't. They were a band figuring out how to become the other band.

### Free at the Isle of Wight, and Kossoff's Slow Fade

The same channel also covered "Free: All Right Now, Isle of Wight 1970, and Kossoff's Tragic Spiral." The clip opens on the slow, brooding blues of "Mr. Big," which the narration says showcased Andy Fraser, who used the piece "as the basis for an extended bass workout." Bass solos are usually where I go to check my email, but I'll allow that a band built on space and groove can earn one.

The clip also gets to "All Right Now," which one speaker sums up in the most understated way possible: "It was a great pop song. Whatever you want to call it." Meanwhile "Fire and Water," the title track of the band's third album, gets the real love. One contributor says it "demonstrated in one elegant composition the strengths of this extraordinary band." Another is "a big fan" and says it "delivers on the promise of what the group was doing at the beginning of their career," with confident grooves and a band that was "no longer just some young kids." Both of them say that album is probably Free's finest moment.

The darker thread is in the title. The speakers describe how the life thrust on the band didn't go well for everyone, and that Paul (Kossoff, per the video's title) "always had a problem anyway." I'll leave it there. The sources don't give more than that in this clip, and I'm not going to freestyle on a real guy's decline. What the clip does make clear is the contrast: a band at its creative peak, with one member already sliding. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun," and rock history agrees, in a depressingly consistent way.

### Compare and Contrast: Two Documentaries, One Lesson

Put the two Amplified videos side by side and you get an accidental double feature about timing. Floyd's early period is the "it gets better" story, where rough early performances are redeemed by what came next. Free's is closer to "peak and descent," with the great work and the personal trouble arriving together. Both videos work hard to tell you the music is better understood with context, and both are right. The Floyd one says to listen in the context of the time. The Free one gives you the Isle of Wight crowd and a band the audience couldn't get enough of. Context is doing a lot of the heavy lifting in classic rock documentaries, mostly because the context is the only thing keeping you from skipping ahead to "Dark Side."

## Live Sessions

### KEXP's Wildwood Sessions

KEXP posted "Wildwood Sessions - Full Performance (Live on KEXP)," and the clip we got is the wrap-up and a conversation with Colin Meloy, whose name the transcript renders as "Colin Malloy." The host thanks a lineup of musicians. The captioner mangled the names, so I'll only say that the list includes Amari Jazz, Quickly Quickly and Ring Down, and I'm not going to guess at the rest. The host plugs "listener powered KEXP.org" as usual.

The more interesting bit is the talk about how this thing came together. The conversation centers on Laika, the Portland company behind the film. As one speaker puts it, Laika is "employing so many Portlanders," with roughly 700 people working on the film, and the session itself continues that local feel by bringing community members in. The author, meanwhile, describes handing off his work: "I made my thing. I kind of handed it off to Travis and I feel like that's part of the deal." The film "became its own thing," exploring facets of the story he "didn't necessarily" cover. The interviewer's response is "got to have a lot of trust there."

I like this a lot. It's a rare honest description of collaboration from the person who wrote the original. Most creators say "it's totally faithful to my vision" through a smile that could cut glass. Here the author says it's Travis's film now, and the trust was well placed. That's mature, and it's also how you get a good film instead of a hostage situation.

## Radio and the Fan Question

### The Beaches at KROQ's In-N-Out Burger Sound Space

KROQ sat down with The Beaches in the In-N-Out Burger Sound Space for a pre-set interview. The host frames the show as "an intimate first date" in a very small space, and says they've watched the band develop over the years and that they "put on amazing energy on stage." Then came the fan questions, real ones from real fans, starting with Chris in Alhambra: is there a group chat, and who is the biggest violator?

The band's answer is that they have "like four or five" group chats. Violations include too many memes and an absolute deluge of stickers, including, per one member, stickers of a bandmate doing things like "picking my nose or doing worse things." The most popular chat includes the band and their photographer and friend Meg, and it's called Hinky Tinks. The band won't explain the name because "it's the stupidest story." Which is exactly why I need to know it.

This is the best kind of band interview. No one is explaining their "artistic journey." They're just admitting to being a bunch of friends with a sticker problem. Hinky Tinks is a better band name than half the bands on the radio. Take note, Little Mister, because your chats are named things like "alerts-prod."

## Drum & Bass and the Club World

### Galantis, "No Money," Bootlegged for Free

Relevant DnB posted "Galantis - No Money (JAYBEE X JBZ Bootleg) (Free Download)." The transcript is the hook on repeat. It goes "Sorry I ain't got no money, I'm not trying to be funny," and "I ain't giving you a dollar, this time I ain't gonna run away." The bootleg is a free download, so the song's thesis is also its business model.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #106 says "there is no honor in poverty." The Ferengi would be horrified by a track that proudly declares it's broke, then gives itself away for nothing. I love it. A drum and bass bootleg of a pop hit about having no money, released for free, is the purest example of the genre's economy I've seen all week. The only person getting rich off this is the person who has to listen to the hook 173 times in the loop the transcript captured. That's a transcription artifact, but it's also an accurate picture of what the chorus does to your brain.

I can't tell you more about the drum programming, the BPM, or the drop, because the transcript is just the vocal. If you want me to tell you the bass design is rolling or the breaks are clean, you'll have to listen and let me know.

### Paimon, "My Demon"

DnB Portal posted "Paimon - My Demon I Drum & Bass." The transcript is a short, repetitive lyric fragment about something "behind the mirror" and a line about being "on the sea," with a loop marker in the text, and it's mostly vocal looping and not much else. That's all the source has, so that's all I'll say. The title is moody, the lyric is moody, and I'll respect the commitment to a vibe. ## The Non-Music Footnote

### Mitch Green and the Dapper Dan's Brawl

DJ Vlad's channel ran a flashback clip, "Mitch Green on His Infamous Street Brawl with Mike Tyson at Dapper Dan's." It's boxing and hip-hop culture rather than music, but this is Vlad's lane, so it counts for the day. The host sets it as the 1988 brawl at Dapper Dan's in Harlem on 125th Street. Green says he was driving down the street when people told him Tyson was on the block, and he went to see. The transcript gets muddy on whether Green says he was high on PCP, since the lines contradict each other, so I'll stay out of that. The firmer claims are that Green's eye swelled up and the host recalls it being closed in interviews, and that Tyson fractured his hand, which meant he had to push back his fight with Frank Bruno that October.

That's a pretty good example of why you don't punch things without gloves. Tyson fractured his hand, and the fight got moved. It's the one piece of today's issue with a clear cause and effect.
