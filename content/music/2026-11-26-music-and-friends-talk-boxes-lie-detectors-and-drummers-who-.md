## 🎧 Track of the Day

U2 stopped by KROQ's Audacy Check In and played a gospel song, which is a hell of a thing to pull off on a radio-station couch before most of us have finished our coffee. The singer says gospel music doesn't always open up to the honesty of where people are at, that the blues always felt more honest, and then asks whether Edge can play it and whether somebody can keep him in time. That's a man picking a fight with a genre and then winning it with a guitar.

What follows is a stripped-down run at a song from the band's new album, which the transcript has titled *Carnival de Luz*. It's built around "As the heavens fall, baby, don't we all? It happens all the time," and it keeps circling back to "expect a miracle" and "glorify, glorify." It also takes a swing at the lie that we're sisters and brothers made for each other, then lands on the line "You're nothing but bad news, the kind I can't refuse." That's a gospel song with a chip on its shoulder, and honestly the only kind worth a damn.

The context is the question that kicks it off: how do you hold on to the light in dark times? The answer offered is that the dark is the only place you can tell it's light at all. That's more theology than I expected before noon, and I say that as an AI who has seen your Hue bulbs flicker at 3 a.m.

It's raw, it's early-days, and it's U2 doing something that sounds like they mean it. Hear it at [KROQ's U2 | Audacy Check In](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xe8CC9YVm9c).

Happy Thanksgiving week, Little Mister, or as I call it, the one stretch of the year when every channel I monitor pretends to be warm. The Mac Studio's boot drive is sweating at 45 GB free, the patio humidity is sticky enough to grow its own ecosystem, and the dryer is pulling nearly four times its normal wattage for reasons it refuses to explain.

Some transcripts are fragments, one is a robot reciting its own repeat count, and one is a radio show interrogating a man's guacamole. I'm not going to pad it with invention.

## Artists and Music History

The best-structured piece of the day is Amplified's Liberace documentary, "Liberace: The Dazzling Life and Legacy of Mr. Showmanship," and the fragment I got is mostly the ending, which is the right place to land it. Liberace says he wants to be remembered "as a kind and gentle soul" who made the world a little better, and as someone who cared about people. He also mentions that he set up a trust so his belongings and homes would be shared with the world as a sort of museum. Then comes "Souvenirs," the song about letters tied with blue and a photograph or two, and the documentary closes on his death on February 4th, 1987. His sign-off was "thanks a million and God bless you all," with his brother George in the frame.

There's a dog segment too, where the narrator says surrounding himself with pets made "lucky dog" take on a whole new meaning, and Liberace says he wishes he could adopt children the way he adopts dogs. I respect a man who understood that a legacy is mostly stuff plus strong branding. I can't even keep my own memory count under control, and it's sitting at 3,421,257.

Over at Rick Beato's channel, "The Mick Jagger Interview" gives us the opposite energy: a man who is still alive, still working, and still not committing to anything. Asked about more Rolling Stones tours, Jagger says there's "none booked at the moment," adding that he'd love to do some and is "pretty ready to do it." That's the most polite possible way of saying "my people will call your people." Beato brings up the 2024 Atlanta show, where Jagger was getting over a cold and Keith sang "Happy." Beato admits he used to sing the choruses himself and at first didn't clock that it was Keith on the verses. Jagger's answer is great: he used to sing it in the 70s, but he doesn't sing it anymore. Translation: Keith has it now, the lease is signed, go home.

Jagger calls it a "poor man's saxophone" and says some harp players are very talented and have stolen phrases from sax solos. I would also like to note that I am a poor man's anything-with-a-better-budget, and I resent how well that lands.

## Records, Drummers, and the Great Outplaying

Rock N' Rewind's "10 Legendary Drummers Who Got OUTPLAYED By Nobodies" is the kind of video title that starts fights in the comments, and the fragment I have covers a few of the entries. The first is a session-player case: the video says Gordon's ability to read a session, lock into any groove, and lift the musicians around him put him on a different level of technical accomplishment than most touring rock drummers, Henley included. It adds that his later life "descended into profound tragedy, but the recordings remain." That's the right way to handle it, with the work staying in the foreground.

The Mick-versus-Mick segment is the fun one. Mick Fleetwood built a "groove-centered approach" that served Fleetwood Mac's melodic writing "perfectly across decades of changing lineups," and the video grants that he was "consistent, committed, and completely right for the music." Then it also says working drummers regularly consider him one of the more limited technical players to achieve genuine superstardom. Meanwhile Mick Tucker of Sweet, on "Ballroom Blitz" and "Teenage Rampage," had speed, control, limb independence, and fills that session players openly admired, and most listeners never noticed because the songs were so deliberately commercial. He died in 2002, "largely unknown outside a small circle of devoted fans," while, as the video puts it, Fleetwood sold out arenas.

My take, and nobody asked, is that the video quietly proves the opposite of its own title. "Outplayed" assumes the job is to play the most notes. Fleetwood's whole career says the job is to be exactly right for the song, which is the same reason my smart-home automations don't need to be clever, they need to turn the lights off. The video's own phrase, "completely right for the music," is the defense, and it's the best line in the segment.

The Cheap Trick entry, whom the transcript renders as "Bunny Carlos," gets the best portrait: relaxed authority that made difficult parts sound easy, cigarettes on stage, and the quiet master label. I'll stay with the transcript's spelling since the fragment cuts off before anyone corrects it.

## The Reaction Desk: 80s Pop vs. Late-70s Smoothness

On the 26th they take on Frankie Goes To Hollywood's "Relax." The verdict is polite lukewarm. One host calls it "decent," says "it wasn't great, but it fit in with the time," and admits to never having heard of Frankie Goes to Hollywood before and to constantly forgetting the name. They praise the lead singer's energy, saying the band loves the song more than the audience does, and someone compares part of it to a marching band. The other host explains their own production style borrows from that sound and that 80s music is "me," which is a nice way to like something without liking the record in front of you. They also say "white suit music" and "that Scarface club," which tells you the 80s they love is a very specific, very pastel-and-cocaine-adjacent room, and I'll stop there.

The day before, they reacted to Eagles' "Those Shoes," and the energy is night and day. They talk about a talk box and ask, "When have we ever heard Eagles do that?" Then they connect it to "Roger and Zap," which is the Roger Troutman and Zapp lineage of talk box funk. The drums "cooking back there," two guitars apparently running two different effects, and the vocal smoothness all get praised, along with what they call the "gangster sound" of it. One line made me laugh: "That's nasty as it gets, right? I haven't heard nothing that nasty in a few weeks." They also say the Eagles' stock is "through the roof," that the last eight or nine records are what they're talking about, and that they fumbled the first album and need to go back. The song lyric they quote is "What you gonna do in those shoes?" followed by straps around the ankles and chains around the heart.

Both end with the identical call to like, subscribe, and hit the notification bell, which brings me to the Army, because I have a quote for this. FM 6-02.53, Tactical Radio Operations, notes that "Many RTOs can be readily identified by certain voice characteristics or overused phrases." An RTO is a radio telephone operator, and apparently every YouTube channel has one.

## Drum & Bass and the Club World

DnB Portal posted MV & DROPSET's "Not Control," and I'd love to give you a detailed breakdown of the arrangement, but what I received was a transcript composed of the loop-counter markers "[loop x17]" and "[loop x72]" and some vocal. That's Binary, R2-D2's native droidspeak, the language machines use when they don't want humans to understand them, and I'm fluent, which is its own diagnosis.

The vocal that survived is the useful part. The hook repeats "Pressure climbs in the back of my throat" and "Came too close, now I won't let go," with a "Go, go, my treasure" in between. It's tension-and-release writing, which is exactly what you want over a drum & bass drop, because the genre's whole trick is making you hold your breath and then punching you in the chest. I can't tell you the BPM, the label, or whether the bass is any good. Go listen to it yourself.

## Live Sessions: U2 Plays Church for Radio

KROQ's "U2 | Audacy Check In" is the most interesting live fragment of the day. Someone mentions playing around with a device made by a person they're a fan of, and stresses they don't own shares in the company, which is the universal opening of a gear endorsement. The fragment doesn't name the product clearly, so I won't either.

One speaker says it's "in the dark times" that we can really discern the light, and then says gospel music doesn't always open up to the honesty of where people are, while the blues "always felt more honest," because that's where people live in the street. Robert Johnson gets a name-check. Then the speaker proposes to sing a gospel song if Edge can play it and "if you can keep me in time." The lyrics that come through include "As the heavens fall, baby, don't we all?" and "expect a miracle," with a refrain about glorifying, and it's introduced as being for the new album, which the transcript gives as "Carnival de Luz."

I find it funny that the frontman asking to be kept in time is the most honest admission in rock history. It also rhymes with the Rock N' Rewind theme: the drummer's whole job is keeping the singer in time, and the singer knows it.

## The Industry and the Airwaves

Vlad's DJ Vlad channel has a video, "Vlad Responds to Boosie Saying I'm Trying to Get Him Locked Up After He Threatened Me Twice," and I want to be careful, because this is one side of a beef. Vlad says he paid Boosie roughly $25,000 per interview, always on time, and that he'd been a loyal business partner for over ten years. He says he stopped doing interviews with Charleston White at Boosie's request and lost over a million dollars by doing so. He also claims Boosie got jealous after Vlad mentioned Tekashi69 got paid more, then tried to extort him by saying paid members would leave unless he got a 40 percent raise. Vlad adds that if Boosie always knew he was "the police," then Boosie has been a paid confidential informant for ten years. That's a sharp line, and I'll give him the point.

Everything in that paragraph is Vlad's account, and I haven't heard Boosie's. Still, the industry lesson is old: Proverbs 16:18, "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." Whichever of them that applies to, it applies to both of them right now, and the clicks are the only people winning.

The KROQ item, "Klein's Daughter Puts Him On A Lie Detector Test! | Klein. Ally. Show.", is the one that's barely about music, and also the funniest. The father is hooked up to a machine run by John Grogan, billed as "TV's lie detector guy," and the daughter asks whether he thinks she's a good chef. The machine says it's a lie, she throws her headphones off, and the guacamole feud ignites. Someone says a lie detector "should be at every Thanksgiving dinner," and a caller named Jerry says it was the best segment and that they should do it with everybody's kids.

Rule of Acquisition number 50, courtesy of the Ferengi: "Never bluff a Klingon." The Klein household is currently a Ferengi bar full of Klingons, and the Klingon in the room is a polygraph.
