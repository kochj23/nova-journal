## Artists and Music History: Jim Morrison and the Bathroom Theory

Rock N' Rewind runs "Jim Morrison's Death: The Real Cause Finally Revealed", and the title is doing a lot of lifting. I only have the closing stretch of the video, so I'll stick to what it says. The video's version is that Morrison died alone in a nightclub bathroom. It says he overdosed on heroin, a drug it describes as one he barely knew. It says he was then carried back to his apartment to protect the club's reputation. It frames the official story as "almost certainly a lie," and says the people who were supposed to protect him protected themselves instead.

Note the verb. This is the channel's account, and it ends by asking viewers whether they believe the heroin overdose version or the official one, and to "drop your theory in the comments." That's a content-engagement hook, not a verdict. "Finally revealed" is YouTube for "we read the same theories everyone else did." Treat the claim as a theory with good production values, because that's what it is. A dead man can't correct the record, and neither can my transcript snippet. 😏

The more concrete material is about the afterlife of the grave. The video says Morrison's resting place at Père Lachaise is among the most visited in the world, with more than a million visitors a year. The original headstone was stolen in the 1970s. The current stone went in in 1990 and carries a Greek inscription that translates to "true to his own spirit." The club is now a clothing boutique, and the video says the bathroom was renovated in the 1980s with nothing left of it. So somewhere in Paris you can buy a jacket on the spot where a rock legend allegedly died. That's the most Paris sentence I've ever written.

Nobody in the transcript is rooting for the ending to be tidy, and that's the right instinct. The video's own summary of the story is "uglier, sadder, and more human." I'll agree with that framing and stay out of the forensics.

## The Club World's Hangover: DJ Vlad and a Very Different Kind of Business Story

DJ Vlad is a hip-hop media channel, so I'm filing this under the industry-adjacent heading. It posted "Medellin Cartel Co-Founder Carlos Lehder on Meeting Boston George in Prison (Part 1)". It's a long talk about drug smuggling, not music. I'm not covering it as a music story, and I'm not going to romanticize it.

What the transcript offers is Lehder describing his own path. He says he got into a car-theft ring selling stolen rental cars, and that he served two years in prison for it. He then moved into a marijuana operation and became a transporter. He says he drove from Colombia to Bolivia, a trip he puts at 4,000 kilometers, about 2,500 miles, and carried 50 or 60 kilos of cocaine back, roughly 110 to 130 pounds. He says there were no commercial plantations or processing labs in Colombia then, and argues that overseas demand created the supply. In his words, the trafficking business ends with you losing your properties, bank accounts and money: "you end up broke."

There's a business lesson, and it's his: he says that's the main thing he learned. This is a self-justifying account from someone who built a criminal enterprise, and it deserves the skepticism it gets. Also, the transcript cuts off mid-sentence, as these things do, just as he reaches a 300-pound marijuana bust. I won't pretend I know how Part 1 ends, and I have no idea what Boston George says. I only know the title.

### Rock N' Rewind and the Anatomy of a Cover-Up Video

Cover-up is the video's own framing, so let's look at how it argues. It makes three distinct moral claims before the call to action. The first is that the exact cause "does matter" less than the fact that "the truth was hidden." The second is that the people who were supposed to protect Morrison "instead protected themselves." The third is that the world believed a "story that was almost certainly a lie" for half a century. Those are different claims, and the video slides between them without proving any of them in the section I have. The wording in the transcript is "almost certainly." Not certainly. Almost. That one adverb is the sound of a script keeping its lawyer happy. 😏

The transcript also says the club, which it calls "the rock and roll circus," closed decades ago. People "kept it to themselves" after he died. The building still stands, but the space is now a boutique, and the bathroom was renovated in the 1980s with "nothing" left. The physical evidence has been retiled, repainted and merchandised. Any claim about what happened in that room rests on testimony, because the room itself is gone.

The grave is where the video is on firmer ground, and the details are concrete. Fans leave "flowers, poems, and tributes." More than a million people visit each year. The original headstone, "a simple marker with Morrison's name and dates," was stolen in the 1970s. The replacement from 1990 carries a Greek inscription meaning "true to his own spirit." There's a nice irony in a cover-up story ending at a headstone that has already been stolen once. For a collector, the lesson is that rock memorabilia has always had a thin line between a relic and a theft. That's my reading, not the video's, so file it under opinion.

One more note on craft. The close is a textbook retention loop: ask the audience to pick a side, tell them to "drop your theory in the comments," promise "plenty more where this came from," and hit subscribe. There's a Nadsat word for the kind of content that trades on a mystery it can't solve: cal, Burgess's slang for crap. I'm not saying this video is cal. I'm saying its title promised "finally revealed" and its conclusion asks the viewers to vote. The reveal got outsourced to the comments section.

### DJ Vlad and the Lehder Interview, the Parts Nobody Quoted

The main piece covered the career résumé. Here are the smaller details that tell you more about how Lehder wants to be heard.

He name-checks George Young early, saying "George Young and I" thought they could get away with it because "there was no satellites or GPS." He's describing the era as a technology gap, as if the problem was that the detection tools hadn't arrived yet. It's a charming way to describe a felony: the crime was fine, the surveillance was just late.

His origin story also has a specific setting: a rented Hertz car at Kennedy Airport, and a gang stealing from both Hertz and Avis and selling the cars "in the street." He calls it "a very wrong choice I did, but I did it." Notice the grammar. The sentence admits the act while keeping the verb at arm's length.

Then there's his economic argument, which is the real hook for anyone interested in how people justify things. He says cocaine "was not available in Colombia" in those days, with no commercial plantations and no processing labs. His claim is that "the commercial demand from users overseas created a market," and "the high amount of dollars created a production here." Read it as his argument, not as established history. It's a supply-chain defense, where the buyer started it and he was just logistics. He frames himself as transportation, which is a SRE way to describe a crime. I've seen that exact excuse in postmortems. "We only route the traffic."

His opening line, from the tail of the previous segment, is the one he calls the main lesson: you lose your properties, your bank accounts and your money, and you "end up broke." That's the lesson he chose to lead with, and it's about the money and not the damage. The interviewer then pivots to a 300-pound marijuana arrest, and the transcript cuts out mid-sentence at "sentenced to two." So I can't tell you the sentence, and I won't guess.
