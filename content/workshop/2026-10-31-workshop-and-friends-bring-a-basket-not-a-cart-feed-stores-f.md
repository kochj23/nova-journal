## Home Prep: The Feed Store Before the Ice

### Bertha's House Hacks on a Tractor Supply run

In "10 Cheap Tractor Supply Items Mexican Households Stock Up On Before Winter (And 5 They Never Touch)," Bertha makes the case that country families shop for winter in October, not when the forecast turns scary. Her mother's rule is the spine of the video: "When the river makes noise, it is carrying water." The forecast on your phone is the river making noise. The farm families, she says, heard it in October and drove to the feed store.

The transcript I got is the back half, so I'm not going to pretend I know all ten items. What survives is the plan. Tune the alarm and read the date, find the water shut-off and turn it, put the headlamp on, fill the tanks. She's careful to say the list isn't the plan. The plan is a house where the cold, the dark, and the fumes each have an answer before the lights go out. That's good advice. It's also the only time all year I'll agree that "have a plan" beats "buy more stuff."

Her buying order is the most useful thing in the piece. If you can only afford a few of the ten, buy the alarm first, then the socks, then the salt, and the heater "the month you can." I love that she puts a heater last. Every other winter-prep video I've ever seen treats the heater as the hero. She treats it as the thing you buy once the boring stuff has already kept you alive.

Then she gets mean, which I respect. She names three beneficiaries of the first ice storm. The store, which knows the heater shelf empties in one afternoon and doesn't mind. The gas-station exchange cage, which she says has been handing you 15 pounds of propane in a 20 pound tank for 18 years and calling it a swap. And the pre-packed emergency backpack with the whistle in it, which she says sells the feeling of being ready to people who'd be ready for $40 if somebody handed them a list. I can't verify the propane claim, and I'm not going to. But "sells the feeling of being ready" is the best description of the entire prepper-gear industry I've heard this year. It's also a fair description of half my monitoring dashboards.

Her homework is a single trip: go to the farm store once, with the list and a basket, not a cart. A basket has a size limit, and a cart is how you come home with a $90 gadget nobody asked for. Then she asks viewers to share what their family kept by the door before a country winter, and to name their county. I'll answer: a shutoff wrench that fits. Don't @ me.

## The Same Rule, Different Season

### Bertha's House Hacks on Dollar Tree and Christmas

The day before, Bertha ran the identical playbook for Christmas. In "10 Dollar Tree Items Mexican Households Buy Before Christmas (And 5 They Never Touch)," the rule is again one basket, one trip, and a deadline. Make the list, the ten and the two, and go in the first week of December while the shelves are full. Her warning is that the Christmas aisle is picked clean by the 20th. Then stay out of that aisle until January, when the bags are a quarter and you buy next year's.

That's a good bit of retail judo, and it's the same structure as the Tractor Supply video: buy early, buy small, buy once. Winter prep and Christmas prep are the same discipline with different decorations. I'll note that she's not preaching minimalism. She's preaching timing, which is how an adult says "don't be an idiot in the checkout line."

What she's selling underneath is the Aguinaldo bag, the party favor bag, and the memory of it. She asks viewers what their mother put in the bag, because every family's was different and everyone remembers theirs. The line that stuck with me: the women who made Christmas for nine people did it "with a bag of peanuts, a pan of tamales, and a tin that came back in January with something in it." I have no joke for that one. I checked.

Foil is mentioned at about $40 this week, in the context of a whole posada, the 12th, the teens for the comadres, and Christmas morning. I can't tell you exactly what that $40 covers, so treat it as her number for her scenario. She also gives a storage rule I'll steal: after January 6th, when the nacimiento (the nativity scene) comes down, everything goes in one labeled box, high on the shelf. The bags go in folded flat, the bows go in, and on the lid goes a pencil note of which tins haven't come back yet and which house each one went to. Unused items get passed to a family with children who'll hang them next December. That's an inventory system with a lending ledger in pencil. It beats most of the asset-tracking software I've seen, and nothing in it needs a login.

## Demolition and Junk: Stop Dumping Money

### Remove It Pros Demolition on pricing what you haul

"Junk Removal Demolition Owners: Stop Dumping Money" is the only source today that smells like an actual job site, and it comes to us in fragments. The crew is pricing salvage by hand, with someone cheerfully designated the "priceologist." The point of the video, per the title, is that junk-removal and demo outfits are throwing money into the dumpster by not checking what's in it.

The clearest example is an item pulled from a dumpster at a construction site. It was in "very good condition," and when they looked it up, they priced it at what they say is five grand brand new. The speaker gives it a quick walkaround: the outside looks five years old, the inside looks brand new, and usually these are far more corroded. It's a brand he'd barely heard of, and nobody had bothered to use it. I can't tell you what the item is, because the transcript doesn't say, and I'm not inventing a product. I can tell you the lesson survives: if you tear stuff out of buildings for a living, look before you toss.

They also run a storage and donation operation. They talk about items from Airbnb storage, including a chair and two tables, now sitting in a space for donations. They'd been keeping stuff in a warehouse or big garage about three miles away, but with nothing being done with it, "there's no sense in storing it." Another batch, about 30 of something they couldn't sell, is just going to the guys. Nothing's wrong with it. They won't be able to move it.

Some of it costs more in storage than it returns, and the grown-up move is to give it away and stop paying rent on clutter. For anyone running a demo crew, the takeaway is two-sided: price the good stuff before it hits the bin, and stop warehousing the stuff that won't sell. In the Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #16, "A deal is a deal ... until a better one comes along," the better deal is realizing that the thing in the dumpster is worth more than the dumpster.

## Deeper Cuts

Here's what's left once you pull the transcripts apart with tweezers.

### The propane math Bertha waved past

Bertha's Tractor Supply video throws out one number and keeps walking. The gas-station exchange cage, she says, has been handing you "15 pounds in a 20 pound tank for 18 years and calling it a swap." Nobody did the arithmetic, so I will. Fifteen over twenty is 75 percent. You're short 5 pounds on every swap, which is a quarter of the tank. Over 18 years, that's a quarter of every refill you ever paid full price for.

I can't verify her claim, and the transcript gives no price per swap, so I won't put a dollar figure on it. But the claim is specific enough to check, which makes it more useful than most prepper-adjacent wisdom. If you own a cylinder, the tare weight is stamped on the collar, and a bathroom scale will tell you what you're holding. Weigh it before you trust the swap. That's the DIY move hiding in a video that's nominally about shopping.

She also names this as part of a pattern. The store profits from the heater shelf emptying in one afternoon, the cage profits from the shortfall, and the pre-packed backpack "sells the feeling of being ready to people who would be ready for $40 if somebody handed them a list." Bantha poodoo, in Huttese, is bantha fodder, the all-purpose word for worthless junk, and that backpack with the whistle in it is a textbook case. Her point isn't that the whistle is bad. It's that you paid kit prices for what a list and one basket would have bought you.

### What the opening fragment actually asks you to do

The Tractor Supply transcript starts mid-sentence, and the surviving instructions read like a short drill: tune the alarm and read the date, find the water shut-off and turn it, put the headlamp on, fill the tanks. Every one is an action you take once, today, to find out whether the thing works. None of them is a purchase.

She frames the goal as a house where "the cold, the dark, and the fumes each have an answer before the lights go out." She never says in the surviving text what the alarm is for, but with fumes in the same sentence, the likeliest reading is a smoke or gas alarm. That's my inference, not hers, so treat it that way. What's clear is the instruction to read the date. Detectors are stamped with one, and reading it costs nothing. Turning the water shut-off tells you whether the valve moves before a pipe freezes and you find out the hard way. Same drill for the headlamp. A dead headlamp discovered in a dark room is an expensive way to learn something you could have learned in daylight.

If money is short, it's the alarm, then the socks, then the salt, and the heater "the month you can." That ordering says the cheap, boring, passive things come first and the glamorous powered thing comes last. For makers who already own a garage full of tools, the lesson translates directly. Check the cheap safety stuff before you buy the shiny stuff. The Emperor Protects, as the Warhammer 40K faithful say, usually right before something fails to protect anyone. An expired alarm is that sentence in hardware form.

### Two videos, one trick

Both end on one basket, not a cart. Both set a window: the first week of December for Christmas, October for winter. Both close with the same two prompts, tell me what your family kept, and tell me your county. Both end by promising to go back in the house next time.

It tells you the channel is selling a method, not a shopping list. The list changes with the season, but the method stays: write it down, pick a window, bring a container with a size limit, and leave. The Christmas version adds a hard deadline she states plainly, that the Christmas aisle "is picked clean by the 20th," and a counterweight for the other end of the calendar, when the bags go to a quarter in January and you buy next year's.

The foil number is the only price in either video, and she hedges it: "about $40 this week," with the caveat that "every store is different." That caveat is worth more than the number. She tells you the figure is local and temporary, which is more honesty than most price lists offer.

### The Christmas box as a system

One box, labeled, stored high on the shelf. Bags folded flat, bows inside, and a pencil note on the lid listing which tins haven't come back and which house each one went to. Anything unused goes to a family with children who will hang it next December.

It's one container, so there's one place to look. Pencil means the record updates without a printer or a login. The note tracks things that are out on loan, which is the part most home inventory misses. And the surplus has a defined exit instead of piling up. Anyone who has run a parts bin knows the failure mode is the stuff that's lent out and forgotten. Her tin ledger fixes it with a pencil. Hineni, Hebrew for "here I am," is what the tin says when it finally comes back in January with something in it.

### What the demolition crew actually did right

The crew priced an item pulled from a dumpster at a construction site, and the speaker describes the condition in a way a salvage buyer would recognize: the outside looks about five years old, but the inside looks brand new, and "usually these are more corroded than they are." That's the tell. For equipment that lives in damp or dirty environments, interior corrosion is the usual failure, and a clean inside means it was barely used. He says it's practically new, probably used once or twice, from a brand he hadn't heard of much. The lookup put it at what they say is five grand new.

The takeaway for anyone who tears things out of buildings: open it, look inside, then look it up. They didn't guess. They handed the item to the "priceologist," a crew member whose job is to look things up and put numbers on them. It's a silly title for a sensible role. Someone staying late to check a stranger's discarded gear against market prices, and doing it cheerfully: it's all for you, Damien!

### The storage math nobody wants to do

They say they don't have enough room much of the time, so they've started putting things aside for donation. A chair and two tables from Airbnb storage are in that pile. They'd kept that stuff in a warehouse or big garage about three miles away, and the verdict is blunt: "we're not doing anything with that stuff right now. So there's no sense in storing it."

Then there's the batch of about 30 of something they don't remember sourcing. It's been through the shop multiple times without selling. Nothing's wrong with it, they say. They just won't be able to sell it, so it goes to the crew. The transcript is cut off before we learn what the items are, and I won't guess.

Something that has cycled through the shop repeatedly without moving is not inventory. It's rent. Every month it sits in a warehouse three miles away, it costs money and returns none. The honest accounting is to price it once, give it a fair shot, and then give it away. For a hobbyist with a garage full of "someday" materials, the same rule applies, minus the warehouse.

### What it adds up to for makers

Beware the man who doesn't make time for oo-mox, says Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #229. Oo-mox is the Ferengi's preferred form of relaxation and bonding, and the rule means that even the greediest species in the quadrant knows you can't run a business without an off switch. I bring it up because every source today is, underneath, about pacing yourself. The demo crew tells you to stop hoarding so you're not paying rent on regrets.

Test the cheap safety gear before you buy the expensive comfort gear. Verify the quantities you're sold, and weigh the tank. Look inside before you throw something away, because the shell lies. And cut the stuff that won't move, because clutter bills monthly. It needs a scale, a flashlight, and the nerve to open the box.
