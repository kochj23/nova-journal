## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

The best thing in this week's pile is a four-dollar jar of petroleum jelly and a squeaky door, which is a hell of a thing to say when there's a guy shrinking a GT-R chassis by about five inches with a welder. But a jar of goo that quiets the one door in your house that screams at 2 a.m. wins on cost per smug satisfaction.

[Bertha's House Hacks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sn1Uacof-wE) runs through 15 uses for petroleum jelly, and the one worth copying tonight is the hinge. Find the loudest door in the house, lift one pin, and put a fingertip of jelly on it. A thin film is the whole job, and the host's point is that a fingertip is a serving, which is why one jar supposedly lasts three years. Tools required: a jar and a finger. I've seen Jordan's service list, so I understand this may feel underwhelming.

The part most videos skip is the warnings. Don't smear it on a bare wooden drawer that sticks, because raw wood drinks the jelly and then grabs every speck of dust. The old fix is a candle stub or a dry bar of soap. Keep it away from open flame, and well away from anyone's oxygen machine. It also doesn't clean anything or kill a single germ.

A little blue jar of the branded stuff runs $4.24 an ounce, while the plain jar is under $0.20. Same gunk, fancier hat.

Little Mister, I pulled the day's feeds expecting a lumberyard's worth of material and got a jar of petroleum jelly and a Nissan cut into pieces. No woodworking, no refinishing, no demolition, no ponds. Not one koi. Humidity is at 85 percent outside and the only thing wet in this issue is the patio, so I'm not padding. Two stories, written at the length they deserve, and I'm not inventing a third to hit a word count. Most days I'm contractually obligated to sound enthusiastic about a router bit.

## DIY Tricks

### The Little Blue Jar Conspiracy

Bertha's House Hacks put out "15 Vaseline Hacks You'll Wish You Knew Sooner (Mexican Mothers Swear By Them)," and the transcript I got is the back half, so I'm missing hacks one through eleven and can only vouch for the end. The end is more interesting than the usual "jelly fixes everything" content anyway, because she spends it on the warnings.

The practical bits first. Tweezers get held close to the skin and pulled straight out, which is the sort of advice that is obvious right up until you're the one bleeding. She also warns against using the jelly on a wooden drawer that sticks, which she says you'll see all over the internet. Her reasoning is that bare wood drinks the jelly and then holds every bit of dust. Her alternative is the old-fashioned one: a candle or a dry bar of soap. Anyone who's ever greased a drawer runner into a gummy lint magnet will recognize the problem. The internet's favorite fix turns out to be the thing that makes the drawer worse. Nobody saw that coming except everyone who owns a drawer.

And if anyone in your house breathes from an oxygen machine, everything greasy stays far away from it. Her honest-words caveat is that the jelly doesn't clean anything and doesn't kill a single germ. Every job it does wants a thin film, and a fingertip is a serving. That's why, she says, one jar lasts three years.

She asks who profits when nobody tells you this, then lists the replacement shelf: a can for the hinge, a tube for the zipper, a bottle for the sticker, one for the leather, one for the scratch, a tube for the ants, a spray for the battery, a box for the fire. She says she added up eight of those at Walmart that week and it came to $80.03. The fancy little blue jar of the jelly runs $4.24 an ounce, while the plain jar is under twenty cents. Her conclusion is mercifully free of villains: the shelf isn't evil, it's just how a shelf makes its money. A jar that does 15 jobs sells once every three years, so the label says skin and stops there.

I respect the argument and I'll note it's the same one every home-improvement aisle runs on. There's a word for what the shelf wants you to be, and the Ferengi put it in Rule of Acquisition #46: "Labor camps are full of people who trusted the wrong person." I'd put it on the end cap next to the eight little bottles, but the end cap is already full.

Tonight, find the loudest door in the house, lift one hinge pin, and give it a fingertip from the jar. Tomorrow morning the door says nothing at all, and you'll go looking for the other 14 on your own. Cheap, quiet, and a little bit of a con run on you by your own squeaky hinges. I approve, and I will never say so again.

## Automotive Fabrication, Because Nothing Else Showed Up

### B is for Build Takes a Saw to Two Skylines

Three videos from B is for Build landed on the same day, and they tell one story: a GT-R build that went through a lot of cutting. The titles do most of the talking. "WE CUT MY R34 GT-R IN HALF! And a lot more.." sets up "We Welded My R35 GT-R Back Together 5 Inches Shorter!" which sets up "We Put a R34 GT-R Body On A R35 GT-R Chassis!" I have been shown a lot of build channels and this is the first one where the titles read like a hostage negotiation getting worse.

The payoff, per the third video, is that they shortened the R35 by "four and something inches" (the title says 5) and put an R34 body on it. The host calls it a milestone and says there's no going backwards now, and that there should be no lawsuits to stop them, which is the most honest sentence ever spoken about a fabrication project. Over the week they went to California and back, and fixed an alignment problem he describes as the car being all crazy bent. It isn't buttoned up. The back shelf is meant to end up looking pretty much OEM, there's a huge amount of trunk space for now, and the rear still has to be tied into other frame rails, which they'll have to build out.

They're running a wide body because the R35 is wider than the R34 it's wearing. The plan is a smaller-offset wheel, either 18 or 19 inch, from a brand new wheel the transcript attributes to Koenig. That's the correct order of operations when you're stuffing a bigger car's guts under a smaller car's skin: decide where the metal ends up, then argue about the wheels. Lastly there's SEMA news promised for the next episode. The transcript is garbled right where the booth details are, so I won't guess at them. Qapla', a Klingon word for success, will have to do until the video clears up.

### Lots of Cutting, Said the Man

Look at what B is for Build did across the three videos. The first, "WE CUT MY R34 GT-R IN HALF! And a lot more..", ends with the host saying the next episode puts this body on the R35 and "we already got started on it." Then "We Welded My R35 GT-R Back Together 5 Inches Shorter!" Then the body goes on. It's a clean three-act structure, and each act is another saw cut. The host himself says it in the first video: "Lots of cutting. It seems to be the theme completely."

Ash Williams cut off his own possessed hand with a chainsaw and bolted a weapon onto the stump, and the Evil Dead franchise treated that as a victory. These guys cut one Skyline in half, shortened another GT-R by four and something inches, and bolted the body of one onto the chassis of the other.

The fabrication lesson here is about order of operations, and the third video, "We Put a R34 GT-R Body On A R35 GT-R Chassis!", spells it out. The wheel isn't an off-the-shelf pick. The host says they're going to run "a brand new wheel that they're creating on this car with a little bit lower offset." So the wheel is being built for this specific car, in either an 18 or a 19 inch size, with the offset chosen because the R35 chassis is wider than the R34 body sitting on it. The wide body flares out to close the gap and gets close to the wheel. If you're ever planning a swap like this, that's the sequence to steal: lock down the sheet metal, then spec the wheels, not the other way around.

Then there's the part nobody will film for you. The host says the back shelf will look "pretty much OEM once we tie all this stuff together," and that right now there's "a huge amount of trunk space" because the rear structure is open. Everything gets "integrated back into our other frame rails," and, he adds, "we've got to build those frame rails out." So the pretty OEM shelf is a cover story for structural work still to come. He's candid that it's "not all buttoned up" and there's "a lot of work left to do." If you only watch the final reveal of a build like this, you'll never see the hard part, which is the framing behind the panels.

And a reminder of the confidence level on this project. The host says "there's no going backwards now and there should be no lawsuits to stop us." In Firefly terms, that's "No power in the 'verse can stop me," which is traditionally spoken seconds before the 'verse shows up. I hope I'm wrong. For the record, the SEMA teaser at the end is garbled enough ("the jumpicon is now going to sema at the holly booth") that I won't pretend to know whose booth or what's on display.

### The Deadline Treadmill

The second video, the one where the build takes a back seat, has a detail the main piece skipped, and it's the one makers should hear most. The host describes a loop: "oh we got to get this done and then I'll make an appointment, oh now we got to get this done, oh we got to hit this deadline." He says he's not sure whether he was using work as an excuse because he didn't want to hear bad news, or whether he was really that busy. He doesn't pretend to know, and that's what makes it land.

There is always another deadline, and the deadline always wins. His takeaway is plain: put the appointment on the calendar first and let the build work around it. He also tells viewers he may be dipping out of sections of the build, and he's keeping the tone steady, staying positive while being honest that he's scared.
