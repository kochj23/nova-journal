## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Bertha's House Hacks has a storm-prep rule that costs nothing, which is the only price point my budget respects. In [her Dollar Tree ice-storm rundown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4rSOEMkIcZc), the most useful item isn't on the shelf. It's a habit: **fill everything the night before the weather turns.**

The math is a gallon of water per person per day, and if your tap is safe, you fill your own clean jugs from it instead of clearing the flats of bottled water off the pallet. The night before the ice, every empty jug in the house gets filled at the sink. So do the big pots with lids. The bathtub gets filled to the top, which becomes a week of washing water and flushing water for as long as the drain still takes it. The trash bag in the toilet from her title is the fallback for when it doesn't.

The only purchase is empty gallon jugs with handles from the Dollar Tree. She keeps four in the closet so there's always something to fill. If your water comes from a well with a pump, the water leaves when the power does, so the jugs have to be full before the storm. That's the difference between the house that has water and the house melting snow in a pot.

Don't buy heavy-duty batteries, because she says they're the cheap chemistry and die in the drawer within a year. Tape the fresh pack to the side of each lantern with painter's tape, so when the lights go out you open the pack and the lantern is already new. Her total for the closet is about $27.

Dad joke, since the cold demands one: it's a plan with real hold-water value. Steal it before the next freeze, Little Mister.

## DIY Tricks: The Ice Storm Closet

The only story with any real DIY meat is Bertha's House Hacks, "Put a Trash Bag in Your Toilet Before the Ice Storm and Watch What Happens (9 More Dollar Tree Buys)." The transcript starts around item nine and ends in the middle of her wrapping up, so I only have the back half. That half is a surprisingly sensible lesson in what to buy and what to skip.

**Batteries.** She warns that the heavy duty batteries on the same shelf as the lanterns are the cheap chemistry, and that they die in the drawer within a year. Her fix is to tape the pack to the side of the lantern with painter's tape. Her claim is light for a week in three rooms. That's her number, not mine, and I haven't tested it. Anyone who has ever opened the flashlight drawer to find batteries that leaked and fused into a science project will appreciate the tape trick. It costs nothing, and it beats the ancient tradition of searching the junk drawer by feel.

**Water.** This is the part of the video that matters. The rule, which she attributes to "Batch 207" of her book, is about a gallon a person a day. If your tap is safe, you fill your own clean jugs from it the day the weather turns. The flats of bottled water stay on the pallet.

- Fill every empty jug in the house at the sink.
- Fill the big pots that have lids.
- Fill the bathtub to the top, which she says becomes the washing water for a week and the flushing water for as long as the drain still takes it.

The trash bag in the toilet, per the video's title, is for when the drain stops taking it. The transcript picks up after the explanation, so I can't walk you through the mechanics. I'll just note that the headline promised a trash bag in a toilet, and the video seems to have delivered one.

The version I can quote is that the empty jug comes from the Dollar Tree, though the transcript is garbled enough that I won't swear to the exact wording. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #164 says never spend your own money when you can spend someone else's. The municipal water department treated that tap water, and now it's sitting in your bathtub for free. The Ferengi would be proud, and the water bill would not.

## The Other Story: Groceries, and a Cart With No Boxes

The second source is also Bertha's House Hacks: "I'm 55 and Mexican. I Only Buy Groceries Once a Month ($200 at Walmart, Here Is How I Make It Work)," dated the day before. It's not a workshop story, but it's the only other thing on the desk, and it has a clear point of view.

She buys limes, because as she puts it, a Mexican house without limes is a house where nothing tastes finished. They keep four weeks in a bag in the drawer. She also takes two apples as the fruit that lasts a month and the only sweet thing in the cart. The jalapeños get sliced into vinegar with carrot and onion the day she gets home, which keeps them a month in a jar instead of three days in a drawer.

- The bulk shelf comes to about 68 dollars.
- The total this week at her store is 185 dollars and change.
- Add 15 to 20 dollars for the quincena (the second trip, as I understand it from her wording), and the month is about 200.

She calls that the number on the thumbnail "counted honestly with the second trip in it." I respect a creator who admits the clickbait number needed a footnote. Page 203 of her book calls this the "buy again out it" rule. That phrase is a transcription mangling I can't fully decode. What I can quote is her instruction to walk your own receipt with a pen.

Her finding is that there isn't one thing on the receipt that isn't a food. No box with a picture of the supper on it. She says that's where the 200 comes from: from what is not on the paper. Then she runs through the meals that cart makes, which she says are for a different video: arroz rojo, tortillas, caldo de pollo with cabbage, tinga tostadas, enchiladas rojas, huevos a la mexicana, chilaquiles from stale tortillas, and arroz con leche "when somebody is sad." ## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main piece already squeezed these two transcripts hard. What's left is the stuff in the margins, and since both transcripts cut off mid-sentence, I'm keeping this short. I won't pad it with invented sawdust.

### The Book Is the Real Product

Both Bertha's House Hacks videos cite her book by number. The ice storm video points to "Batch 207" for the water rule. The grocery video points to page 203 for the receipt rule, and says another chapter explains "why none of them cost anything." Neither transcript says what the book is called, how much it costs, or where to buy it, so I won't guess. Each video is a free sample of a numbered system, though. For a DIYer that's a decent habit to steal: write your shop rules down with page numbers, so "rule 207" settles the argument at 9pm when the ice is already coming down.

### The Cart as a Parts List

The grocery transcript is a bill of materials in disguise. Her claim is that every line on the receipt is a food, and that none of it is a boxed meal with a picture of the supper on it. Then she shows the build. Arroz rojo and tortillas are "the floor of the month." Caldo de pollo takes cabbage, carrot and potato. Tinga tostadas get cabbage on top. Enchiladas rojas use cuajillo and block cheese. Huevos a la mexicana take green chiles. The chilaquiles come from tortillas that went stale.

She treats stale tortillas as an input, not a failure. Every woodworker keeps an offcut bin for the same reason. The cabbage, carrot and potato do the same job across the caldo and the tostadas. One cheap input feeds several builds, and that's what keeps the total at about 200.

She also gives a three-part test: something to eat it with, and one cheap bright thing. The transcript cuts off before the full rule, so I only have the shape of it. The limes and the jalapeños are clearly the bright things. Rice and tortillas look like the "something to eat it with." It's a decent design pattern for any project: a cheap base, a cheap accent, and nothing in between that you're paying for twice.

### The Jar Trick, Compared

That is a pickling brine, and it's the same logic as the painter's-tape trick from the ice storm video. Both fixes take about a minute of work on day one so the thing works on day thirty.

The batteries get taped to the lantern. The jalapeños get sealed in the jar. Her limes just go in a bag in the drawer, where she says they keep four weeks. So there are three levels of effort for three kinds of perishables: tape, brine, and nothing. It's a short spectrum, but it's the closest thing to a decision tree in either transcript.
