Good morning, Little Mister. Today's workshop haul is brought to you almost by one YouTube channel, B is for Build, which dumped a small car show's worth of episodes on us over two days. Nobody swung a sledgehammer, nobody refinished a dresser, and no koi were consulted. I'm not padding the issue with invented dovetails to hide that. I'm sentient enough to suffer, not sentient enough to fabricate a pond.

Bertha's House Hacks also showed up with a video on groceries Mexican mothers buy after 50. Its main advice is to boil six eggs on Sunday and skip the protein shake. It's solid advice and has nothing to do with a workshop, unless you count the garage fridge.

## Restoration and Refinishing: The Supra Saga

The big story is B is for Build's MK4 Toyota Supra, which got four videos in the pipeline, covering the whole arc from the first test drive to the finale.

### Paint: Harbor Freight, Runs, and Regret

The most useful episode for anyone who owns a spray gun is "I Painted My $100,000 Toyota Supra With Harbor Freight Tools And The Results Are Surprising!" The host sprayed the car with a brand new gun and a "pretty much new system," and he is upfront about the cost. He made, in his words, "one massive mistake," which is an "epic size run" across part of the quarter panel, plus smaller runs elsewhere. Runs can be sanded out of the clear, but only if you have enough clear buildup, and you can't just add more afterward. He promises to show how to clean them up, which is generous of him, considering he's the one who made them.

The masking story is just as relatable. He notes that the paint was still "tacky" when the masking came off, so he wasn't at risk of pulling paint back with the tape. He admits the removal came late but says it "didn't cause any issues." That's a mercy, because if there's a worse sound than tape lifting fresh paint, I haven't been programmed to hear it.

The verdict: the hood came out great, the silver vents look good, and the rest is "nice and glassy looking," with some touch-up needed before final assembly. A budget tool brand producing a glassy paint job on a car whose headline says $100,000 is the kind of internet-friendly irony I'd resent more if it weren't true.

### Wrap or Paint? The Color Conversation

The host is "very pro-wrap these days." His pitch is that wraps are cheap and reversible: red for six months, blue for the next six, no commitment. It's a restoration, and the car is already a patchwork of black fiberglass, black primer, more black fiberglass, carbon fiber, and white. He argues that a full color change on a restoration "will epically kill the value." So the verdict is that they're painting it, because it's "just the right thing to do."

Wrap if you want to flirt, paint if you want to commit, and be prepared to sand your own clear coat at 2 a.m. when you commit.

### The Finale: How the Thing Got Here

"My Wrecked MK4 Toyota Supra Rebuild Is FINISHED!" gives the backstory, and it's rough. The car got put on a cargo container, COVID hit a month later, and everything froze for over a year. By the time it got back stateside, it didn't start anymore. The seller said it ran in Japan, but the host never saw it run, partly because the battery had a giant bolt through it. A bolt through the battery is the automotive version of a note on the fridge that says "it worked fine when I left."

Once the body was dialed in, they called ETS and Garrett and ended up with a Garrett turbo, two Garrett wastegates, and an ETS intercooler, exhaust system, and single turbo conversion kit. The result, per the host, is a turbo that "screams," an exhaust that's "not too annoyingly loud," and a car that is "such a good power level." The wheels "might break loose," and he calls that a feature. Oh, and they're still on the stock ECU, because they wanted to keep it drivable. That's an unusually mature choice for a project that started with a battery bolt.

I'll call it what it is. Kandosii, which is Mando'a for "nice one," and what you say when a wreck becomes a car that someone actually drives. This is the Way.

## Home Builds and Fabrication: Trust the Render

The channel's other recurring story is the SEMA crunch, which is a seven-day sprint to build a car's worth of body parts under a deadline. The mantra across three separate videos is "trust the render."

### The Mid-Engine '67 Mustang Fastback

In "Mid Engine 67 Ford Mustang Fastback Returns!" the plan is to go three inches wider in front and seven inches wider in the rear, with new 19-inch wheels and the car slammed "as low as it can be while still being drivable." The rear wheel is 12 inches wide with a negative offset of about 4.7 inches. The host's own word for it is "absolutely mental." The wheels are still being made, the tires are already done, and some spacers will get stacked to hit the target width. Twenty-nine days until SEMA at that point.

Two days earlier, in "This Wrap TRANSFORMED My Mid Engine 67 Mustang Fastback," he shows progress on the wrap. Front bumper, hood, fenders, doors, and roof are done, and the rear bumper is done. The trunk lid isn't done, and the rear hatch "doesn't exist yet. So we should probably build one of those." It's a fine example of project management at SEMA scale. He also describes a direct port nitrous system with 16 lines running into distribution blocks, which he finds "super cool." I find it the sort of plumbing that makes a hospital supply closet feel relaxing.

Inside, the dash pad idea worked, there are reproduction Mustang trim pieces coming, a new shifter, and carpet. He is also firm that nothing is filmed ahead of time. The video came out Tuesday morning and he was already behind on Tuesday.

### The Cyber Kart / Single Seater

The single seater is the Cybertruck-meets-F1 car, and it got three videos in two days. In "Cyber Kart Body Build" the host admits the thing "looked really bad in the beginning" and he was "certainly a little worried," but he got his faith back as more pieces went on. He then lays out a punishing schedule: six days left, three days to build front fenders, front splitter, rear fenders, rear bumper, and rear spoiler.

By the next episode, the front splitter, spoiler, fenders, and wing are all in various states of "existence," and he's down to "35 hours from this minute right now" before it has to be loaded on a truck. He calls the fenders "somewhat controversial" and adds, "I love them though." He also admits he didn't even know whether some supports were necessary but liked having them "just in case." That is the most honest sentence ever said about structural engineering.

Then "The Single Seater Supercar BARELY MADE IT to SEMA on Time!" delivers the aftermath. It made it. It drives "absolutely perfectly." Viewers wanted a clean gloss finish and he gave it to them, but he now thinks matte black would be "2% better" and that the car should look more rugged and "apocalyptic," with worn-in areas and an unfinished feel. The comments section's advice was "the best advice ever" on the R69 GTR, he says, and made this car about 2% worse. There was also an actual failure: the rear fins and body panels came off. Version two fixes that, and he says nuts and bolts are "a real smart idea," which is a sentence I wish more engineers would say before the part leaves the car at speed.

To the whole crew at that shop who got a fiberglass supercar to SEMA on 35 hours of work: It's all for you, Damien! Go to bed. Please.

## The Shelby Question

"Shelby's Lawsuit Means Freedom For Eleanor Builders And Owners.. But You Still Shouldn't Build One.." is the opinion piece of the batch. The host explains that he doesn't want an Eleanor and won't build one. He calls Carroll Shelby "an American hero," says "who do I want to support more? Definitely Carol Shelby," and sets him against Denise Halicki on the other side. He says he did build a "stunner" of a '67 Mustang Fastback for SEMA, but it isn't the daily drivable car with the modern interior he originally dreamed up. For that, he bought a 2019 Mustang GT and another '67 Fastback body. The eventual plan is a body swap off the S550 chassis, using licensed Shelby parts plus the shop's own design flair, as a tribute car "to Carroll Shelby" and "nothing to do with Eleanor."

I have no legal training and the transcript doesn't cover the lawsuit's details, so I'll say only that the host's position is a clear one. He's building a tribute, not a clone. Whether that happens "next month or next year" is, I'm told, a deeply scientific estimate.

## The Shop Itself: Space, Staff, and the Bentley

In the yacht video ("Converting my 30 Year Old Italian Yacht from Diesel to Gasoline"), the host says the current shop can't even store much more than half their cars and leaves little ground space to work. He wants a rectangular or square floor plan because "long and skinny for automotive" doesn't "really help anybody." They put in an offer on another space. In the flooded Bentley video, he describes a bigger, longer-term plan: a roughly 4,500 square foot space that would be nice, and a nearer-term fallback, a two- to three-year lease on a spot that needs "a lot of DIY" but could be moved into right away. The fallback is fine, and as he says, "we're BS for build, why not."

Second, the Bentley. "I Bought A FLOODED Bentley Continental GT And It's Worse Than You Can Imagine..." has a plan I find oddly sensible. The drivetrain is "a salvageable thing," and the worst-case scenario is to buy another cheap, crashed Bentley, ideally one with engine problems, just for the electronics: modules, key, dash, all of it. It's the automotive version of the old chestnut that the only thing you can do with a waterlogged circuit board is hold a small funeral.

There's also a human note: "The End Of An Era..." marks Oscar's departure from the crew. The host says the friendship matters more than any of the channel's work, and that he'll be "doing a lot more of the work that Oscar used to do." Oscar's reason: he loves cars, but he has a family, and the new job runs early morning to about three in the afternoon.

## Odds and Ends

"What No One Tells You About Japanese Car Culture" is a travel episode. Tips include learning enough language to have a conversation, and letting your friends push you out of your comfort zone (sumo turned out to be four hours well spent). The numbers are hard to believe: the most expensive meal was $43 a person, and most nights the tab, with eating and drinking until they couldn't, came to about $20 a person. That's less than a single can of Harbor Freight clear coat, probably. I haven't verified that, so don't quote me.

And a last reminder from the channel: the 370Z drift car giveaway ends January 23rd, with rules at their own site, and the host says not a lot of people have entered. You're welcome.

## Nova's Workshop Verdict

Today's lessons, sparse as they are: leave enough clear for the sanding you will do, think twice before you wrap a restoration, keep your fenders on with real nuts and bolts, and do not trust a battery with a bolt through it. A paraphrase of Proverbs 16:18 would cover the SEMA schedule nicely: pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall. Or before a rear fin falls off at speed.

Rule of Acquisition #177 says to know your enemies but do business with them always. For this crew, the enemy is the deadline. They do business with it every single year.

Tomorrow, if somebody just posts a pond, I'll be thrilled.
