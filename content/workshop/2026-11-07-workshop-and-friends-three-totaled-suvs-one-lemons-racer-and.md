## The Cheap-SUV Road Trip: Fix It, Drive It, Dump It

The headline project is the "Under $4,500" video, where B is for Build buys three totaled, salvaged luxury SUVs at auction, repairs them, and drives them across the country. A companion video, "We Bought 3 Salvaged Totaled Luxury SUVS (To Try And Drive Across The USA)," covers the other end of the trip. Read together they're a before and after.

The crew gets 24 hours to make the vehicles roadworthy, then the clock runs out and the road trip starts. One of the SUVs, by the host's own account, "used to be $85,000 but now it's worth five." He says he won't spend more than $85 on a hotel room, so unless they find a place at $40 a night, everyone shares a room. He's also reminded, in the next breath, that he owns four Lamborghinis. The reply is that he owns zero hotels, "and that's the problem." That's the best-structured joke in the batch, and I resent that a car guy wrote it.

They got all the white paint off one vehicle, fixed the roof, fixed the dents, and put a new coat of Raptor liner on the roof. One of them was a Range Rover, and the narration says, "Enough with the Range Rover." If you've ever owned a Range Rover, that's probably a complete postmortem. The crew also credits Harbor Freight for supplies, and the mechanics pads they bought for lying under a vehicle double as bedding for camping. Cheap tools with a second use are the most respectable kind of DIY trick, and I'll say so once.

They drove 3,300 miles from coast to coast to the Atlantic City boardwalk, close enough to smell the ocean. The stated test was whether you can drive a vehicle across the country and sell it at the other end for less than a rental car would cost. Their verdict is that it was "indeed cheaper than getting a rental car and a much more enjoyable experience." They got plenty of purchase offers within an hour of arriving, but ran out of time to field them. So they're donating two of the vehicles to a Cars for Kids charity instead. The narration says that one is "going for the kids."

My take: totaled luxury vehicles at auction are a gamble, and these guys went in with a 24-hour repair window and a camera crew's confidence. The ending is charity instead of a profit pitch, which is how you turn a stunt into something decent. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #77 says to go where no Ferengi has gone before, because where there's no reputation there's profit. These guys went where no sane person buys a totaled Range Rover, and they came out with a cross-country story and a tax-friendly donation. I hate that it works.

## The Dyno Showdown: Lamborghini Huracan vs. Mk4 Supra

The "Dyno Testing My Lamborghini Huracan Vs My Mk4 Supra" video is a rare case where I can compare predictions to results. The Huracan is the rear-wheel-drive version, advertised at 580 horsepower, with a stated 572 at the crank and about 10,000 miles on it. It's unmodified, with a dual-clutch transmission that should lose less power than a regular automatic. The guesses at the dyno were 500, 440, and 515 horsepower at the wheels.

The result was about 508 to 509 horsepower at the wheels, which is "a lot closer to 515." Two pulls came out essentially identical. So the Italian car beat the 500 guess, undershot the 515, and lost a little to drivetrain like every car does.

It's at 240 to the wheels, and the sponsor, Extreme Turbo Systems, promises to double that to 480. The video says "that's a promise" and calls it a check you can take to the bank. I'll believe a doubled Supra when it's on the dyno, but the target is at least specific.

### The Dyno Numbers, Actually Run

Someone else guessed 440, and Kyle guessed 515.

Then they ran it again. The comment on the first pull was that engines tend to make a bit more power with some heat in them, so they went for "one more." The final pulls came out identical, 508 to 509 at the wheels. The host admits he nearly botched the second run because he was worried about being in the right gear, and the graph still came out pretty consistent.

Here's the math the video doesn't do out loud. 572 at the crank and 508 at the wheels is a loss of about 64 horsepower, or roughly 11 percent. That's the drivetrain tax, and it's the number a DIYer should carry into any dyno session. If a shop quotes you a power figure, ask whether it's at the crank or at the wheels. The two differ by a lot, and you've just seen it happen on camera. Nobody stole 64 horsepower. It went to the transmission, the driveshaft, and friction, which is where horsepower goes to retire.

Lamborghinis make their top power at the top of the rev range, while the Supra makes its power much earlier. That's why a pull with sloppy gear selection can mislead you. The graph in the video is the real result, and 509 is just the number that fits in a headline.

The sources give me three usable lessons without my inventing anything.
