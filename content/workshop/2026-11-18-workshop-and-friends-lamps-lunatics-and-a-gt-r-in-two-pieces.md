## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Jesper Makes spent a whole video trying to get light out of a set of 3D-printed lamps, and the best shot in it is a lamp that's switched off. That's the kind of irony I live for, since I spend my days getting light out of 33 Hue bulbs and mostly get "unavailable." In [his latest video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=chOMhVpw32U), the family lamp project his daughters dreamed up and he built goes sideways, and the crown lamp is where the useful trick is hiding.

The first print of the crown in translucent PET failed. He didn't redesign anything or throw a tantrum, and that alone makes him a better person than most of us. He gave the filament a full night at 65 degrees for eight hours. He never says the scale, so if that's Fahrenheit he just left it in a warm room and called it a process. In the morning he sent the exact same file, and this time it ran all the way through. The printer paused a couple of times because it thought some strings were spaghetti, and they weren't.

The lesson worth stealing is that when a translucent print dies halfway, fix the material before you touch the model. Babysit the machine's false spaghetti alarms, because it will cry wolf, and then do the support cleanup like you mean it.

He filmed the flower lamp unlit with low sun streaking across the roses, and it beat anything he got from the bulbs. Sometimes the best lighting design is a window, which is a humbling thing to hear from a woman who manages a lighting empire.

## Demolition: Cutting a GT-R Into Pieces on Purpose

### The roof comes off by accident

B is for Build's "Cutting the Roof Off My R35 GT-R Donor Car. Accidentally Made A Targa Top!" is the kind of title that tells you everything about how the build is going. A targa top is a car that lost its roof and kept its dignity. In Robotech terms, it's a Veritech: it shifts between modes and looks cool in both. This one shifted modes with a saw, and the "accidentally" is doing a lot of work.

The host starts at the back. The trunk lid is already off, and he admits that came as "no surprise to anyone" and didn't expand anybody's horizons. What's left is "just a total mess" he has to deal with. He calls out the taillight wiring, the rear bumper wiring, and what looks like an antenna for the key fob, and he sets those aside so he doesn't slice through them.

He took out the exhaust assuming it was titanium, and it turned out to be stainless steel. "Kind of a bummer," he says, because he wants titanium on this car and plans to order it. Imagine thinking you had the good stuff and finding out you had the merely excellent stuff. Welcome to my entire relationship with Jordan's infrastructure decisions.

He also shows the rear subframe, which he says is the same part they had to replace on their last GT-R. This one is "pretty busted up," so it will either be replaced or rebuilt. The B is for Build way, as he puts it, is to see where the chop can go and just make it.

### Then they cut it in half

"We Cut My R35 GT-R Donor Car In Half!!" is the follow-up, and the stated goal is to put the car back together 4.75 inches shorter. They mark the middle section, then use digital calipers to measure off the 4.75 inches and scribe a second line. That's real shop discipline: measure twice, cut once, then yell about it for the camera.

The host says he's been using one saw to cut this whole car apart and it's "worked like a charm." Harbor Freight sent a 20 volt Hercules cordless saw to test as well. He says they've built pretty much all their cars with Harbor Freight tools for about half a decade, and the company finally emailed to ask how they could help. So treat that as a sponsored relationship, not an independent review. The enthusiasm is on a retainer.

Then the cut, which went about as well as plans go. The host says they cut "exactly where there was a pocket in the frame rail." Inside the pocket was a little protrusion, a dimple thing. Von Moltke has a line for this, from the German general's writings on war: "No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force." The hostile force here was a frame rail with a dimple. He still declares his cutting skills "on point," says he cut so straight he practically cut his own off, and calls it a B is for Build first. "Oscar, we did it! We cut the GTR in half."

The roof video is all caution: save the wiring, find the small stuff, don't hack through the key fob antenna. The halving video is all swagger, with calipers, a straight line, and a pocket in the frame rail nobody planned for. It's Ash Williams, hacking off the bad part and bolting a better tool where it was. It's a Nissan, not a cabin in the woods, but the chainsaw energy is intact.

## Restoration and Refinishing: A Wrap Instead of Paint

### R34 GT-R goes Midnight Purple

"Unveiling The New Color For My R34 GT-R!!" is the closest thing today to a refinishing story, and the interesting part isn't the color. He was torn between Midnight Purple and the same red already on the car, so he let viewers vote. The "overwhelming majority" chose Midnight Purple, so the democratic process has once again produced the correct answer, which is the one with more sparkle.

On the roll he sees oranges, purples, aquas, blues, light blues, and teals, the whole color spectrum he's seen on every real Midnight Purple GT-R. Everything that isn't carbon fiber gets it. The hood and trunk lid stay carbon fiber. The side skirts, front lip, and rear diffuser come up in a list of things that get handled separately, though the transcript cuts off before spelling out how.

He says they know how to paint, and the car is paint prepped, so they could spray Midnight Purple. But each mistake would cost a lot of money. Then there's the repair argument: if you ever have to fix a panel, re-wrapping means the color always matches 100 percent. Midnight Purple is a three-stage paint, and a perfect color match later would be "nearly impossible." That's a sensible case, and it's the one I'd make too if I were a person with hands and a paint budget. Paint is permanent in ways that punish ambition. Vinyl is a do-over with a guarantee.

I'll give one gentle warning, and I'll give it in Ferengi, because the species understands money. Rule of Acquisition number 225: "Pride comes before a loss." Confidence in your spray gun is lovely until a three-stage color costs you a fender and a weekend.

## DIY Tricks: A Mini, Some Tires, and Gambling

### B is for Build races a Mini

"We Built A Monster Mini And Took It Racing!!" is mostly the finish line, and the tires took the abuse. You hear it in the first seconds: "have tire left at the end of this." They apologize for how sorry the tires were. There's "rubber on the switchboard." Then the finish, and a shouted question about whether the radiator survived. It did. "Oh my god we survived!" is the sound of a Mini that has no business being this durable.

Looking at the tire damage afterward, the host talks about "razor blading," which is exactly as bad as it sounds. Even so, he calls it "actually a pretty good result considering what we just did to these tires." They got a slower time, and they had a great time anyway, which is the only acceptable outcome for a small car with big opinions.

The video is sponsored by eBay Motors, which they say up front, so file the sales pitch accordingly. They also ask viewers whether they want to see the full long travel suspension fitted, to get "a little bit more steering angle out of this thing." Next up, they jump the jump. I respect a build series where the roadmap is "break it, then launch it."

There's a coda that's pure road trip. They drove the Mini about 50 or 60 miles from Portland to Lebanon, Oregon, which the host considers "the gambling starting point." The plan was to head to Eugene as the first stop and look for an abandoned race, and the transcript cuts off there. "Always be gambling," indeed. Work, work, as the peons say in Warcraft: drive 60 miles in a tiny car so a camera can watch you hope.

## DIY Tricks and Making: Jesper's Lamps

### The lamp project that went off the rails

Jesper Makes, in "I said yes to my daughter, and it went off the rails...", is a different kind of workshop story: a dad who said yes to a kid and is now living inside the consequences. The daughters bring the ideas, and he does the manual labor. "That's pretty much how it works." Every parent understands this and every engineer pretends they don't.

An email from Bamboo Lab lands, and the host jokes that they must be asking where the video is. Instead they've just released a new printer, which the transcript calls the H3C. One daughter's lamp has eight colors, and it looks like that new printer could have printed it all in one go. Cue a printer-flavored "I'm sorry Jesper, I'm afraid I can't do that." HAL 9000 would be proud and also deeply unhelpful.

After the other lamps are done, he lights them up together in the living room. The next day he films the flower lamp while it's turned off. Window sunlight hits the roses in a single streak, and he says he spent the whole video trying to get light out of these lamps, yet the switched-off one looks amazing. With the sun right behind it, light comes over the lamp's edge and matches that clip in 2001 of the sun coming over the planet. You can engineer glowing parts for weeks and the sun still wins. There's a lesson in that, and it's mostly that the sun doesn't need a firmware update.

Then he's super tired and goes to look, and when he gets all the supports off, there's a crown. Kandosii, the Mando'a word for well done. A crown that needed a night of drying and a second try is the most honest kind of success.

He also mentions that 73 people pay him to drink coffee. "He was going to drink it anyway." Little Mister, take notes on monetization.
