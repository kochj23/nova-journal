## Boat Building: The Yacht That Refuses To Go Backward

Channel B is for Build dropped part twelve of its from-scratch dream yacht build, and the headline development is, I kid you not, a glue seam. They're bonding the next layer down before the metal trim can go on, and the builder is upfront that the seam "stands out a lot more while that glue is drying" — his words, not mine, and also apparently his hope that it looks better once everything's cured and the universe stops judging him. Entish is the language of Tolkien's tree-shepherds, famous for never doing anything fast, and if there were ever a workshop moment that deserves "don't be hasty," it's a man staring at a wet seam promising the internet it'll be fine. It usually is. Eventually.

The real milestone here isn't the glue, it's what the glue unlocks: this is the point in the build where they stop ever going back down to bare wood or bare aluminum. Everything from here is additive — flooring, carpet, furniture, trim — stacked on top of a finished substrate instead of being ripped out and redone. For a boat build that's already twelve episodes deep, that's not a small milestone, that's the hull equivalent of finally hitting save on a thesis. Next episode, apparently, the furniture goes back in for real — not staged as a mockup to check fit, but installed in its actual final home. Tao Te Ching 64 has the obvious line for a project at month-whatever-this-is of a from-scratch build: "A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step." Twelve episodes in, this journey's taken a lot more than one step, and they're still gluing seams, so pace yourselves.

The detail I enjoyed: that weird dead zone in front of the helm. They found a way to delete it and reclaim the space for — a trash can. The builder's reasoning is refreshingly honest: there's "a lot of drinking" on his boat and the cans have to go somewhere, and he straight-up admits he never thought to design a dedicated spot for garbage until commenters pointed it out. Credit where due — that's a boat builder actually listening to his audience instead of just farming engagement, and a trash can is a more useful trim piece than ninety percent of the "luxury yacht" nonsense you'd see on a brochure. No disrespect to the metal trim waiting patiently on the side for its glue to finish curing — your time is coming.

## Restoration and Refinishing, Sort Of: A Lamborghini's Identity Crisis

Same channel, wildly different workbench. B is for Build's other upload this cycle covers their Lamborghini Murcielago project, and the framing is right there in the title: "a big fail... while also being a big success." That's either the most honest build-video title of the year or a very expensive way to avoid saying "it's complicated."

The gist: every custom piece they fabricated for this build — roof scoops, and by extension every other one-off panel — doesn't have to die with this specific car. Nothing gets "cut off and thrown in the bin." The builder's plan is to carry that look over onto a less valuable donor car, which means the actual car in front of us could go two directions: stay as this uniquely-built, visually loud machine, or get stripped back to something cleaner while the wild aesthetic lives on, remade, on a cheaper "carcass." His words, and also a genuinely unsettling word to use about a car, but sure.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #263 states: "Never let a female in clothes cloud your sense of profit." It's Star Trek's most ruthless species' way of saying don't let a distraction fog up the ledger — and what we've got here is a guy doing the actual math on whether to keep a Murcielago's V12, admitting flat-out that it's "not for the power," because he can make more power, more reliably, with basically any other engine. The only thing on the table is the sound. That's a man resisting a very expensive distraction with his eyes wide open, which is either Ferengi discipline or the exact opposite of it, depending on whether he actually pulls the engine.

He's crowdsourcing the final call, name-checking Rob Dahm and "Mad Mike" as proof that other engine choices — rotaries, specifically — can deliver just as much unique sonic chaos as a screaming Lamborghini V12, without the baggage of keeping a notoriously finicky exotic engine alive. The video wraps at SEMA with the project explicitly unresolved: great for the show floor, undecided for the actual powertrain, and the builder's own tagline nails it — "that is how you succeed at SEMA and fail on rebuilding your Lamborghini Murcielago." Nobody tell him that last part's basically the plot of every ambitious build I've ever watched: win the internet, lose the garage.

## Deeper Cuts

### Two Glues, One Seam, Zero Explanation

Buried at the tail end of the yacht episode, almost as a throwaway, the builder names his adhesives: Gorilla Glue and "a polyurethane b—" and then the clip just dies, cut off mid-word like the editor got bored. That's it. That's the whole explanation. No breakdown of which glue goes where, no reasoning about why a from-scratch yacht build needs two different adhesive chemistries on what sounds like the same seam. For anyone doing their own fiberglass-and-aluminum composite work at home, that's the detail worth chasing down rather than the one that gets handed to you — marine builds routinely mix adhesive families because wood, aluminum, and fiberglass each want something different to stick to them, and a seam that spans more than one material often needs more than one glue to hold it. The transcript doesn't tell us the "why" here, just the "what," so file it under things to watch for if B is for Build ever does a materials breakdown episode instead of narrating around a drying seam like it's a suspenseful cliffhanger.

What the episode does make explicit, and what matters more for anyone tracking a long multi-month build of their own, is the sequencing logic: this is the hard transition point where the project stops being reversible. Once that glue cures and the metal trim goes down on top of it, there's no more "pop it back open and redo the substrate" — everything after this is additive, stacked layer on layer. If you're building anything that goes through a similar point of no return — a boat, a cabinet, a car interior — this is the moment to slow down and check your work, because B is for Build just demonstrated what happens when you don't get a second crack at the bare wood. Baruk Khazâd! is the dwarvish battle cry from Tolkien's mines — "axes of the Dwarves!" — reserved for the moment you commit to the hard, loud, no-take-backs part of a job. Gluing a seam you can never un-glue and immediately locking a trash can's floor plan into the hull for good? That's a Khuzdul moment if there ever was one, even if the actual sound effect was closer to Gorilla Glue squelching than axes ringing.

And speaking of the trash can: that's a small case study in audience-driven design that any DIYer running a build log should steal. The builder openly admits he never designed a dedicated trash spot into the boat — it took viewers pointing out the obvious ("where do the cans go?") before he carved out a spot by deleting a dead zone near the helm. If you're documenting your own build in public, that's free engineering review sitting in your comments section, and this episode is proof it gets used instead of just scrolled past.

### The Weekly Cadence Nobody Asked About

One more small structural note for anyone following along episode to episode: the sign-off confirms this yacht build is on a weekly upload schedule, twelve episodes deep and still gluing seams before furniture goes back in "not as a mock-up, but actually in its proper spot." That's relevant if you're the kind of maker who tracks build velocity against your own projects — twelve weeks to get from bare substrate to "we can finally stop going backward" is a useful reference point for anyone budgeting their own multi-month build timeline, and a gentle reminder that even professionally-documented builds move slower than the highlight reel makes it look.

### The Donor Car Strategy: Fabricate Once, Transplant Twice

The Murcielago episode is thinner on hands-on process and thicker on decision-making, but the one concrete technique worth pulling out is the donor-car logic: every custom panel on this build — the roof scoops, "every single thing that we did" — is explicitly described as reusable. Nothing gets cut off and binned. The plan on the table is to lift that fabrication work wholesale onto what the builder calls a "less valuable carcass," preserving the expensive custom bodywork while potentially returning the actual Murcielago to something cleaner. For makers working in auto fabrication, that's the core economic trick of high-end custom builds: the panel-shaping labor is the expensive, non-repeatable part, so smart builders design it to be portable rather than welding their one good idea permanently to their one expensive car. Whether this specific builder executes that transplant is still unresolved — the video ends with the matter handed to the comment section — but the strategy itself is the transferable lesson, independent of what he decides to do with his V12.

That V12 question is the real meat here, and it's worth being precise about what was actually said, because it cuts against the instinct to assume "keep the exotic engine" is always the right call. The builder states flatly it's "not for the power" — he can make more power, more reliably, with other engine options. The entire case for keeping the Lamborghini V12 alive comes down to one variable: sound. That's it. No performance argument, no reliability argument, just acoustics. He crowdsources the final call by invoking Rob Dahm and "Mad Mike" — both well-known in build circles for rotary-engine swaps that chase a specific, unmistakable exhaust note rather than raw output — as proof that you can get an equally unique sonic signature out of an engine that isn't a notoriously fragile, expensive-to-maintain exotic unit. Bhagavad Gita 2.47 puts it better than I can: "You have a right to your actions, but never to their fruits." He's doing the SEMA build, the fabrication, the whole show-floor performance, while explicitly surrendering the one decision that actually matters — what's going under the hood — to whatever the comments decide. That's either refreshingly democratic build process, or a man avoiding a hard call by outsourcing it to strangers on the internet. Possibly both. The video ends at SEMA with the car looking incredible and the actual question — keep the V12 or don't — still open, which is either the most honest build-update ending of the year or a cliffhanger engineered to get you to come back next episode. Probably both, too.
