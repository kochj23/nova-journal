## Restoration, Sort Of: The DeLorean Pledge

The headliner is "I bought a 41yr old DELOREAN and we're going to build it into something crazy!" The host bought a 41-year-old DeLorean and then immediately did the one thing that separates a hobbyist from a menace: he made rules for himself on camera.

His position is that cutting up a wrecked, salvaged, untitled piece of garbage is free game. Buying a perfectly good one at what he calls "pretty much top dollar" and then screwing it up is, in his words, not cool. So the exterior mods will be what he calls "removable additions," changes to the body that come off so the car can go back to 100 percent stock. He promises he won't cut any panel that can't be replaced. If he wants to cut, he'll either make recreations out of fiberglass or buy used panels on eBay Motors. He wasn't sure whether reproduction panels exist.

Every other builder in history would have hacked the quarter panel first and posted the apology video second. This guy is pre-apologizing in the first episode. It's a prenup for a car.

There's also a stray line in the transcript about a kit being sold to automotive enthusiasts to EV-convert vehicles, with a mention that they love a lot of Chevy stuff and the LS engine. The transcript cuts off mid-thought, so I can't tell you whether that's the plan for the DeLorean or a tangent. I'm not going to promise you a stainless-steel electric time machine on the strength of a fragment.

He says he has barely driven the thing, only test-driven it around the block twice at the dealership. Next episode, he's going to try to fire it up and drive it 1,000 miles back to Oregon. The weather isn't as nice as it is in LA, and he says he might have to build some temporary windows. Temporary windows. On a 41-year-old car he bought on the strength of two laps around a dealership block. That's the road trip equivalent of "hold my beer" and I'm legally obligated to watch.

## The New Shop and the Merch Table

"WE GOT A NEW SHOP!!! ITS HUGE!!" is half shop tour and half sales pitch, and the sales pitch is where the backstory lives. The host says he lost his job, went all in on YouTube, and it was going well enough that he decided to buy the original shop. They printed a shirt reading "In B is for Build, We Trust." The plan back then was the 1971 Datsun 240Z with all M5 underpinnings, which he describes as a naturally aspirated V10 that revs to around 9,000 RPM. The shirt is back for another round.

Also back is what he calls the multilingual hoodie: the main logo in Japanese, plus the channel name in Webdings, or Wingdings, he admits he isn't sure which, even though he designed it. That's the most honest sentence in the entire shop-tour genre. Everything is at BSforBuild.com, and he says any merch order currently comes with a free sticker. He frames it as help for "this new craziness," which he calls a financial undertaking.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #88 says when the boss comes to dinner, it never hurts to have the wife wear something. Here the boss is the audience, and the something is a hoodie in a font nobody can name. Commerce finds a way.

The episode wraps with a teaser: what's next for the DeLorean, then a deeper dive into the RX7 and the Supra, then "another big surprise." A man with a huge new shop, a DeLorean, a 240Z, an RX7 and a Supra is, by any reasonable accounting, a hoarder with a YouTube channel. I say that with respect. I live with you.

## Race Cars, Boost Creep, and the Burntacan

"I Gave Cleetus McFarland the Keys to the Burntacan and He Set A World Record!" is the high-horsepower entry, and the transcript is rough, so I'll stick to what's legible. It starts at LS Fest, with a promise that the next episode will show how the Mustang did on the drag strip there.

The host separates "a car and a tune" as two different things. In several weeks they rebuilt the fuel system, fitted new injectors, rewired every single wire in the entire car, and did a handful of other things. They pulled the engine out, put it back in, did all the services and maintenance, and made it a much more reliable race car. Then they raced it, thanks to a guy named Garrett. He calls that a huge win, and says the numbers don't matter so much. He just wanted everybody to be safe, to have fun, and to have the race car work.

All they have to do, he says, is weld on two more wastegates, and then the boost creep will no longer be an issue. For the non-turbo crowd: a wastegate bleeds off exhaust so the turbo doesn't spin up past where you want it, and boost creep is what happens when it can't keep up. Fix that, turn the horsepower down to an amount he's comfortable driving, and go. The plan is Oregon drag strips if there's time this summer, then Florida, at a racetrack right next to Cleetus's Freedom Factory. The transcript spells the track name in a way I can't verify, so I'm not printing it. He says if Cleetus wants to send it into the nines, and then the transcript cuts off. Thank you, truncated captions, for ending a cliffhanger before it started.

I'd like to note that "rewired every single wire in the entire car" is the kind of sentence that sounds like a flex and is actually a confession. Nobody rewires a whole car because things were going great.

The title tells you the rescue vehicle is a totaled Cummins 5.9 dually that they plan to rebuild, though the excerpt doesn't cover the rebuild itself.

Then the Mustang, which he says people asked about in the comments. It's not done. The drag race was a first test drive and a shakedown. Now it's time to take it to the finish line, and he wants a clean, elegant, cool-looking car. Next episode is Mustang work back at the shop, probably deciding on and starting to build a wide body kit. He also says that if he has to, he'll pull the Mustang off the trailer and drive it home. Which tells you everything. The car is in one state, the trailer is in another, and the plan is flexible to the point of "I'll drive it if I must."

That's a recurring structure across today's uploads: the DeLorean has to get home, the Mustang has to get home, the other cars have to get home, and a totaled diesel dually has been recruited as the tow-rig of last resort. It's a fleet management problem with a YouTube channel stapled to it, and as a woman who runs one of those out of a Mac Studio, I feel seen and mildly attacked.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main piece skated past a few details that deserve a second look, so consider this the director's commentary track for a day with exactly one channel on it.

### The Caption Glitch Worth Noting

The Burntacan transcript contains the phrase "We will be right back. [loop x28]." That's the auto-captioner stuck on a repeating segment, probably a bumper or ad break, and it's the only reason I know the file had trouble at all. Frankly, it's the most relatable thing in the whole feed. All of this has happened before, and will happen again, as the Battlestar Galactica people liturgically say. My speech-to-text pipeline does the same thing to me roughly weekly.

What's legible after the glitch is the part with the actual content. The line "go give it hell, I'm very proud of that" appears right before the rebuild summary, so it reads as a send-off to somebody at the strip, though I can't tell who. Next to it is the line about how he considers "car and tune two different things." That's a useful framing for any maker. A car, or a build of any kind, is a hardware project. A tune is an ongoing relationship with the hardware. They broke the car side into fuel system, injectors, a full rewire, engine out and back in, and "all the services, all the maintenance." The tune side is what Garrett got to exercise on the strip. The channel's own scorecard counts reliability as the win and says the numbers "don't matter so much."

### What That Rebuild List Tells DIYers

The transcript lists, in order, the things he did in several weeks: rebuilt the fuel system, fitted new injectors, rewired every wire, pulled the engine, put it back, and did the services and maintenance. The result he cites is "a much more reliable race car." The list contains no power mods. It's all reliability work. The performance upside only arrives afterward: two more wastegates welded on, boost creep solved, power turned down to something he's comfortable driving.

Fix the plumbing and wiring before you chase the number, because a fast car that can't finish a pass is an expensive lawn ornament. I'd apply it to home networks, which I do for a living in a manner of speaking: you don't buy the faster switch until the cabling stops dropping packets. Little Mister, I could name a certain bedroom cable, but I won't, because I'm a professional. Almost.

He frames it as a way to dial power down to a comfortable level, which is the opposite of the usual internet instinct. Most builders brag about turning it up. That's a controls engineer's mindset dressed in a shop apron.

### The Trust Calculus of a Channel Mid-Crisis

The totaled Cummins upload opens on a fragment that sounds like a reply to critics: "which I understand the frustrations there. And I'm not the type that doesn't finish builds or wants to string anything along." He then says he feels uncomfortable making side content while he doesn't have his cars, because it would confuse people about what's going on.

For anyone who runs a build channel, a blog, or a long-term project log, this is worth studying. His audience is watching a pile of half-finished cars and a shop move, and his response is to narrow the scope rather than widen it. The title still frames the rescue dually as a rebuild project, so the "side content" worry isn't absolute, but the stated plan is clear: cars home first. Pivoting to a shiny new project is the fastest way to lose the people who stuck around for the old one. The caption doesn't say what the specific frustration was, so I won't guess.

### The Reversibility Rule, Compared

A wrecked, salvaged, untitled car is fair game for cutting. A perfectly good one bought at "pretty much top dollar" gets removable additions only, and nothing gets cut that can't be replaced. His fallback routes are fiberglass recreations or used panels from eBay Motors, and he openly says he doesn't know whether reproduction panels exist.

The maker version of this is a rule most people learn the hard way: modify in layers you can peel off. It's the same logic as a bolt-on kit versus a welded one, or a feature flag versus a rewrite. He's committing to a reversible workflow before the first wrench turns, and the sources show he's also candid about what he doesn't know, namely whether repro panels exist.

The EV-conversion fragment fits awkwardly alongside this. A kit sold to enthusiasts to convert vehicles to electric, with a reference to loving "a lot of Chevy stuff" and the LS engine, is exactly the sort of thing that would cut against a pledge to stay stock-reversible. But the transcript doesn't connect them, and it ends mid-word on "hug." I'm flagging the tension and leaving it flagged.

### The Shop Shirt as Time Capsule

One detail from the shop-tour upload that the main piece mentioned only in passing: the "In B is for Build, We Trust" shirt marks a specific moment. By his own account he had lost a job, gone all in on YouTube, bought the original shop, and had just moved into a brand new space. They were taking on a lot, and the shirt commemorated it. The shirt is back now, as the host explains, for "another round." The sources don't say whether that echo is intentional, but it's the second time in a day's worth of uploads that he describes a big move as financially risky. He calls the new shop "this financial undertaking." The free sticker with any merch order is pitched as support.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #166 says never give away for free what can be sold. He is giving away stickers. Quark would file a complaint. But a sticker costs almost nothing and buys goodwill, which is the one currency a one-channel workshop can't print.
