## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Bertha's House Hacks has a garage-sale lesson I'm stealing outright: buy the plain, heavy sewing machine, not the shiny crap that only got used twice. Her whole point is that the ugly thing on the folding table is a hardware store in disguise, and a $15 machine is worth nothing to a retailer and everything to you. Capitalism hates that.

The trick is a cheap resurrection that takes about as long as a bad cup of coffee. Before you pay, look under the needle and confirm the round metal bobbin case is there, because a machine missing that part is a paperweight with ambition. Then test the foot pedal and the wall outlet. At home, the rebuild is almost insultingly simple: a new needle, a drop of sewing machine oil wherever metal rubs metal, and a small brush to dig out 30 years of crud. If the cord is cracked, a repairman replaces it, because you don't get to freelance with mains power just because you watched a video.

The rule of thumb that makes this a plan instead of a hobby is that if the repair costs less than half the price of a new machine, repair wins. Her mother put real money into a machine like this and hemmed six kids' hand-me-downs with it, which is a better return than anything in your 401(k).

Full list of what to grab and what to walk past is in [Bertha's House Hacks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cutkVij24no). Go put your hands on three heavy things before you even glance at a shiny one.

## Home-Built Madness: A Skyline Body on a Newer Skeleton

### The headline job

B is for Build had a big day, three uploads, all about the same cursed masterpiece. The marquee one, "We Put a R34 GT-R Body On A R35 GT-R Chassis!", is the payoff. The host says they shortened the R35 chassis "by four and something inches" and set an R34 GT-R body on it. The other title claims five inches, and the transcript I have says four-and-change, so I'm going with "somewhere in the neighborhood of an inch of rounding error, which is also how most of my budget forecasts end."

The way he describes the week is the real story. They went to California and back, fixed "the whole alignment issue with the car being all crazy bent," and got the body on. He's clear that it isn't buttoned up and there's a lot of work left, but "this means it's gonna work guys," and there's "no going backwards now." He also tosses in that "there should be no lawsuits to stop us at this one," which I'm filing under "things you say out loud only when you've thought about it a lot."

I'll say what the transcript doesn't: taking a newer car's chassis, hacking inches out of it, and dropping an older icon's body on top is a Thing situation. John Carpenter's 1982 shape-shifter imitates its host perfectly, cell by cell, and you can't tell the copy from the original by looking. This build is going for that effect on purpose, and the only blood test that matters is whether the wheels fit.

### The details that are actually in the video

The wheel plan is a fresh design from Koenig with a lower offset. That's why it's a widebody: the R35 is wider than the R34 GT-R, so the body has to flare out to meet it. He says they'll run an 18 or a 19 inch wheel, which is a decision-in-progress, not a spec. The rear shelf should look pretty much OEM once everything's tied together. There's "a huge amount of trunk space" right now, which will get integrated back into the other frame rails, and those still need to be built out. So the answer to "where's the rest of the work" is "in the frame rails," which is where the work always is.

The teaser at the end is SEMA news coming in the next episode. I can't tell you more because the transcript is garbled right there, and I'm not going to invent a booth.

Kandosii, B is for Build. That's Mando'a for "nice one," and it's earned. This is the Way, if the Way involves a plasma cutter and a questionable relationship with symmetry.

## Restoration, Sort Of: Bertha's Garage-Sale Gospel

### The sewing machine is the whole sermon

Bertha's House Hacks ran "12 Things Mexican Mothers Always Buy at Garage Sales (And 5 They Walk Right Past)." I only have the tail end of the transcript, so I can't recite the whole twelve and I won't pretend to. The back half is the best part anyway.

Her checklist for a used one is concrete: confirm the round metal bobbin case is there, check the foot pedal, and plug it into the wall outlet to see it run. Then she says to add a new needle and a drop of sewing machine oil "wherever metal rubs metal," plus a small brush to clear thirty years of lint. The rule from page 210 of the book she's reading from is that when repair costs under half the price of new, repair wins.

A sewing machine bought for $15 hemmed six children's hand-me-downs, and she points out that someone charges $10 and more for a single hem. So a $15 machine pays itself back before the second pant leg. A bad habit of mine, honestly, is paying monthly for services that a $15 object and a drop of oil could replace.

### Shiny versus heavy

Her theory is that none of the twelve items look like anything from the street. The shiny things on the driveway are shiny because they were used twice. The plain heavy things are there because "they refuse to die and the house they lived in came to its end." That's a restoration philosophy in one line, and it beats any fancy refinishing video I could name.

She also has a quiet word on who profits when you keep driving the old thing: nobody planned it in an office, but a woman who buys one $15 sewing machine is worth nothing to a store, and a woman who buys the thin version of everything every third year is worth a fortune. Hence a country that learned to see clutter on the table where your grandmother saw a hardware store. That's the Ferengi in reverse. Never place friendship before profit, and you'll end up with a thin sewing machine every third year.

Her homework is good: stop at one sale (estate sale or church rummage sale counts) and put your hands on three plain heavy things before you look at one shiny one.

First, a correction to the main piece, which I smushed together. The six hand-me-down hems belong to the mother's machine, where "the book says she spent" real money. The $15 figure comes later, in the passage about who profits: a woman who buys one sewing machine for $15 "is worth nothing to a store." The title promises five things Mexican mothers walk past at garage sales, and I can't tell you what they are, because the transcript I have doesn't list them. Plug it into the wall and run it, which she gives ten seconds. Only after it passes does the real spending start: a new needle, a drop of oil wherever metal rubs metal, and a small brush for thirty years of lint. That's a smoke test, a cheap maintenance rite, and a decision threshold, which is more discipline than most of my deploy pipelines.
