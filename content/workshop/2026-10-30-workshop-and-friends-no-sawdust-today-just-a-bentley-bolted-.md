## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Hub adapters are supposed to be simple, and then you remember the horn exists. Build It Yourself's [K-Swapped Mustang Is Done!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fIkLaylzb-M) has the fix, and it's the kind of fiddly detail most swap videos skip because it's boring as hell.

The crew swapped the "bloated 90s Ford wheel" for a new OMP wheel. Getting the old one off was easy. Two bolts hold the airbag in, and once it's out, a single Torx bolt holds the wheel on. They hoped "a little pull" would do it, and it did.

The aftermarket hub adapter would normally kill the factory horn circuit, so they kept the original clock spring in the column. That meant the adapter had to fit around it, so they broke out the drill and cut some metal to make room for the horn wire to pass through. The result is a quick-release-style aftermarket wheel with a horn that still honks. The source cuts off mid-sentence, so I can't tell you how the wire was finished.

The same stretch of the build covers a custom bezel that lets a display slide into the original dash hole, and a gauge pod where each gauge was wired up and pushed into its hole. The white pieces of that bezel were stuck together with super glue. The builder calls this "the golden era of car modification", because you can design a custom part and have it made in days.

Tools named are a drill, metal cutting and super glue. The wheel itself needs only a Torx driver. Next up is a dyno run, where they'll find out if the K24 makes 500 wheel horsepower or turns into shrapnel.

That's the kind of safety fabrication you want explained to you before you sit in it at speed. ## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main piece covered the headline gags.

### The Seat Is Part of the Cage

The B is for Build roll cage video, "We Converted my E92 BMW 335i to a Bentley Continental GT Supersports!!", has a detail worth a second pass. The crew isn't only welding tubes around a driver. They're adding a bar that comes down and then out to support the back of the seat. The stated reason is specific: the support matters "when your seat gets too far away at any point from your back bar." In a hard rear-end hit, the seat flexes into that support instead of breaking off.

A cage is a system, and the seat is a load path. Most of us would weld the main hoop, admire it, and bolt a seat to the floor like it's a barstool. This crew treats the seat as one more thing that needs a defined place to stop. I'm not telling you to build a cage in your garage from a cartoon bit of transcript. I'm telling you the logic is worth stealing for any structure: ask what happens when the thing you attached moves, and give it somewhere to go.

The same video says the plan is to paint the cage, fit the harnesses and take a test drive. The speaker clarifies he means getting on the road as soon as possible, "not driving as fast as we can." Fine. A shop that says it out loud is a shop I trust slightly more than one that doesn't.

### Quarter-Inch Plates and the Adapter Problem

They aren't modifying the Bentley part to suit the BMW, or the other way around. They're making a bridge between two cars that were never meant to meet.

That's a pattern any DIYer will recognize. When two parts don't mate, you don't redesign both. You build an adapter and make it the thing that takes the abuse. Build It Yourself does the same thing in "K-Swapped Mustang Is Done!" with the hub adapter on the new OMP steering wheel.

### Two Channels, Two Philosophies on Keeping the Factory Part

Compare that to the B is for Build approach, where the factory car's front end is discarded in favor of a donor Bentley bar. Build It Yourself keeps the factory piece that does a job and trims only what's in the way. B is for Build cuts it all off and starts again. Both are legitimate. If you're building a one-off race car on a deadline, you chop. If you're doing a street build where the horn has to honk at a stoplight, you retain the clock spring.

That's the maker's takeaway if you want one. Ask whether the factory part is doing something you'd have to reinvent. If yes, work around it. If no, cut.

### The Cage Fits the Human

I mentioned the tall co-driver in the main piece, but I skipped the part that matters for makers. They sourced "the biggest seat from Corbo" and made as much seat room as possible. They also built roof standoffs to gain headroom, and ran the door bars "right up into the door."

The roof standoffs are the clever bit. If the roof is in the way of the person, you don't drop the person, you lift the roof contact point. The driver is the fixed constraint and the car bends around him. Most amateur builds do it backwards and then wonder why the driver's helmet is touching the cage.

The video also gives a small look at pacing. Anyone who's ever built anything knows that extra days vanish into the stuff you didn't plan for. The speaker says the aim is to test on Friday before the race, so the car doesn't show up "completely untested." That's a schedule discipline worth copying: put the test drive on the calendar before the build finishes, not after.

### What the Tesla Video Actually Teaches

The Tesla Model 3 video from B is for Build is more of an entertainment piece than a how-to, but it contains one honest observation that makers should hear. After cutting the body off and gaining a full second in the zero to 60 run, the speaker admits he "couldn't even really tell that it was that much faster" while test driving. "Really fast is just really, really fast."

I read that as a warning about measuring. You can build something that tests better and doesn't feel any different from the seat. If you're modifying anything, the stopwatch and the butt dyno are two separate instruments, and they will disagree. The source gives no detail on how the numbers were taken, so I won't pretend to know. The speaker's own comparison to the Plaid is his recollection, not a sourced spec, and I'm treating it that way.
