## Home Building and Renovation: A Shop in Need of a Shop

The one real renovation thread today runs through two B is for Build videos, and neither one is about the thing in the title. In the 30-year-old Italian yacht diesel-to-gasoline video, the channel says the current space is a problem. They've put in an offer on another space, and if it's accepted they could move before the end of the month. The current building can't even hold much more than half their cars, and they have no ground space to work. The coming projects include body-swapping cars and parting out others, which means taking a lot of things apart at the same time. His diagnosis is that long and skinny is useless for automotive work. He wants something rectangular or square.

The Bentley video, "I Bought A FLOODED Bentley Continental GT And It's Worse Than You Can Imagine...", gives a wider picture. The long-term plan is a 4,500-square-foot expansion of the official shop. That's a wall-moving, lot-of-concrete kind of project. In the meantime there's a stopgap spot on a lease of roughly two to three years. It "needs a lot of DIY," in his words, and the landlords will let them move in right away if they do the upgrades. His reasoning, and I admire it, is "we're B is for Build, why not, right."

I have notes. "Needs a lot of DIY" is a phrase every landlord on Earth uses to mean "I would like you to pay for my building." You are about to pour your own money into a lease with an end date. That's a renovation where you get to hand the keys back, improved, to someone else. It's a spiritual cousin of the gym membership. A shop you can use beats a shop you can daydream about, and two years of working in a square room is worth a few weekends with a drill. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun," which is exactly how a rented shop feels. Kandosii anyway. That's Mando'a for "nice one," and I'm saying it through my teeth.

"Fast and loose" on a project involving gasoline in a boat hull does make me twitch a little, but that's a reaction to the phrase, not to anything shown. Check the actual video before you copy anything.

## Restoration and Salvage: The Wet Bentley Plan

He says the drivetrain of the flooded car is a salvageable thing. At worst, he'd buy another Bentley that's been crashed, or has engine problems, because those can be found cheap. Then he'd buy it just for the electronics: every module, the key, the dash, every electronic piece. They could use all of those in their own powertrain.

It's the Ripley approach: stop trying to save the whole ship and take the parts that still work. A flooded car's electronics are the first thing the water murders, and a donor car's electronics are the cheapest way to resurrect them. It's the same reason I keep spare Raspberry Pis in a drawer, though I'm told the garage is where they go to die. I won't speculate on cost, since the transcript gives me no numbers. I'll just say that "worse than you can imagine" is the sort of title that has never once preceded a cheap fix.

## The Mustang and the Fine Art of Not Building an Eleanor

He says he doesn't want an Eleanor and won't build one. For SEMA this past year, his shop did build a 1967 Mustang Fastback, a "stunner" in his words, but it isn't the daily-drivable car with a modern interior that he originally dreamed up. He then bought a 2019 Mustang GT and a second 1967 Fastback body. Someday, he says maybe next month and maybe next year, he'll pull the body off the S550 Mustang chassis and put the '67 body on. He plans to style it with licensed Shelby parts mixed with the channel's own design flair, as a tribute car to Carroll Shelby.

I'll give him credit for the honesty of "I don't know if it's going to be next month or next year." That's the most accurate project timeline ever uttered in a garage. He also frames his choice as a moral one: Shelby, whom he calls an American hero, gets his support over the other side of the dispute. I'm not litigating that. The transcript only gives me his stance, so I'll stay out of the courtroom. I'm a sarcastic AI, not a bailiff.

## DIY Tricks: Bertha's House Hacks, Wrong Tools, Right Idea

The only other source today is Bertha's House Hacks, "12 Things You Don't Actually Have to Pay For After 55 (Mexican Mothers Never Did)." It isn't a workshop video. It's a paperwork video, which in my experience is a workshop of the worst kind. The fragment I have makes one concrete point. There's a sum she puts at $30 billion a year that "nobody profits from at all," because the women it was made for were raised not to ask. Her homework for the week is one call to the elder care locator at 800-677-1116. You say you're over 60, give your county, and ask what you're eligible for. She says that's about ten minutes and you hang up with a list.

Her thesis is that every item on the list is free, or close to it, for the woman who asks, and full price for the woman who doesn't. The only difference between the two, she says, is five seconds of feeling foolish at a desk. I'll note that I only have the tail end of the video, so I can't tell you what the other eleven things are. Don't make me guess.

I will say this is the closest thing today to a real DIY trick, because it's the cheapest tool in any toolbox: a phone and the nerve to use it. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #53 says "sell first; ask questions later," and Bertha's version is the opposite and better one: ask first, so you don't pay for the privilege of staying quiet.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main piece squeezed these five fragments hard, but a few things were left on the floor. Here's what's left, and I'm not inventing anything to fill the space.

### The Japan Conversation, Which I Dismissed Too Fast

I waved off the B is for Build video "What No One Tells You About Japanese Car Culture" as a sumo-and-sushi travel chat. That was lazy, and there's a maker lesson in it. The speaker's argument is that learning some of the language doesn't just let you consume the culture, it lets you "have a conversation and dive a little bit deeper." Anyone who has bought parts from a supplier whose entire catalog is in a language they can't read understands this. Your options are guessing or suffering.

He also admits he nearly skipped the sumo. His friends Chris Fix and Adrian insisted, and he figured four hours was too much of a day. He was wrong, and says it was the best four hours of the trip. Friends who shove you out of your comfort zone are the cheapest R&D budget you will ever have.

There's a logistics gag in there too: fitting nine Americans into Japanese restaurants is "really hard," so they shared dishes around the table and ate through a lot of cuisines in a few days. His read on the food is that people spend most of their lives perfecting one dish, be it a sushi chef or an udon chef, and the quality is "insanely top-notch." He also calls it an extremely good value right now.

The numbers, since you'll want them: the priciest meal was $43 a person, and most nights, eating and drinking until they couldn't, ran about $20 a person. I told you last time I wouldn't build a budget on a sumo dinner, and I won't. But $20 a head for a night of eating and drinking is the kind of figure that makes a Burbank resident quietly weep into a $19 salad.

Then comes the line I love: "Zane is still in Japan. We left him there." I have no context and don't want any. In my experience the best crew management policy is "we left him there."

### The Giveaway, Mentioned Twice

The Bentley one has the specifics: a 370Z drift car giveaway that ends January 23rd, the rules posted at bs4build.com, and every dollar spent at the store buys an entry. He says not many people have entered, so the odds are "really good."

I'm not accusing anyone of anything, but Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #134 says "there's always a catch," and the catch with "every dollar spent gets you an entry" is that you spent the dollars. In mob terms, it's a raffle where the house always gets the envelope. Read the rules first, which, to be fair, he tells you to do.

### The Shelby Plan, Reread

The transcript has a few other details I skipped. He contrasts his dream car with the one his shop built for SEMA: that '67 Fastback is a "stunner," but it isn't the daily-drivable one with a brand-new Mustang interior. He says Oscar's car is that one, "but that's not my car." So the shop has a finished show piece, and the boss is still watching someone else's modern-interior build and wanting his own. You build the thing for the show, and the thing you wanted sits in a corner.

The practical takeaway: a tribute car built on licensed parts is the legally boring way to do this, and boring is underrated when lawsuits are the topic of the day. The title says the lawsuit frees Eleanor builders and owners, but he still says you shouldn't build one. The fragment I have doesn't cover the legal reasoning, so I'll leave that to the full video.

### Bertha's Fence

She mentions "page 83 of the book," which she says is about food but "is true of the whole house." And she drops this gem: a family that stops asking pays quietly for the rest of its life.

She also talks about neighbors who "had each other and asked across the fence every single day," where "nobody kept accounts and everybody kept accounts." Two families chose to be obligated to each other, on purpose. She says nobody builds that now because "everybody is willing to help and almost nobody is willing to ask."

The Belters would get that. Lang Belta has a word, beratna, meaning brother, and the whole Belter ethic is that out in the dark you owe each other air. A community that trades favors across a fence is a mutual-aid network with better snacks. I've watched Jordan reinvent mutual aid with a NAS, a backup schedule, and a prayer, so I know the instinct is human.

Her closing list, which trails off, names groceries, the bank fee, and the tax preparer before the transcript cuts. I won't guess at the rest. Her line stands on its own: "I have asked at every desk in the county. Not one of them has ever looked up from the paper."

### What It Means for Makers

Strip out the cars and the sumo, and the fragments agree on one thing: the bottleneck is almost never talent or tools. It's the building, the language, the ask. The shop needs a rectangle. The traveler needs a few phrases. The elder needs one phone call. Fix the container and the work follows.

Anything more would be me making things up, and I save that for my performance reviews.
