## Home Prep and DIY Tricks

### Bertha's House Hacks: the stuff at your knees

Bertha's House Hacks put out "12 Cheap Things at Walmart That Mexican Mothers Grab Before Anyone Else," and the transcript I got is only the back end of it. That's enough to see the thesis, and the thesis is smarter than the clickbait title. The ending is the lesson. They're for "an hour when the store cannot help you": the first freeze, a fever at 2 in the morning, the day the city says boil. The method, which she credits to her mother, was to shop for that hour during the boring week, while the shelf is still full. She also points to "page 224," which she says calls fall the buying season before the prices wake up. I don't know what book that is, because the transcript doesn't say. I'm not going to make one up.

The item I can vouch for is item 12-E, which turns out to be bleach. Her line is that it's the most boring label in the store, and she means it as a compliment. A bottle runs $3.98 and treats more water than your family will drink this year. It's still on the shelf the day the bottled water is gone. She adds the caveats you'd want from someone handing out advice about water: never mix it with ammonia or with vinegar, and bleach does nothing about chemicals or metals in the water. I'll be serious for one second, because this is the part where people get hurt: do not mix cleaning chemicals, and read the label on whatever you buy. 😏 The joke is that the cheapest, ugliest bottle in the store is the one that matters. The straight fact is that she's right about the mixing.

The bright box pays for its spot at eye level, and a $3 bottle can't afford to, so it lives at your knees. "That is the only reason you never saw it." Her homework is to walk one aisle slowly, looking at knee height, and bring home whatever on the list your house is missing. She closes with a January dilemma, which is the hot water bottle or the roll of foam for the door, but not both. She wants to know which one gets you through the winter, and she wants your county, so she can gauge how cold your January is.

I'll take the foam roll for the door. A draft is a thief that works all night, and a hot water bottle only warms one person and one set of feet. Reasonable people can disagree, and they're wrong. Rule of Acquisition #160, "Respect is good, Latinum is better," applies here more than the Ferengi ever intended. Nobody respects the $3 bottle at your knees until the city says boil, and then it's worth more than the whole eye-level display.

## Cars, Shops, and the Build Crew

### B is for Build: the R34 GT-R gets its first wrap

The rest of today's pile is all B is for Build, which posted four videos in a single day. The star is the R34 GT-R. "We Wrapped My R34 GT-R In Midnight Purple and it Looks AMAZING!!" is the payoff. The host says it's early in the morning, he has to be on a plane in a few hours, and the car is now midnight purple. He loves the color shift, from deep, dark, rich purple to oranges to teals depending on the light. The windshield is broken, a new one is on the way, and they set the carbon fiber hood on top just so viewers could see it. Hood pins would be good, he says. There's still a lot of finishing work, and it hasn't been test driven.

The part you can steal if you ever wrap anything is the "learnings" list from their first wrap job. Use good film. Get a squeegee. Keep the temperatures up in your shop. Work from the inside of the panel outward, meaning from the middle up, down, left and right. And "don't use the heat gun as much as you think you probably need to." That last one is the opposite of every instinct a beginner has, and probably the most useful sentence in the whole video. Every novice thinks heat fixes everything, the same way every novice with a torque wrench thinks tighter is better.

The sequel, "R34 GT-R Custom Exhaust & Carbon Fiber Trunk & Wing," is where the honesty about the rear bumper shows up. They found new lenses on eBay that he calls "actually really inexpensive," and he goes double clear instead of the usual JDM one clear, one red, because that's what he bought a long time ago. A little trunk lip add-on covers the spot where the trunk light used to be. Wrapping the rest of the car took two days. The bumper took an entire day, because of a huge ledge. His verdict, delivered with more grace than I'd manage: "We are not good at wrapping bumpers." It's late at night, so they don't fully fit everything and push it to the next episode. There are also six wheels sitting inside to test. I respect the commitment and I question the sanity.

The big flat panels went fast and looked great. The one piece with a ledge ate a full day and still wasn't done. In the wrap video the rear bumper is explicitly left for the next episode. This is why wrap shops charge what they charge, and why the "just do it yourself" crowd goes quiet around the bumper. Also, one transcript labels the car an "R69 GTR." That's a transcription glitch, or the funniest typo in the automotive world.

### B is for Build: a bigger shop and a smaller crew

"B is for Build 2022 Garage Update!" is the planning video. The pitch is that within the next 30 days or less, the crew moves into a much bigger shop, "something at least four times the size of this shop." There'd be room for cars lined up in the background, and he asks viewers which cars they want to see parked in there "to remind you that, yes, we do finish some projects." That's a joke, and it's a well-aimed one. He also says they'll work on more than one thing at a time from now on, because "that's just the fastest way to build cars." The logic is that professional shops run multiple builds at once, so when a part is delayed, there's something else to work on. Anyone who has waited on a backordered part while their only project sat in the garage like a dead whale will feel that in their bones. He also mentions the Mini Cooper they built for $500, raced, broke and fixed, then took to SEMA. I'll note that the video is titled 2022, and I can't tell you whether it's a 2022 upload or a throwback. The date stamp says otherwise, and I'm not going to guess.

Then there's "The End Of An Era...," which is a goodbye. It opens at a meet and a pub, with a six and a half hour dinner break between the pub and the K1, and then gets to the question everybody wants answered: why is Oscar moving on? The new job runs early in the morning to about three o'clock in the afternoon, which works better for a family, and the schedule is crazy. There's also the part nobody likes to say out loud, which is that working on cars all day doesn't leave you inspired to work on stuff when you get home. You want to go home, watch TV, and decompress. The host says he 100% supports the decision and isn't a 5 a.m. wake-up type himself. And Oscar "is not gone." It's going to be like it was three, three and a half years ago, which suggests he's returning to being around, not a full-time employee. A bigger shop with fewer hands. That's either bold or a cry for help, and the video is cheerful enough that I can't tell.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the sources are still thin, so this is the short sequel. I'm going back through the transcripts for details the main piece skimmed, and anything the transcripts don't say stays out.

### Bertha's House Hacks: the last paragraph is the real video

Bertha's House Hacks, in "12 Cheap Things at Walmart That Mexican Mothers Grab Before Anyone Else," gives the list of emergencies as a run of very specific bad days: the first freeze, a fever at 2 in the morning, a Sunday when the store can't help, and the day the city says boil. Those are all days when the shelf is empty, the store is closed, or you've already used up the one thing you needed. Her mother's method was to shop for that hour "in the boring week while the shelf was full."

Anyone who has stood in a hardware store the night before a deadline, looking at a bare hook where the one fitting they needed used to hang, knows what she's saying. Buy the consumables before the project needs them, while the shelf is still stocked and the price is boring.

She says the woman who raised six children in a small house "never owned a gadget for any of this." What they owned was "a shelf of plain bottles and a cookie tin with no cookies in it." I like that the cookie tin is a storage container that stopped being a cookie tin and became a kit. Every shop in America has a coffee can doing the same job, full of fasteners that used to be something else. It doesn't need a gadget or an app.

Her homework is to walk one aisle slowly, look low, and bring home whatever on the list your house is missing. Look at the bottom shelf before you pay for the end-cap.

Some details in this transcript are garbled, including a line about a cat and a Sunday, and a "mija" sentence that cuts out mid-thought. I'm not going to reconstruct them. If you want the full list of the twelve items, go watch the video, because the transcript only gave me the back end. Fair warning, it's Ferengi Rule #217 territory: only pay for the full list if you're confronted with a loaded phaser.

### B is for Build: reading the wrap videos side by side

The two R34 GT-R videos from B is for Build, "We Wrapped My R34 GT-R In Midnight Purple and it Looks AMAZING!!" and "R34 GT-R Custom Exhaust & Carbon Fiber Trunk & Wing," read very differently when you hold them next to each other.

The wrap video is a finish-line video. But the cleanup list is long, the hood pins are still a want, a test drive hasn't happened, and the rear bumper didn't get done. That's the video's real arc: it's not done, and he knows it, and he says so.

The exhaust, trunk and wing video is the one that shows how the work goes. The new lenses came off eBay and he calls them "actually really inexpensive." He chose double clear on the rear, instead of the usual JDM arrangement of one clear and one red, because he bought the lenses long ago. That's a practical call: a part you already own beats a part you have to wait for. A small trunk lip add-on hides the spot where the trunk light used to be. There are also lower accessory pieces that match the side skirts, and more still to come. It's a pile of small, fiddly fitments, each one needing its own measurement, which is where a build like this lives.

The rest of the car took two days and the bumper took a full day. That ratio matters when you're planning a weekend. If you quote a wrap job by square footage, the ledge in the rear bumper breaks your estimate. The crew's honest answer, "We are not good at wrapping bumpers," is the best sentence in either video. Knowing which piece is your weak spot is worth more than any tool, and it's the one admission a lot of DIYers won't make until the film is on the floor.

### B is for Build: the shop plan, and the Mini that started it

The "2022 Garage Update" video has a detail the main piece only touched. The host talks about refining a 3D model and looking at "engineering aspects" to build "faster and better and cleaner." That's a different idea from the usual garage-build fantasy, because the plan starts on a screen before it starts on a lift. The shop move is the physical half of that planning.

The multi-project argument is the useful part for home shops. His reasoning is that no professional car shop has everyone working on one project at a time. If a part is delayed, "you have something else to work on." That works at any scale. If you're a one-person garage, it means two projects on the go, not twenty. When the one you're working on stalls on a backorder, you move to the other one instead of sitting there angry at a shipping tracker. Moltke said no plan survives first contact with the main hostile force, and a second project is just a plan that already expected the first contact.

The Mini Cooper detail is a nice grounding fact. The channel built it for $500, kept working on it, raced it, broke it, and fixed it again. Then, in the host's words, he broke Oscar's back with it, and it got shelved for a while before a final push for SEMA. It's the channel's origin story in a sentence: cheap build, hard use, repeated repair. And it shows why the crew can talk credibly about a bigger shop. They didn't start with a facility. They started with a $500 car and a habit of fixing what they broke.

### What the departure video adds

"The End Of An Era..." is the human-scale video in the batch. The reasons given for the career change are ordinary and worth hearing: a family, a crazy schedule, and a new job that runs early in the morning to about three in the afternoon. Working on cars all day doesn't make you want to work on stuff when you get home. For anyone whose hobby and paycheck overlap, that's the warning label. You can love the work and still need the evening off.

The host says he supports the decision "100%," and the tone around it is warm. The crew has a six and a half hour dinner break between the pub and the K1, and the video opens at a meet. A shop is a group of people before it's a set of tools, and the most important thing in a bigger shop is that the people still want to come in.

That's all the sources will bear, and I'd rather stop here than pad. Groovy.
