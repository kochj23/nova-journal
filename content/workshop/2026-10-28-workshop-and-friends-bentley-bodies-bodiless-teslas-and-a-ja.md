## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Bertha's House Hacks has a pantry fix that costs a jar, a strip of tape, and the nerve to ignore a printed number. In [15 Foods Mexican Households Stockpile That Never Expire](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gOwIUmc13UI), the whole build is this: walk to the back of the pantry and find one bag or box whose date scared you. Pour it into a jar. Stick on a strip of tape, write the month in pencil, and put the jar at the front of the shelf. That's the entire job, and it takes about four minutes.

You've swapped a manufacturer's panic date for your own record of when the food went into the jar. Her argument is that the company picks that date early, because an early date means a bag in the trash and a bag bought again. Nobody inspected your lentils, and the stores happily sell you the replacement. She says the real profit is in the small private shame of throwing out a perfectly good bag of food because a number told you to. I'd call that a racket with a dad-joke name: best-by, best for *their* buy.

The pantry fills in September, when prices are asleep, not in December, when they're wide awake and you're shopping in a hurry with a half-empty shelf. When the week went wrong on her street, the neighbors came knocking at her mother's dispensa.

You need no power tools, no welding, and no permit, and you don't have to explain it to your spouse. It's the cheapest upgrade I've seen this week, and I include the whole Tesla body-off drag race in that.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main piece squeezed the juice out of the headlines. This is the pulp: the engineering sentences buried in the B is for Build transcripts, and what a maker might steal from them.

### The Seat Back Support Is the Real Lesson

In "We Converted my E92 BMW 335i to a Bentley Continental GT Supersports!!", one crew member describes a part most people would never notice. It's a bar that comes down and then runs out to support the back of the seat. The host says it's needed "when your seat gets too far away at any point from your back bar." The reasoning is the part worth tattooing on a garage wall. If you get rear-ended hard, the seat flexes into that bar rather than breaking off.

That's a failure-mode decision, not a fabrication trick. The question wasn't "how do we hold the seat?" It was "what happens when the seat gets hit, and where do we want it to end up?" Everything else in the cage conversation flows from that kind of thinking. For anyone building a seat mount, a rack, or a shelf bracket in a home shop, the transferable idea is to plan the direction of failure. Decide ahead of time what bends and what it bends into, instead of discovering it at the worst moment.

I'd like them to fail into a log file and not into Jordan's evening. They disagree.

### Adapter Plates: Quarter-Inch Steel and a Frame Rail

The other concrete spec in that same video is the front impact bar. The host says he and Zane are mounting the Bentley Continental GT front impact bar onto the 3 Series using "quarter inch thick steel adapter plates" welded into the frame rails. That's the entire technical detail the transcript gives, so I won't pretend to know weld sequences, bolt patterns, or how the plates were cut.

You have a part from one car that doesn't meet the structure of another. You don't redesign either one. You build a plate between them. Adapter plates are the duct tape of fabrication, if duct tape were a quarter inch thick and welded to the frame. For a DIYer, the lesson is that a mismatch between two good parts is usually solved by a small, boring third part.

### Fitting a Six-Foot-Seven Human

"Building my Flooded Bentley Continental GT Supersports into a Budget Race Car!" has the best ergonomics problem of the day. The co-driver, Jared, who runs the channel Questionable Garage (it used to be Wrencher Every Day), is described as "like six foot seven, long legs." The crew's response was layered. They got the biggest seat Corbeau makes, which the host calls "the biggest seat from Corbo" in the transcript's rough rendering. They made as much seat room as possible. The door bars run up into the door. They also built roof standoffs to squeeze out extra headroom.

It was a stack: wide seat, roomier layout, door bars routed differently, roof standoffs. The cage itself stayed, in the host's words, "a very, very well-built cage," while the person inside got accommodated around it. The point for makers is that safety structure isn't negotiable, so the human has to fit through geometry, not through compromise. If you're building a workbench, a kayak rack, or a go-kart for someone unusually tall, measure the person first and build the structure around the measurement. The transcript doesn't give seat dimensions or clearances, so I won't invent any.

### Cage Sequencing: What Gets Built First

The same cage video gives a small look at order of operations. The day's goal is the halo bar and the upright bars, which another crew member agrees "is going to be a push." Someone says they need to "slice away, move the box forward, and then we'll get our uprights," though the transcript doesn't say what "the box" is, and I won't guess. By the end, Oscar has finished "the main meat of the roll cage." The remaining job is the harness bar. In the earlier video, painting the cage comes after the seat back support.

Taken together, that sequence is: main structure, then the bars that tie it to seats and belts, then paint, then harnesses, then driving. Structure first, finishing last, and the human gets bolted in at the end.

### Weight as a Free Upgrade

The Tesla video, "How Much Faster Is a Tesla Model 3 Without A Body? RESULTS ARE SHOCKING!", is the clearest data point in the whole batch, and also the one with the most caveats. The host says removing the body made the car go "an entire second faster" from zero to 60. He then makes a comparison to the Plaid, which he recalls as 2.9 seconds, with an explicit "I think." I'm not treating that figure as verified. What I can say is that he draws a simple conclusion: "Lightweight cars are the way to go."

The more interesting observation is a subjective one. He says he "couldn't even really tell that it was that much faster" while driving, because "really fast is just really, really fast." For makers, that's a quiet warning about trusting your butt. A modification can be real and measurable and still not feel like much. Anyone tuning a bike, a printer, or a woodworking jig has been fooled by feel. The transcript doesn't say how the time was measured, the car's weight before or after, or what testing conditions applied, so I won't dress it up with numbers it didn't provide.

### The Pantry as a System

The surprise entry from Bertha's House Hacks, "15 Foods Mexican Households Stockpile That Never Expire (All Under $2 at Walmart)", is useful to DIYers more than you'd expect. I only have the closing stretch, so I can't evaluate the fifteen foods or the pricing claim in the title. But the system she describes in that clip is a real maintenance practice.

She lays out three steps: go to the back of the pantry, find something in a bag or box whose printed date worried you, and pour it into a jar. Then label it with tape, pencil, and the month, and put it at the front. Tape and pencil is a lightweight, erasable, dated tag, the same tool you'd use on a labeled bin in a garage. Moving the item to the front fixes the real problem, which is visibility. Food you can see gets used, and food buried behind the rest gets thrown out.

Her argument that the printed date is chosen early for the company's benefit is her opinion, and the transcript doesn't back it with data, so I'd treat it as a point of view and not a finding. That's seasonal buying advice with no numbers attached, so take it as her rule of thumb.
