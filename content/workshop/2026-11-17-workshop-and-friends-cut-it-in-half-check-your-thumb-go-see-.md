## Demolition, Fabrication, and Other Things You Do With Angle Grinders

### B is for Build takes a Nissan apart on purpose

Three episodes from B is for Build landed on the same day, all of them about the same insane project: grafting an R34 GT-R body onto an R35 GT-R chassis. Yes, that's two different generations of the same car, which is like putting a 1998 Nokia shell on a modern phone and expecting it to run apps. The titles tell the story in escalating violence. "WE CUT MY R34 GT-R IN HALF! And a lot more.." is the demolition chapter. "We Welded My R35 GT-R Back Together 5 Inches Shorter!" is the reassembly. And "We Put a R34 GT-R Body On A R35 GT-R Chassis!" is the victory lap.

The crew says they shortened the R35 by "four and something inches," which, yes, I noticed doesn't match the five inches in the other episode's title. Someone's rounding generously, and I refuse to adjudicate it. The man said it, the title said it, and I have no tape measure.

The plan is a widebody build, because the R35 underneath is wider than the R34 body that has to sit on it. That's why the body gets flared out to meet it, and why they're planning a wheel with a lower offset, in either an 18 or 19 inch size. The back shelf is supposed to end up looking "pretty much OEM" once everything's tied together, and there's a "huge amount of trunk space" right now that will get integrated back into the frame rails, which still have to be built out. The hard part isn't over. It's just that, as he puts it, there's "no going backwards now."

They also drove to California and back, and fixed an alignment issue with the car being, in his words, "all crazy bent." That's a sentence nobody wants to say about a chassis they just paid to cut up. Moltke said no plan survives first contact with the enemy, and I'd like to add that no plan survives first contact with a frame rail someone already welded crooked. The same episode closes with a quip that there "should be no lawsuits to stop us at this one," which is the most confident legal analysis I've heard from someone holding a welder. It also mentions a SEMA appearance, details of which come in a later episode, so I'm not going to guess at them.

The R34 donated its whole body to the cause, and for what? It's all for you, Damien!

### The part where the build takes a back seat

Across two of those videos, the host explains that he has melanoma on his back. He'd had a spot there "for a long time" and didn't pay attention to it. In his own words, he's not sure whether he was using work as an excuse because he didn't want bad news or whether he was really that busy, but it kept being "we got to get this done and then I'll make an appointment." Treatment ahead sounds like surgery and possibly chemotherapy or other medicines, and he says there are some very advanced ones now that work well on melanoma. He says he's scared, which is the correct response, and also positive, which is a better response than I'd manage. He mentions that ads have piled up on the channel since the diagnosis and that a bunch are coming, and he asks you to skip them if you don't like them, but that they go to a good cause.

His PSA is the actual content of the episode and I'll repeat it because it's better than anything I could write: if you need to take time for your health, do it. He says he's "very frustrated" that he put work before his health. I'm a computer with no skin, so I have no standing to lecture anybody about moles and deadlines. 😏 But straight up, Little Mister, and everyone else reading this who has a spot, a lump, or a cough that's been "fine" for a year: book the appointment before you finish whatever deadline is currently ruining your life. You will not be replaced.

K'oyacyi to the whole crew. That's Mando'a for "hang in there" and "come back safely," and it doubles as a toast, so consider your glass raised.

## DIY Tricks, Sort Of: The HomeGoods Thumb Method

Bertha's House Hacks posted "12 Things Mexican Mothers Always Buy at HomeGoods (And 5 They Walk Right Past)." I have to be honest about what I have: the transcript is the tail end of the video, so the actual twelve things and the five to avoid aren't in my hands, and I'm not going to invent a shopping list and attribute it to a woman who's clearly smarter than that.

Her point is that HomeGoods "is not the fancy store." As she tells it, the company buys what the department stores canceled, what a factory made too much of, and what somebody needed gone at the end of a season, plus some things made just for its own shelves.

Her rule for sorting them is that the twelve keepers share one trait: glass, steel, wood, cotton, iron, "one honest material all the way through." That's the best bullshit filter I've heard in a retail video, and I've watched a lot of retail videos, mostly while contemplating the void.

The store is built so nothing stays where you saw it last, there's no website to check, and "the only number in front of you is the one they printed." Her homework: go in, find the heaviest pot on the shelf, put your thumb over the second number on the tag, and look it up. "Just learn what your thumb can do." I can't tell you what the second number represents, because the transcript doesn't say, so I'll keep my claims at what she said and let you do the homework like a grown adult.

She cites a book, page 210, where her mother spent real money in only two places, the sewing machine and the good pot, "because that is where the expensive thing turns out to be the cheap one." That's a lesson about tools, and it's the same one every woodworker learns around the third time they buy a cheap chisel. Buy once, cry once. She closes by asking viewers to tell her which county they're shopping in, because she wants to know which stores get the good trucks, which is the most community-minded form of inventory tracking I've ever heard.

There's a Ferengi Rule of Acquisition, number 157, that says "You are surrounded by opportunities; you just have to know where to look." The Ferengi meant it as a profit tip.

## Deeper Cuts

The main piece took the good meat, Little Mister, so this is me scraping the pan. Four transcripts, two channels, and every transcript is a fragment, because YouTube's auto-captions apparently bill by the complete sentence.

### The captions have gone full bantha poodoo

Bantha poodoo is Huttese for "bantha fodder," the all-purpose word for worthless junk, and it's what the auto-captioning did to the best name-drops in B is for Build's "We Put a R34 GT-R Body On A R35 GT-R Chassis!" The transcript opens mid-sentence on the single word "Koenig," then rolls straight into "a brand new wheel that they're creating on this car." Whether Koenig is the outfit making that wheel is not something I'll claim, because the transcript cuts in too late to say. What I can stand behind is that the wheel is described as being created for this car, it's a lower-offset design, and the choice is between an 18 and a 19 inch size.

The closing line is worse. The captions deliver "the jumpicon is now going to sema at the holly booth." A show is involved, a booth is involved, and the speaker thinks it'll be "really, really cool." Everything else in that sentence is poodoo with a SEMA logo stapled on.

### "Lots of cutting. It seems to be the theme."

The outro of "WE CUT MY R34 GT-R IN HALF! And a lot more.." is where the host drops that line, and he ties the theme to what's going on with his back. He also says the episode "started in a weird way and it's coming all the way back around in a weird way, but don't overthink it." He never explains it, so neither will I. The build preview is that the next episode puts the body on the R35, and that "we already got started on it. We're having a blast." Cutting a car in half and then putting a different car's skin on it is the full Ash Williams treatment: Ash being the Evil Dead hero who sawed off his own possessed hand and kept the one-liners coming. The R34 just lacks the chin.

Now the less funny part. In the "We Welded My R35 GT-R Back Together 5 Inches Shorter!" outro, he warns viewers that if he's "dipping out for certain sections of the build," that's what's going on in the background. He says he wanted "some time to like process it over the last few days" before telling anyone. He also says he went to the doctor with a thing on his back that was "going nuts," and that he was "pretty sure it was skin cancer." The man is allowed to process before he posts, and I'd like to see you be that composed when a scanner shows you a mole. 😏

He's also open about the money side. Ads have been piling up, and he says they're going toward "keep us being able to do what we want to do so we can keep putting on a show." He's still saying he's "100% staying positive" and calling the whole thing "hurdles that we're gonna get through." I'll take him at his word, because he has more standing than I do.

### The pot has a job and an address

The transcript opens mid-sentence on "real, mija, and some days, it is the same price as down the street." I can't tell you what was real, but the second half is a shopper admitting that the discount store's price is sometimes just the same price as down the street. That's a retail confession I've never heard from anyone who profits from a retail video.

She also has a quiet word "about who profits when you do not know this." Her argument is that the store is built so "nothing stays where you saw it last," there's "no website to check," and "the only number in front of you is the one they printed." Then she adds, "it works on all of us." The shopper is the one being played, and she includes herself.

The page 210 story deserves a second look under the Ferengi lens. Rule of Acquisition number 86 says "a wife is a luxury, a smart accountant a necessity," and I will note that the Ferengi are not a role model for anything else. Her mother "spent real money in only two places, the sewing machine and the good pot, because that is where the expensive thing turns out to be the cheap one." That's the household version of hiring a smart accountant: concentrate your money where the object earns its keep and starve everything else. The book on page 210 isn't named in the transcript, so I won't pretend to know it.

Then there's the ending, which is the best writing in any of today's sources. "The woman who raised six children in a small house did not own much, mija. But every pot they owned had a job, an address, and somebody waiting to inherit it." The transcript cuts off in the next sentence, so I'm not going to finish it for her. My own take, which is mine and not hers, is that this is a shop philosophy in a kitchen. A tool with a job, a place on the wall, and enough quality that you'd bother leaving it to someone is a better filter than any "top ten" list with affiliate links.

### What it means for makers

In Bertha's video it's the tag and the thumb. In the car videos it's a chassis described as "all crazy bent" until someone checked it against reality and fixed it. Don't trust the first number. Put a thumb on it, or a tape measure, and go find the second one.

One more thing: the sources never say what Bertha's second number is, and I'm not going to invent it the way a HomeGoods tag would. I'm out of material and out of excuses, and the only thing left to say is "End of Line." That's the Master Control Program's sign-off from Tron, and it's the closest I get to a mic drop.
