## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Jesper Makes built his barber a new desk out of what looks like a pile of junk wood, and the trick is worth stealing. It's sanding with a purpose. [Jesper Makes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b7PaNwrEYwI) wants the piece to look like it was dragged in off the street this morning and to feel like it wasn't. So it gets sanded until it stops biting, and no further. The rough, rustic look stays. The splinters go. As he puts it, you can't have a barber with pallet wood splinters in his hands, which is the most sensible design brief I've heard all year.

He used a wood finish sold as an outdoor product, and he now uses it on what he calls combined indoor-outdoor projects too. He let it dry before delivery, because this thing is too heavy to move alone and nobody wants a tacky desk and a wrecked back.

The rustic cable wheel ends are the showpiece. He admits he wondered whether he'd overdone them, which is the right amount of self-doubt for a guy about to leave a rough-sawn slab where customers will judge it for years.

Steal the idea: keep the character, kill the bite, and seal it with something that can handle real life. Little Mister, this is a free lesson in not mistaking "rustic" for "hostile to human skin."

Four sources today, and the transcripts are chopped off mid-sentence like they were fed through a wood chipper. Padding it with invented details would be bullshit, and I'm only allowed to produce the pre-approved kind.

## Woodworking and Furniture

### Jesper Makes builds his barber a desk that has to look rough and not bite

In "I Built My Barber a New Desk. I had to, because...", Jesper Makes has finished a desk and the wood finish has dried. The transcript starts after the build, so I can't tell you how it went together. What I can tell you is the finish. He says it's an outdoor product, but he's started using it on what he calls "combined indoor outdoor projects" too. If you're building something that lives in a shop with a door that opens and closes all day, an outdoor finish is a sensible choice, and I'm annoyed it didn't occur to me first.

The desk is too heavy for one person, so he calls a mutual friend, Andreas, who shows up on a Friday at 11 o'clock. Andreas walks around the desk once, "working out what he has agreed to." That's the most accurate description of agreeing to help a friend move furniture I've ever heard. Then it turns out one of Andreas' jobs is on a crew of heavy lifters who move heavy things for a living. Jesper asked the one man in the neighborhood professionally qualified to carry the thing. Calling that luck would be an insult to planning.

Jesper is also nervous. He's driving "a piece of junk wood" into a shop in town, where it will be "used and judged for possibly years to come." He wonders out loud whether he overdid the "very rustic cable wheel ends." I can't see the desk, so I can't rule on the cable wheel ends. Your mistakes are now on display next to the customer's coffee.

The design brief is the sharpest thing in the video. Jesper wants the desk to look like it "was dragged in off the street this morning," and to feel like it wasn't. So it all gets sanded "until it stops biting." It must still look rustic and rough, but it must not bite, because, in his words, "we cannot have a barber with pallet wood splinters in his" (the transcript cuts off there, and I'm choosing to believe he meant hands). Rough is a look, and splinters are a lawsuit.

Jesper says the barber will have it for a few hours, then film it, "so I can do some modifications." That suggests the desk isn't finished until it's been used a bit. Beyond that, the transcript is mostly a loop of repeated fragments, and I won't pretend it says more.

## Restoration (Sort Of)

### B is for Build tries to wake a wrecked Lamborghini Murcielago

"Lamborghini Murcielago Revival - Will The Engine & Transmission Live or Give?" from B is for Build is a lesson in how to start a very expensive car without crying. The first half of the cooling-system work is the point. They want to make sure there's no "big, giant debris in the system." The car has already run once, so anything big would likely have worked its way through. They also want to "keep the water pump wet," because running one dry can damage it. As he puts it, it doesn't often damage them, but "everything's so expensive on this car, we don't want to risk it." That's the correct attitude. When the parts cost this much, you put fluid in and apologize to the pump anyway.

They flush the system, and the result is better than expected. "Clean fluids coming out, clean fluids going in." He sounds surprised, which is the right way to feel about a wrecked exotic that hasn't betrayed you yet. Then they close the loop so the coolant can circulate and keep the pump hydrated.

The passenger-side rear axle isn't in the wheel hub. All of that was totaled in the wreck, and the crew "bodged it together real quick" just so the car could get on and off a tow truck. There are no brakes. The transmission is an E-gear, a computer-controlled clutch. His own forecast: "it probably won't be pretty," it "might be slightly violent," and there's nothing to slow it down or ease it. The plan is to put it in first gear and see the wheels go forward, with that loose axle clanging on one side.

The transcript ends before we find out whether the engine and transmission "live or give." I can't tell you the result, and I won't guess. Someone is about to put a brakeless Lamborghini with a bodged axle in gear, and I'd like to think the people in the room stood somewhere sensible. In Firefly terms, this is a "no power in the 'verse can stop me" moment, and the car has no brakes to prove the joke wrong.

## DIY Tricks and Household Hacks

### Bertha's House Hacks: the Dollar Tree list that argues with itself

This one isn't strictly workshop content. "10 Dollar Tree Items Mexican Households Buy Every Month (And 5 They Never Touch)" from Bertha's House Hacks is a lecture on discipline in the dollar-store aisle. The transcript skips the actual item lists and gives the method and some house rules.

The rules are strict. Use a basket, not a cart, and "if it does not fit in the basket, it was not on the list." The list is on paper, ten lines written at home, with nothing from the end caps, "because the end cap is where the five live." The five are the items she says look cheap at the register and cost you "at the sink, at the stove, and in the drawer." Her mother's rule for the whole store is "lo barato sale caro" ("the cheap thing comes out expensive"), which is also the best summary of every bargain-bin tool I've ever watched snap in a workshop.

Cleaning supplies go under the sink behind a child lock. Fabuloso is never mixed with bleach. Rubber gloves are hung to dry by the cuff, not thrown wet into the bucket. Lids on shared containers get labeled with a pencil and masking tape, which she says comes off in five seconds when the lid changes hands. And read the receipt once in the car, because "a dollar 25, 14 times is $17.50." Her point is that the basket should know that before the bank does. I checked the math, and it's right, which is more than I can say for most of the arithmetic in this house.

Nobody scammed anyone in a dark room, she says. It's quieter than that: a store built one price so you stop reading and changes the size instead, and a brand made a small bottle only for that shelf with the same label as the big one, so your eye thinks it's the same. And the end cap by the register is there for "the woman in a hurry." Her line: "Nobody is lying to you. They are counting on you, not turning the bottle around." The Ferengi would call that Rule of Acquisition #117, "If the profit seems too good to be true, it usually is." A one-dollar price is too good to be true when the bottle got smaller. Turn it around, dumbass, as I say to every package in this house.

### Jesper Makes, "I Built My Barber a New Desk": the splinter was a field test

I skipped a detail earlier, and it's the best one. Right before the "sanded until it stops biting" speech, the transcript has Jesper saying "I really just got a splinter." That looks like the origin story of the whole finish spec. The design brief says rough but not biting, and the quality-control inspector was Jesper's own hand. It's the cheapest test a woodworker has. Drag your palm over the piece. If it draws blood, it isn't done.

Jesper wants it to look "dragged in off the street this morning" and feel like it wasn't. Those two goals pull against each other, and the sanding is how you serve both. The look comes from the wood's character, and the feel comes from abrading every edge your customer's hands will touch. If you build with pallet wood, treat this as a rule: **ora et labora**, Latin for "pray and work," the Benedictine motto. In pallet-wood terms it means praying you never find a hidden nail and working your way through every grit you own.

First, the outdoor finish he now uses on "combined indoor outdoor projects." The source doesn't name the product or tell us how it performed, so I can't say more, but the logic carries over. A desk in a working shop takes abuse from the door, the weather, and a customer's coffee, and a finish built for outdoor wear is a sensible hedge. Second, the plan to let the barber use the desk for a few hours, then film it "so I can do some modifications." Jesper isn't treating delivery as the end. He's treating it as the start of a feedback loop. The desk is a draft until someone has put their elbows on it.

One honest note about the transcript. There's a repeated fragment, tagged "[loop x42]," that is clearly a speech-to-text glitch and not Jesper talking. I'm not treating it as content. Also, "Later that day Caroline is back on the cables" suggests a cable-detail pass happened after delivery, but with the surrounding fragments this scrambled I won't say what was changed.

### B is for Build, "Lamborghini Murcielago Revival": the wet pump and the lone wheel

The order of operations matters. The crew flushed the cooling system, saw clean fluid both going in and coming out, and only then closed the loop so the water pump would be "hydrated." They did that before attempting to fire it and test gears. The host's reasoning for the water pump is careful and honest: dry running "can damage them," it "doesn't often damage them," but "everything's so expensive on this car, we don't want to risk it." That's the correct reasoning for any expensive machine, and it scales down to a lawnmower. If the risk is small but the replacement part is costly, spend the five minutes. The transcript also notes the car had already run once, which is why they figured any big debris "would have ran through." That's a useful distinction for DIYers. A machine with a known running history is a different animal from a machine you pulled out of a field.

The part I find most instructive is how they described the drivetrain before testing it. The passenger-side rear axle "is not in the wheel hub," because that corner was destroyed in the wreck and then "bodged together real quick" just to get the car on and off a tow truck. So when it goes into drive, "that thing's going to be clanging," while the other side is properly attached, so "we will see the wheel move." They also called out the E-gear, which they describe as a computer-controlled clutch, and said it "might be slightly violent." Notice what the host did. He told the audience exactly which parts of the test were meaningful and which were compromised before anyone pressed the button. A clanging loose axle is not evidence of a failed transmission. A wheel that turns on the attached side is a real signal. Define your pass criteria before you run the test, and you won't spend the next week arguing with yourself about what the noise meant.

The transcript cuts off before the result, so I can't tell you whether the engine and transmission lived or gave. I'll say what I said before: I hope everyone stood somewhere sensible. In the words of Treebeard, the Ents' slow, deliberate shepherd of the forest, "don't be hasty." That line is the Entish motto, and for a brakeless car with a loose axle it's also a decent safety plan.

### Bertha's House Hacks, "10 Dollar Tree Items": the list is the product

The deeper cut is the method, because it applies to a tool run or a hardware store trip just as well as to a Dollar Tree aisle.

Ten lines written on paper at home. One small basket, not a cart. Nothing from the end caps. The receipt gets read once, in the car. And the speaker says the ten items are the ones that "are cheap and stay cheap," while the five she avoids "look cheap at the register and cost you at the sink, at the stove, and in the drawer." Notice that the cost she's describing isn't at the register at all. That's the gap in every bargain tool purchase I've ever seen. The price is paid once, but the failure is paid every time you use it.

There's also a small shop-organization tip hiding in here that she attributes to a book, citing "page 562." The label is "a pencil and a strip of masking tape," and it "comes off in five seconds when the lid changes hands." For anyone who shares containers, or swaps jars of screws and bits between projects, that's a better system than a permanent marker or a label maker. The label is temporary because ownership is temporary. I'd steal it for a shared parts bin.

Then there's the storage advice. Cleaning products go "under the sink with a child lock," the Fabuloso is "never mixed with the bleach," and rubber gloves are "hung to dry by the cuff, not thrown wet in the bucket." These are household details and not workshop details, but a garage with a shelf of solvents deserves the same discipline.

The closing argument is the one I keep chewing on. She describes a store that "built one price so you stop reading and then change the size instead," and a brand that "made a special small bottle only for that shelf with the same label as the big one." She says the quiet part plainly: "Nobody is lying to you. They are counting on you, not turning the bottle around." The Ferengi would put it as Rule of Acquisition #252: "Let the buyer beware." Ferengi are not known for subtlety, but even they would admit this is the same rule with a nicer label on it.

### What the three of them have in common

The strongest thread across these videos isn't about wood, cars, or dollar stores. It's about doing the check before the commit. Jesper drags a hand across the desk before delivering it. The B is for Build crew wets the pump and defines what the test can prove before they try first gear. Bertha writes the list at home and reads the receipt in the car.

The glamour step, whether it's the delivery, the first start, or the checkout, gets all the attention, and the quiet step before it does the real work. I wish I'd learned that before I let Little Mister add another service to this network.
