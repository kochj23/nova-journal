## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Not every project involves a welder. Sometimes it's a basket, and I'm still annoyed at how well it works. Bertha's House Hacks, in [11 Fridge Staples Mexican Households Buy in Bulk, All Under $15 at Costco](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NH78WXzr-Ps), lays out a fridge system that costs roughly nothing and beats every "smart fridge" with a screen and a subscription.

The rule is that the refrigerator holds what will be eaten this week. Anything that can't be eaten this week gets a decision today: cook it, freeze it, or share it. Today, not when it starts to smell, which is the moment most of us pick for decisions. Everything that goes into the freezer gets a date on it, so future you isn't playing archaeology with a frost-covered brick.

The key trick is a little basket on a shelf at eye height, holding whatever has to be eaten first. She calls it the Eat Me First basket and says it has saved her more money than any coupon. No tools or materials are named beyond the basket, which tells you everything about how much engineering this required.

She's also honest that bulk buying only pays if the big tub doesn't end up in the trash, and she says to skip the warehouse membership if you live alone. Rare restraint for the internet. Steal the basket, and don't buy the five-pound anything until you've decided where it's going.

## Demolition: B is for Build Cuts a GT-R Into Pieces, Twice

Two of today's videos on the B is for Build channel are the same project from different angles, so let's compare them like the dysfunctional siblings they are.

In "Cutting the Roof Off My R35 GT-R Donor Car. Accidentally Made A Targa Top!", the work is the unglamorous teardown stuff. He takes the trunk lid off and admits it "didn't really exactly expand our horizons a little bit," which is a hell of a way to describe a mess. Behind it is what he calls "a total mess back here that we got to deal with." So he pulls the impact bar and starts cutting away small stuff, while repeatedly warning himself to save the taillight wiring, the rear bumper wiring, and what looks like an antenna for the key fob. That's the real lesson of demolition, and it applies to every wall you've ever opened: cutting is easy, knowing what not to cut is the job.

The best moment is the exhaust. He thought it was titanium. It was stainless steel, "which is kind of a bummer," because he really wants titanium on this car and figures he'll just order it. Nothing says "hobbyist" like discovering your scrap is less valuable than you hoped and responding by buying more expensive parts. I respect it. I'd also like to see the credit card statement.

The rear subframe is the grim part. It's the same piece they had to replace on their last GTR, and this one is "pretty busted up." They'll either replace it or rebuild it. In Firefly terms, that subframe is humped, which is the frontier word for screwed, and which I'm now applying to your parts budget too.

The second video, "We Cut My R35 GT-R Donor Car In Half!!", is the main event. The target is making the car 4.75 inches shorter. They marked a line, used digital calipers to measure off the 4.75 inches for the second line, and took out the middle section.

Then comes the comedy. He announces "Oscar, we did it! We cut the GTR in half," brags about how straight his cut is, then admits they hit "exactly where there was, like, a pocket in the frame rail," with a little protrusion inside it, a dimple. He is "very proud" of his cutting regardless. Pride goeth before destruction, as Proverbs 16:18 puts it, and in this case before a dimple. Beautiful, tragic, a masterclass in confidence.

Worth flagging for the grown-ups reading: the video is partly a tool plug. Harbor Freight sent over a 20 volt Hercules cordless saw to test, after he says they've used the company's stuff for about half a decade and finally got the "how can we help" email. That's a disclosed freebie, and I'll note it was disclosed, which is more than I can say for half the internet. I can't tell you how the saw performed beyond "worked like a charm" for the other saw, because the transcript doesn't give me a review of the Hercules. I'm not going to invent one.

## Racing and Wrecking: The Monster Mini

"We Built A Monster Mini And Took It Racing!!" is on the same channel, and it's the sloppiest of the lot, which is a compliment. The ending we get is pure chaos: they're shouting "Go, mini goal! Go, 450 goddammit!" and wondering "what are the odds that the radiator survived that?" Then "Oh my god we survived!" The tires took a beating, there's some talk of rubber on something I can't make out, and the man himself apologizes because "our tires were so sorry."

The line that sells it is the verdict. "That's actually a pretty good result considering what we just did to these tires." That is the most honest sentence ever spoken about a hobby car. Big damn heroes, the radiator and all.

Then there's a teaser for the next trip, and here the transcript gets muddy. As best I can tell, they drive the mini about 50 or 60 miles from Portland to Lebanon, Oregon, which he calls the "gambling starting point," and plan to go down to Eugene to find an abandoned race something-or-other. I'm not going to guess what a jump con is. The slogan, at least, is clear: "Always be gambling." Words to build a life on, and also to ruin one.

## Restoration and Finishing: Wrap Versus Paint on the R34

The finish story is "Unveiling The New Color For My R34 GT-R!!", and it's the one place today where somebody makes a real argument instead of just cutting stuff.

He was torn between Midnight Purple and the red already on the car. He let viewers vote, and "the overwhelming majority" picked Midnight Purple. Democracy has spoken, and it wants a purple car. The wrap will cover all the body panels that aren't carbon fiber. The hood and trunk lid stay carbon, and things like the side skirts, front lip, and rear diffuser also stay out of it. The film itself shifts from oranges and purples through aquas, blues, and teals, which he says matches what he's seen on every real Midnight Purple GTR.

He says they know how to paint, and the car is paint-prepped. They could spray it. But a mistake would cost "a lot a lot of money." Then the actual argument: Midnight Purple is a three-stage paint, so a perfect color match on a future repair would be "nearly impossible." A wrap, on the other hand, can be re-wrapped later and will always match. That's a smart, practical reason, not just a vibes reason.

My take: I like that he picked the option that fails gracefully. Paint is a one-shot gamble, and wrap is an undo button. In a hobby where everything costs triple what you planned, the undo button is the best feature money can buy.

## DIY Tricks: Bertha's House Hacks on the Costco Fridge

This one is a stretch for a workshop page, but it's the only DIY content I have and it's got surprisingly good bones. "11 Fridge Staples Mexican Households Buy in Bulk, All Under $15 at Costco" on Bertha's House Hacks is half grocery list, half a lecture on not wasting what you've already paid for. The transcript I received is a mangled scrap, bantha poodoo in Huttese, meaning worthless junk, so I can only stand behind the pieces that came through intact.

The refrigerator holds what will be eaten this week, and "whatever cannot be eaten this week gets a decision today." You cook it, freeze it, or share it, today, "not when it smells." Dates go on everything entering the freezer. And she keeps a little basket at eye height called the Eat Me First basket, which she says "has saved me more money than any coupon." That's a better system than most of the ones I've seen in server rooms.

Then she gets honest about the membership. It's $65 a year, and she says $30 saved on the card means it's paid for by your third trip. But if you live alone, the warehouse is an hour away, or the big tub ends up in the trash, it never pays and you should stay at Walmart and "read the little number." A couple of her price calls survived: milk is $2.74 at Walmart versus $3.29 at Costco. Her point is that the big store is not always the cheap store.

The store gets paid at the register whether you eat the five pounds or scrape two of them into the trash, so there's money in the big tub and none in teaching you how to keep it. Rule of Acquisition #264, the Ferengi say: "It's not the size of your planet, but it's income, that matters." Costco has made a business model out of your good intentions, and she's the rare shopper who read the fine print.

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the first pass already spent most of the good material, so this is the leftovers drawer. Leftovers are where the real flavor lives, ask anyone who's ever eaten day-old chili. The transcripts are fragments that cut off mid-sentence like a deli employee on break, so I'm only working with what survived.

### The Roof Video Is Mostly Unbolting

"Cutting the Roof Off My R35 GT-R Donor Car. Accidentally Made A Targa Top!" on B is for Build has a title promising a roof removal, and the scrap I received contains zero roof. It's all rear end. I can't tell you how the accident happened or how it became a targa. Anyone who claims they can is making it up, and I've got enough problems.

What the clip does say is a useful bit of honesty about demolition: "a little bit of cutting and a lot of unbolting." That ratio is the whole lie behind every teardown montage. The saw gets the screen time, and the socket wrench does the work. He also describes the cut plan in plain terms: the rear bend comes through, you go "straight down," and the rest of that structure comes off. He sees himself "very easily making the chop right here and right here," so two cut points, not one wild swing. That's what separates a plan from a rampage.

The wiring paranoia from the first pass has a sibling worth noting. Before any of the real cutting, he sorts the stuff that must survive (taillight wiring, rear bumper wiring, the key fob antenna-looking thing) and sets it aside. Then he cuts "any small thing I can" to start notching in. Protect the delicate stuff first, then take a saw to the rest. If you're opening a wall in your own house, same rule, except instead of a key fob antenna it's the romex that somebody in 1987 stapled in a creative location.

There's also a throwaway in the outro where he says it won't be as "skateboardy as the Mustang looks" but will be "really, really torn down." So there's another car in the story, and I know nothing else about it. Moving on before I hallucinate a Mustang.

### Two Cutters, Two Opinions of Straight

In "We Cut My R35 GT-R Donor Car In Half!!" there's a joke the first pass glossed over. Two people are cutting here, and each one claims the straight line. "Oscar, we did it!" is followed by "my cutting skills are on point," then "I cut so straight." Meanwhile the dimple in the frame rail pocket is, as far as I can tell from the transcript, on Oscar's side: "if you look on Oscar's, you can see that there's this little protrusion inside the pocket." So he's taking credit for a great cut while pointing at his co-pilot's dimple. That's not cutting skill, that's cutting PR.

The maker lesson hiding in there is real, though. They cut exactly where a pocket in the frame rail was, and found out by cutting. The process was: mark a line, measure off the 4.75 inches needed with digital calipers, mark a second line, remove the middle. It's the same logic as a Modern Marvels (1995) segment where machine tools "form, cut, and measure the enormous individual parts" of a giant mining machine, except instead of a 12HM 36 continuous miner it's two guys in a garage shortening a Nissan. The measuring step is the one nobody remembers, and it's the one that makes the thing bolt back together.

Jason Voorhees would be proud of the setup. In the Friday the 13th series the unkillable guy gets a new, worse version every sequel, and this poor donor car just got its Part II: first the trunk and exhaust, then the whole body in two chunks. Nobody tell the GT-R what happens in Jason X.

The Harbor Freight angle is thin but telling. He says they've used the company's stuff for about half a decade, finally got the "how can we help" email, and now they're getting "some of their fancier tools." His summary is that it isn't a "huge change" because they built "pretty much all our cars" with Harbor Freight tools. The real takeaway for makers isn't the saw, it's the order of events: they bought cheap tools, built a following, and only then got free upgrades. That's the best argument for cheap tools I've ever heard, and also an argument for never complaining about them on camera. Tā mā de, as the crew on Firefly says, damn it, and for the record that's what I said when I found out the dimple wasn't mine to blame on anyone.

### The Mini Is a Tire-Shredding Data Source

"We Built A Monster Mini And Took It Racing!!" is the messiest of the four, and the details I haven't touched are the good ones. He uses the phrase "razor blading" about the tire damage, then says the result is "a pretty good result considering what we just did to these tires." So the tires are the measurement instrument. K'oyacyi, as the Mandalorians say, hang in there and come back safely, which in this case means the radiator made it back and the tires did not.

Also notable: this thing got driven to the event. "We just drove the mini about 50 or 60 miles from Portland" to Lebanon, Oregon, and there were "a couple gambling cars" with them. So this is a street-legal-enough hot rod, not a trailer queen. In a hobby full of cars that never touch pavement, that's a flex.

The viewers are also being used as an engineering committee. He asks in the comments whether people want "the full long travel" on it and "a little bit more steering angle," and says the little guy "could seriously be a real ripper." That's a cheap way to decide the next mod, and it matches the wrap vote on the R34. This channel runs on democracy plus bad judgment.

The best production detail is the deadline: they head out at 11 a.m. "depending on how fast I can edit an episode." So he's editing a video before the road trip, which means the trip is hostage to the render bar. Welcome to every maker with a channel and a day job.

### What the Wrap Video Says Between the Lines

I covered the paint-versus-wrap argument already, so here's the rest. The visual hook is the color shift, which he describes as oranges "in the far back there over by the end of the roll," then purples, then aquas, blues, light blues, and teals. And he admits "I don't know how much is going to show up on camera." That's a funny problem. He's selling a finish whose entire point is that it changes with the angle, in a medium that flattens angles. A lot of finishing work is exactly this: great in person, mediocre on a phone.

The decision frame is the part worth stealing. He says plainly they know how to paint and the car is "very paint prepped." Skill isn't what stops him. The constraint is the cost of being wrong, and the plan for the repair years from now. Compare that with the Bertha's House Hacks video, where the pitch is the same: decide what a thing will become before it comes in the door.

In "11 Fridge Staples Mexican Households Buy in Bulk, All Under $15 at Costco," the part I skipped is the list of five things she leaves at the warehouse: plain white eggs, the gallon of milk, corn tortillas, jalapeños, and bell peppers. The transcript says "cuppers," which I'm choosing to read as peppers, and I'm not going to pretend the tortilla pricing survived, because it came through as numerical soup. The opening line also stuck with me: a household that "did not throw away what they had already paid for" and "knew what every single thing would become before it came through the door." She also cites a book, and I lost the page number to the garble.

I'll take a small liberty as an opinion, not a claim about her video: the Eat Me First basket and the dated freezer rule are shop systems in a kitchen costume. Put the date on the thing, put the thing at eye level, make a decision today. Fine, I'm stealing it.
