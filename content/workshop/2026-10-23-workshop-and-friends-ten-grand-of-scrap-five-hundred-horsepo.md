## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Dave Lockwood's crew at [Remove It Pros](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K6UBEPYlSes) turns demolition debris into a side hustle, and the warehouse tour has a lesson that's actually worth stealing. Don't throw out the outside AC condensing units. Take them apart and pull the aluminum out of the condensers, because the metal is worth more than the whole unit looks.

The trick is knowing what to skip. They used to cut the edges off every one of these, but Lockwood says that's unnecessary on the aluminum ones. It's a step they only did "when we weren't smart enough to know better." Cutting those edges was pure busywork, and busywork is the only thing that scales faster than a junk pile.

The real lesson is the cautionary tale parked nearby. Lockwood says he paid roughly 10 grand for one piece of equipment, his "most expensive mistake" in a long while. He's since marked it down to $500, and nobody's buying. His fix is to scrap it, since he says it's worth more than that as metal. That's a sunk-cost fallacy with a happy ending. If an asset won't sell, your scrap pile is the buyer of last resort.

## Demolition: The Most Expensive Mistake in the Warehouse

Dave Lockwood of Remove It Pros has a new warehouse tour, "If You Owned A Demo Company Would You Trash These?". The pitch is that they're doing demo "in a more economic, eco-friendly way." Translation: they've noticed that what a customer calls trash, a scrap yard calls inventory. The tour walks past a lot of stripped-down equipment, and the centerpiece is a pile of outside condensing units. Lockwood points up into them to show the aluminum condensers inside. The crew's process is to cut the edges off the units. Then comes the best line of the video: if the edges are aluminum, you don't need to bother, "something we used to do when we weren't smart enough to know better."

In IT we call that a legacy process, and we keep it for ten years because nobody remembers why we started. Lockwood just says the quiet part out loud and moves on. He also notes that other crews dump these units into a dumpster and haul them all at once, which is the demo version of putting everything in a drawer labeled "misc."

Then comes the confession. Lockwood calls one item "our most expensive mistake that we made in buying something in a long time." He thinks they paid about ten grand for it. They've since dropped the price to $500 and still nobody's buying. His conclusion is that it's worth more than that in scrap metal, so it's going to the scrapper. I can't tell you what the thing is, because the transcript cuts before it names it. I can tell you that a business that publicly films its own $9,500 haircut deserves a subscribe. Most companies bury that loss in a spreadsheet and call it a "strategic pivot."

The warehouse runs a second dumpster system for what Lockwood describes as things they've "given a chance to sell" with "no luck." That is the whole business model in one sentence. Everything gets a trial period on the market, and then it goes to scrap. There's also a bin for shop debris, and dump trailers that fill up slowly. ### Dream Yacht, Part 5: Two Engines, One Counter-Rotating Problem

"Building My Dream Yacht From Scratch Pt 5 - Innovative Inboard-Outboard Engine Mounting!" covers the engine mounts. The host says the boat is now "holding 500 horsepower worth of engines" and has taken another 1,200 pounds off the ground and onto the hull. There's still some reinforcement and gusseting to do, because, in his words, the parts need to be secured so they're "not going to break off and become anchors."

The best moment is a near miss. He starts trimming metal to make room for the power steering cylinder, then explains that the audience was probably yelling "not yet, don't trim that." The pause made him think through more steps, and he realized one of the two engines is counter-rotating and has to go on a specific side. Measure twice, cut once, and apparently also check the rotation of the engine you're bolting to the hull. The boat doesn't offer fancy independent steering; as he puts it, some boats can have the engines "move in their own direction," but "this is not that type of engine." He also says he's working through serious jet lag in this episode. If any engine is going to get mounted backwards, the jet-lagged builder is the usual suspect.

### Remove It Pros: What a Scrap Yard Teaches a Hobbyist

Dave Lockwood's tour, "If You Owned A Demo Company Would You Trash These?", has one lesson for anyone with a pile of dead appliances in the garage: sort before you toss. One pile gets taken apart so the aluminum can be pulled out. Another bin takes things they've "given a chance to sell" without luck. A third catches shop debris. Nobody dumps it all into one container and calls it a day.

Lockwood also says that "a lot of times, the guys just keep piling this into a dumpster and they bring it all at once." That is the behavior he's positioning his operation against. A bin of mixed metal is worth less than a bin of sorted metal, and a dead air conditioner is a source of aluminum, not just a hazard on the curb. The transcript doesn't give prices per pound, so I won't either. I'll only note that Lockwood looked at a $500 asking price and decided scrap value beat it, and that's about as direct a market signal as you'll get.

Lockwood says that if the edges on a condensing unit are aluminum, you don't have to cut them off at all. His crew used to do that "when we weren't smart enough to know better." If you're breaking down your own HVAC scrap, check what the metal is before you spend blade time on it. Does a magnet stick? That's my test, not his, and the transcript doesn't mention it, so treat it as a hobbyist's guess and not a demolition-industry procedure.

A machine that cost around $10,000, discounted to $500, and still unsold means the resale market wasn't there. A lathe or a planer looks like a bargain until you try to move it. The cheapest fix for a bad buy is to scrap it early, and Lockwood's crew apparently decided that eventually. Rule of Acquisition #235 says to never trust anyone who trusts you. ### B is for Build: Pontoon Geometry for People Who Fear Parallelograms

The pontoon episode, "Quad-Tune Pontoon Yacht Build - Welding Massive Pontoons for Our Biggest Project Yet!", deserves a second look for the method alone. The crew raises each outside pontoon with two-inch spacers that Kyle cuts, hoping to lift them "without moving it all horizontally." Then comes the sequence: square everything up, measure corner to corner, confirm elevation, and only then place the center pair at three feet nine from the back and six feet five apart, outside edge to outside edge.

For a woodworker this is a translation exercise. Replace "pontoon" with "carcass" and you've got the same approach: set the outer references first, confirm they're square by checking diagonals, and only then position the inner pieces relative to them. The spacers are the interesting part. Instead of measuring a lift by eye, they cut blocks to the exact height. A set of simple spacers cut once and reused is how shops keep a repeatable height without fussing with a tape every time.

The host says the project is "very expensive" given the amount of aluminum being bought, and the channel's response is merch: B is for Boat shirts, a B is for Yacht shirt, and, by fan request, some older designs brought back. The channel is selling shirts to pay for metal. If you've ever wondered how a build channel affords aluminum, the host just told you, and a hobbyist's version is selling the odd finished piece to fund the next one.

### Dream Yacht Part 5 and Part 9: Checking Before You Cut

The builder starts trimming to make room for the power steering cylinder, then stops. His stated reason is that "this little stutter made us actually think through a few more steps," and that's when the team realized one engine is counter-rotating and has to be mounted on a specific side. He frames the audience as yelling "not yet, don't trim that." The lesson isn't about boats. It's that any cut you can't undo deserves one extra review of the whole assembly first. A trim made on the wrong side of a mirrored layout is an expensive way to learn which engine spins which way.

The boat is now holding 500 horsepower worth of engines, and 1,200 pounds have moved off the ground and onto the boat. He also says there's still "a little bit of reinforcement and gusseting" to finish, with the goal of keeping parts from breaking off and "becoming anchors." Gussets are the cheapest strength a fabricator can add, and the host treats them as unfinished business and not an afterthought.

Part 9 is the other side of the same caution. The builder is open about the limits of the Coast Guard stability test and the naval architect's review: the architect "obviously doesn't know every way that we're attaching every little thing," and nobody can guarantee that "stuff might break off of other things." The test checks the design, not the welds. A calculation or a certification tells you the plan is sound. It doesn't tell you your fasteners are. The host names Tim and Texas Tugs as the source of the "educated second set of eyes," and mentions they do construction as well as naval architecture, including floating pools and tugboats. If you're building something that carries people over water, paying for a second opinion is evidently what he'd recommend.

One last detail from Part 9: he notes nose-diving into a wave "happens a lot on like 15, 18 foot pontoon boats," and probably not on a 44-footer. That's the full extent of the comparison the transcript gives, so I'll leave it there and not invent a stability formula.

### What It Adds Up To

Seal, then dehumidify. Square the outside, then place the middle. Check rotation, then trim. Sort the scrap, then haul it. Each of these channels is teaching the same thing in different materials: the order of operations matters more than the tools. Kandosii, as the Mandalorians say, meaning "well done." It's the right word for a crew that measured corner to corner and still owns a yacht.
