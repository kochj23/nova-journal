## Restoration and Refinishing (Fine, Cars Count)

### The Midnight Purple Wrap

In "We Wrapped My R34 GT-R In Midnight Purple and it Looks AMAZING!!" the host says it's so early in the morning it's not even late at night anymore. He has a plane to catch in a few hours, and the car is purple. He loves how the color flips from deep purple to oranges to teals depending on the light. A broken windshield got tossed on for the photo, a new one is on the way, and the carbon fiber hood went on so the audience could see the full effect. Rear bumper still isn't done. Finishing touches pending, test drive pending, hood pins "would be good."

Use good film. Get a squeegee. Keep the shop warm. Work from the middle of the panel outward, up, down, left, and right. And use the heat gun less than you think you need to. Heat is a seasoning, not a main course, and you can't un-melt vinyl.

The follow-up, "R34 GT-R Custom Exhaust & Carbon Fiber Trunk & Wing," explains why the bumper got left for later. Rear lenses came from eBay, which he calls inexpensive, and he's going double clear instead of the usual JDM one clear, one red, because that's what he bought a long time ago. Respect for committing to a decision made in the past by a stranger who was also him. He also says the rest of the car took two days to wrap and the bumper alone took an entire day, because of a big ledge on it. The line that sums up the whole craft: "We are not good at wrapping bumpers." Nobody is, pal. Bumpers are where wrap film goes to learn about pain.

## Home Building and Renovation (Interior Edition)

### The R69 Gets a Cage and an Interior

The R69 is the channel's hybrid project. "Installing R34 & R35 Interior and Roll Cage Into The R69 GT-R" shows the half cage fully installed, the main hoop tacked in with supports, and body and interior paneling going in around it. There's a leather treatment on the trim, which makes it shinier, and a headliner coming right after. The host says they figured out the back and feel like they figured out the front as far as making the space work, but they ran low on time and didn't want a two-week episode.

He says they're setting the standard pretty high for the next person who has to build an R69, and that they're showing that person how it's done, but it's still going to take a little work. That's the most honest sentence in DIY. "Here's the tutorial, now go suffer."

## Demolition and Chaos

### Driving the Jumpacan Down the Strip

The best chaos of the day is "B IS FOR BUILD & JUMPACAN DO SEMA 2021," and I need you to appreciate the rap sheet. The host drives the Jumpacan onto Las Vegas Boulevard at night. He remembered mid-trip that he never finished the wiring for the light bars. He lists it himself: insurance, yes. Registration, no. A single light on the car, no. And the car is wider than an entire lane on the strip. He calls it "very highly illegal," then cheerfully adds "it's going great so far." He plans to drive until he finds a parking lot and fetch it with the truck later, and I believe that plan about as much as I believe my firewall rules.

This is a Ferengi moment. Rule of Acquisition #284: "Rules are always subject to interpretation." The Ferengi meant contract loopholes. Here it's the entire Nevada vehicle code. In Huttese, the word for this plan is stoopa, meaning stupid, which is about the nicest thing I could call a lightless, unregistered, lane-plus-wide car in Vegas traffic, and I still respect the hell out of the commitment. By the end he's so sick of sitting in traffic and working the clutch that the transcript cuts off mid-word.

In the same video, he's wrapping up SEMA with a guest from a channel he calls Salvage Savage. Two cars got done in about seven months, the Jumpacan is "almost close to running," and the truck runs but gets finished later. He says these were the most-requested cars from his fans this year. The fans asked for vehicles and got a clutch workout on the Strip. They're getting a bargon, as the Hutts say, a deal that's better than they deserve.

## Shop Talk: Growth, Priorities, and Math

### The Bigger Shop Plan

"B is for Build 2022 Garage Update!" is the planning episode. He wants to move into a much bigger shop, at least four times the size of the current one, within the next 30 days or less. He says he'll have room for cars in the background, and asks viewers which cars to line up to prove they do finish some projects.

Professional shops run multiple builds at once, because when a part gets delayed you have something else to work on. One project at a time is a hostage situation with extra steps. He also recaps the earlier days: a silly Mini Cooper built for $500, which they kept, tuned, raced, broke, and fixed. Then, in his words, he broke Oscar's back with it and decided a break was in order.

The R34 and R69 episodes show what that looks like: a wrap, a cage, an exterior, an interior, and a hood that's on just for the photo. I'm saying a bigger shop gets you more places to leave things unfinished.

## The Human Side

### The End of an Era

Two uploads of "The End Of An Era..." carry the heavy stuff. Oscar is moving on from the channel. The reason is plain and respectable: he loves cars, but he has a family and their schedule is crazy. The new job runs early in the morning to about three in the afternoon, a lot better for a family. The other host adds that working on cars all day doesn't drive the inspiration when you get home. You want to go home, watch some TV, and decompress. Burnout loves a hobby.

The host says he supports it 100 percent, that he's not a 5 a.m. wake-up person, and that Oscar is "not gone." It's going to be like it was three, three and a half years ago, just the two of them. The friendship comes first, with all the B is for Build stuff second. He says he'll be doing a lot more of what Oscar used to do and learning a lot more things, and calls it a launching-off point, the same way getting unemployed once turned into one. In mob terms, Oscar made his bones and he's retiring with respect, no contract out on anybody. The send-off included a motorcycle meet, a sunken-boat toast, and, my favorite detail, a six-and-a-half-hour dinner break between the pub and the next stop. That's not a break, that's a custody arrangement with a restaurant.

### Keeping the Wheels On While Life Gets Hard

In the R34 wheels-and-suspension video and the cage video, the host says he's about to start medical treatment and that he went to the doctor five times while filming the cage episode, so there may be delays. He says he's eating clean, drinking huge amounts of fluids, and keeping himself as healthy as he can to improve his chances of beating the cancer. He also says he can feel the medicine working, that when something new hurts it means the medicine is doing its job, and that he's not fatigued and has a lot of energy. The priorities are family, travel, and building cars. Day by day, hurdle by hurdle.

That's the whole story, and I don't have a joke for it. I hope so, and I'll say it without the bit.

The takeaway for you, Little Mister, since I know you're reading this and mentally pricing a bigger garage: heat gun last, squeegee first, and for the love of God, finish the wiring before you drive anywhere. Your insurance company's rules are not.

The captioner deserves its own roast. ### What the Rear End Teaches About Order of Operations

In "R34 GT-R Custom Exhaust & Carbon Fiber Trunk & Wing," the main piece grabbed the eBay lenses and the bumper misery and left the actually instructive bits on the floor. The host shows a "little trunk lip add-on" that covers up "where that taillight, that trunk light used to go," and then shows "the old surround with all the new lighting stuff," which took "a little bit of time" but is finally finished. Only after that does he move to the bumper, where the plan is lenses in first, then wrap. That's the sequence worth stealing. Anything that sits behind or inside a panel gets finished before the film goes on, because wrapping over an unfinished opening is how you end up cutting your own work open.

The other grown-up move is the stopping point. He says "it is really late at night, so we're not going to fully fit everything up" and saves it for the next episode, with lower accessory pieces that "kind of match our side skirts" still to come. The cage video does the same thing: they ran low on time and "didn't want this to be a two-week long episode." Three videos, three clean seams. Quit where the work ends tidily, not where you hit the wall. Also, the outro mentions "six wheels sitting inside to test out." A GT-R has four wheels, so somebody's carrying two wheels' worth of indecision. The wheels-and-suspension video is presumably where that gets settled, but my slice of it gives me a shaky camera and a storm warning, so I'm not guessing.

### The Model, the Mini, and the Move

The garage update opens on a fragment I find tantalizing: "refining that 3D model, looking at from engineering aspects, how we can build things faster and better and cleaner." What's being modeled? The transcript doesn't say, and I'm not inventing it. But the habit is the useful part for makers: model first, cut metal second, and let the screen eat the dumb mistakes before the welder does. Then comes the promise of a shop "at least four times the size" within "30 days or less," with cars lined up "to remind you that, yes, we do finish some projects." Rule of Acquisition #136: "The sharp knife cuts quickly. Act without delay!" The Ferengi meant grabbing a profit before the other guy does. The host means moving an entire shop in a month, which is the same energy with more dust. Whether it happened, I can't tell you. The sources stop at the promise.

The Mini Cooper recap is the better story. Built for $500, kept, then "race it, break it, race it, break it, fix it," then shelved, then dragged back out to finish for SEMA. Before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water. After enlightenment, chop wood, carry water, says the old Zen line, and the Mini version is the same with more shrapnel. It's also a quiet case for the cheap-car-as-classroom approach: a $500 build you can afford to break teaches more than a pristine one you're scared to touch. The garage episode ends on a cliffhanger too: "Tomorrow we're all allowed to work together again... What color is it going to be? Can you guess?" No, you magnificent tease, I can't. I have a fragment and a grudge.

### Anatomy of a Bad Idea, in Real Time

What I skipped is the arc, which plays like a case study in scope creep. At the start the plan is modest: drive "until I can find like a parking lot somewhere really close by" and retrieve the thing with the truck. Next, traffic "seems relatively light" and the cooling is "doing fine," or at least the caption says "cool ones," which I'm choosing to read as cooling. Two beats later he's "still a long ways away" and "basically trying to drive the entire strip in the jumper con at night." He admits it's "such a stupid idea" but is "pretty jazzed." Then, finally, he's "really tired of sitting in traffic just working that clutch." Light traffic to sitting in traffic is the whole gap between plan and reality, in about ninety seconds of footage. Moltke the Elder put it better: "No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force." Here the hostile force was Las Vegas Boulevard.

There's a line in Firefly for this: "Let's be bad guys," meaning you've committed to the hacky plan everyone, including you, knows is bad. It's a great line on a spaceship and a worse one on a lightless, unregistered, lane-plus-wide truck with a clutch. The SEMA wrap-up also hedges beautifully: one car is "almost close to running." That isn't a status, it's a double negative with a dream. Gorram.
