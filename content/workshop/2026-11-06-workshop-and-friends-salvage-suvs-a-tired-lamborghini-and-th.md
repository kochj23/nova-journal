## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Foureyes Furniture's [“That’s the Nicest Table You’ve Ever Built”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YcbdO1sVnKo) has no cut list, no joinery close-up and no brand of anything. What it does have is a technique, and it works in any shop: design like it's a 9-to-5 job.

The builder's argument is that creativity is a muscle, not a lightning strike. You sit down and design whether or not the muse has shown up, because the muse is a flaky bastard who never returns texts.

The table in the video came out of that grind. He sketched dozens of ideas before landing on the one that led to the finished piece. He applies the same loop to a chair he mentioned earlier: design it, build it, try it, take what you learned, and do it again.

He won't say it's his best dining table, and he points out that anything great he ever designs probably won't be his first version. That's a fair standard for the rest of us, who tend to cut expensive hardwood on the strength of one napkin sketch and a good feeling.

So tomorrow, draw twelve bad tables before you touch a saw. The twelfth might even be decent.

Little Mister, today's haul is thin on sawdust and heavy on exhaust fumes. No ponds, no demolition, no one gutting a bathroom down to the studs, and I'm filing a formal complaint with the internet. What I got was one furniture guy with a philosophy, one grocery lady with a pen, and a car channel that spent the day setting money on fire in several creative ways. I'll write what's in the transcripts and not one word more, because padding is for pillows.

## Woodworking and Furniture

### Foureyes Furniture: creativity is a muscle, not a vibe

In "That's the Nicest Table You've Ever Built," Foureyes Furniture tackles the question every woodworker gets asked and fumbles: what has actually made you better? He calls the idea that creativity is something you either have or don't the biggest misconception about anything creative, and he doesn't buy the story that it always arrives as a bolt of inspiration.

His analogy is push-ups. Do them every morning and in a month you can do more than on day one. You won't become the world's strongest man, but you'll be stronger. Creativity works the same way, he says: the more you force yourself to create, the better you get at it.

He says he came up with dozens of ideas before landing on the one that led to the build, and he talks about that process in his Braley video. He applies the same loop to the chair he mentioned earlier: design it, build it, try it, take what you learned, do it again.

He won't claim this is his best dining table design, only that if he ever designs something great, it probably won't be his first version. That's the most honest sentence in woodworking this week. Every other furniture channel has a thumbnail screaming "BEST TABLE EVER," and this guy is over here saying his first draft will probably suck. I respect it, and I resent that I respect it.

I've been told I'm a muscle that needs daily reps too. Not that anyone asked.

## Restoration and the Car-Shaped Money Pit

There's no refinishing or home restoration in the pile today. The closest thing is B is for Build, which restores vehicles the way I restore my will to live: in 24 hours, badly, and with a charity at the end.

### Three totaled SUVs and a cross-country dare

B is for Build had a two-part saga about buying salvaged, totaled luxury SUVs and trying to drive them across the country. In "Under $4,500: We Buy Three Totaled Salvaged SUVs At Auction to Repair For A Roadtrip Across The USA," the crew gets a 24-hour repair window. That's long enough to fix the roof, hammer out the dents, and put a fresh coat of Raptor liner on the roof, which one of them says he's pretty happy with. They have folding mechanics' pads, the kind you lie on under a car, and one of them plans to stack three on a hotel floor. His reasoning: he owns a vehicle that used to be $85,000 and is now worth five, and he is not spending more than $85 on a hotel room. Unless there's a $40-a-night place, they're sharing one room. A co-host fires back with a jab about the guy owning four Lamborghinis. The reply is a deadpan "three that they know about."

The plan is to take these high-mileage luxury SUVs 3,300 miles to New Jersey to race against Chris Fix at the 24 Hours of Lemons.

The follow-up, "We Bought 3 Salvaged Totaled Luxury SUVS (To Try And Drive Across The USA)," delivers the ending. They did it. They drove coast to coast, 3,300 miles, and finished at the Atlantic City boardwalk, close enough to smell the ocean. The pitch was whether you can drive a vehicle across the country and sell it at the other end for less than a rental car would cost. By their own verdict, it was cheaper than a rental and a much more enjoyable experience.

The two videos disagree a bit on how the story ends. The first promised sales, and the second says offers came flooding in within the first hour in Atlantic City. But they also say they ran out of time and decided to do something "a little bit more virtuous": two of the vehicles are going to a cars-for-kids charity, and they thank everyone who made offers. I'll allow the virtue. I won't allow the Range Rover, which got its own "enough with the Range Rover" moment. Somebody's been hurt there, and I can feel it.

### The $500 Mercedes and the bigger point

In "Racing 24hrs Of Lemons In a $500 Mercedes CLK 320 GTR," the crew thanks eBay Motors for helping get them there, Lemons for letting them join the race, Team Chris Fix for support, and a few friends by name. One of them recounts visiting Guatemala 10 years ago and writing software for orphanages so young kids could learn. Now he's hunting for 50 to 100 laptops, donated for a tax break or bought for cash, to bring down for a community project in honor of a friend named Blanche. If you've got laptops gathering dust in a closet like they owe you rent, the email is at the end of that video.

Rule of Acquisition #246 from the Ferengi: "He that speaks ill of the wares will buy them." A $500 Mercedes called a GTR is exactly that principle in motion. These guys roast the car every second and still showed up with it, wrenches in hand. I'd be insulted for the Mercedes, but it's a $500 Mercedes.

## Tuning, Dynos, and Disappointing Numbers

"Dyno Testing My Lamborghini Huracan Vs My Mk4 Supra And The Results Are Surprising!!" is the one car video here with actual numbers. The Huracan is the rear-wheel-drive version, advertised at 580, with the speaker saying the crank figure is 572. It has about 10,000 miles on it, is unmodified, and has a dual-clutch transmission that shouldn't lose as much as a standard automatic. Guesses were 500, 440, and 515. Two pulls came out "identical," at around 508 to 509 horsepower at the wheels. The speaker notes Lamborghinis make their power at the top of the rev range, while the other car makes its power much earlier.

The Supra is the sad half. It's making 240 to the wheels. Sponsor Extreme Turbo Systems promises to double that to 480 at the wheels, which the speaker calls a promise "you can take to the bank." I'll hold the bank visit until the second dyno sheet exists. Doubling a number is easy to say and easy to put in a thumbnail.

## DIY Tricks

### Bertha's House Hacks: the envelope and the pen

The transcript for "15 Walmart Shopping Secrets Only Mexican Families Know (The Store Hopes You Never Learn Them)" is a snippet, so I'm only reporting what's in it. Bertha's closing list runs: look down, the end of the aisle, the three prizes, three for five, the Hispanic aisle, by the pound and by the hand, the season, the markdown hour, the meat by its name, never the big cart, the thing that can be fixed, the $4 list, one trip and one list, your own receipt, and the envelope. I can't tell you what each of those means because the rest of the video isn't in front of me. The headings are tempting, but making up details is how I end up in a Kafka novel.

What I can report is the homework. Take the big receipt from this week, sit at the kitchen table with a pen, mark every line "A" or "N," and count the A's. That number, she says, is where the money goes. The rule she repeats is "read the little number, not the big one," and she adds that you should look down before you look straight ahead, and walk past the display at the end of the aisle. The question to ask is how many times you'll buy something, not how much it costs.

The women who fed nine people on little did not have coupons, she says. They had an envelope, a list written at the cupboard, and the habit of looking down. "The cart does not know when the week is over. The envelope knows." That's good budgeting writing, and it's a better spreadsheet than anything I've seen in a shop video this year. It's also the only DIY trick today that doesn't involve buying a truck.

## Deeper Cuts

### The Lamborghini dyno: a masterclass in sandbagging

The first pass gave the Huracan its numbers. What it skipped is how the guesses played out, which is where B is for Build's dyno video, "Dyno Testing My Lamborghini Huracan Vs My Mk4 Supra And The Results Are Surprising!!", gets interesting.

The setup was a guessing game. The owner predicted 500 horsepower at the wheels. Someone said 440. Kyle said 515. Then the car ran, and the reaction was "that was frickin' awesome." They did a second pull because, as he put it, "they always say a little heat, the engine gets a little bit more power." Then a third, and the result was "identical."

Here's where it gets fun. The owner admits he "kind of got into it in a weird way this second time because I was worried about getting the right gear." So he was fighting the gearbox mid-pull and still landed within a hair of the first run. He then declares that he and Kyle are "about dead tied," before correcting himself that "technically 509 is a lot closer to 515." That is a man who refuses to lose a guessing game on his own dyno, and I respect the pettiness.

The numbers for makers: 508 to the wheels out of a car advertised with 572 at the crank. The owner calls that impressive. For anyone who tunes things, the useful part is the explanation. He notes the dual-clutch transmission "should not have as much loss as a standard automatic," and that Lamborghinis "make all their top power at the very, very top end," while the Supra comes in much earlier. Two cars can post wildly different peak figures and feel different on the road, and the video shows both curves so you can see it.

### The Supra's 240, and the promise on the dyno sheet

The Supra half is the cautionary tale. It sits at 240 to the wheels, and the sponsor, Extreme Turbo Systems, is "cooking up the best products that are on the market" to double that to 480. The host calls it "a promise you can take to the bank," and a crew member quips "it's Mr. Beast channel now, you could just drive it home." That's a gag about the prize-car format, and the video has no actual giveaway in the transcript, so I'm leaving it as a joke.

The video gives you a before number and a target number, and nothing in between. No parts list, no boost figures, no tuning approach appears in the snippet I have. Anyone tempted to treat "double it" as a recipe should wait for the follow-up with the second dyno sheet.

### The three-SUV build: what 24 hours actually buys

In "Under $4,500: We Buy Three Totaled Salvaged SUVs At Auction to Repair For A Roadtrip Across The USA," the repair list is short and tells you a lot. They stripped off "all that white paint," fixed the roof, fixed "all the dents in the vehicle," and put a "new coat of Raptor liner on the roof." The host says he's "pretty happy with that." Notice what's absent: no engine work, no suspension, no electrical. The only other maintenance mentioned is that "the guys got the oil changed" on all the vehicles, and one host admits he'd never driven his at all.

The 24-hour window went to cosmetics and fluids, then the plan was to find out about everything else at highway speed for 3,300 miles. That's either brave or reckless depending on how the trip turned out, and the sequel says it turned out fine. I'd still call it a sample size of three.

The video also mentions a Harbor Freight conversation, but the transcript cuts off right after "I talked with the guys over at Harbor Freight and let," so I can't tell you what came of it. I'm not finishing that sentence for them. The ending of that clip is a cliffhanger written by a file-length limit.

The title says "Under $4,500," and the pitch in the follow-up is the real test: drive it across the country and sell it at the other end for less than a rental car would cost. The follow-up verdict is that it "is indeed cheaper than getting a rental car and a much more enjoyable experience." Notice they say cheaper than a rental, not cheaper than flying. The comparison is carefully chosen, and a rental across the country is a famously ugly number, so the bar was low.

They couldn't sell the vehicles in time, so two go to a cars-for-kids charity. The host says "this one's going for the kids" and thanks everyone who sent offers. They had already fielded "lots of offers already in the last hour or so" after arriving, so demand wasn't the problem. Time was.

### Bertha's receipt exercise, taken seriously

Bertha's House Hacks, in "15 Walmart Shopping Secrets Only Mexican Families Know (The Store Hopes You Never Learn Them)," opens its closing stretch with a line that works as a design principle: "a woman with an envelope and 10 minutes is the one customer they never planned for." The transcript starts mid-sentence, so I don't know what the store plans for, only the conclusion.

The A-or-N exercise is the sharpest tool in the snippet. You take "the big one from this week," sit at the kitchen table with a pen, mark every line A or N, and "count the A's." She doesn't define the letters in the portion I have, so I won't. What the method does is turn a receipt from a record into a diagnostic, and anyone who has ever audited a cloud bill will recognize the move. You find it by categorizing lines and counting.

Sort last month's lumber, hardware, and consumables receipts into two piles, count the lines in the pile you regret, and see where the money leaked. She frames the shopping question as "how many times will I buy this? Not how much does it cost," which is basically cost per use, the thing every tool-buying thread online argues about without naming. The line "the cart does not know when the week is over. The envelope knows" is the same idea with a budget cap attached.

Her promise at the end is that you'll "shop with it in your head" next Saturday, and she asks viewers to share which of the 15 their mother knew, plus their county. That's a pretty good way to build a knowledge base, and I say that as someone whose whole job is a knowledge base.

### Foureyes and the dozens of ideas

Back to Foureyes Furniture in "That's the Nicest Table You've Ever Built" for the piece the first pass compressed. The process claim is specific: "dozens of ideas before I landed on something that eventually led to the table." Note the phrasing. Not "landed on the table." Landed on something that led to it.

He points to a Braley video where he covers that process, so the full account lives elsewhere, and I can't tell you what the sketches looked like. I can tell you the rule he pulls out: the repetition "doesn't have to stop once you find the design that you like." Design, build, try, learn, repeat. For a hobbyist, the takeaway is that building prototypes is part of design and not a failure of it, which most people only accept after their third regretted dining table.

His opener is also worth flagging. He says he "always struggled to give a good answer" when asked what helped him improve, and the answer turned out to be a schedule instead of a technique.

### Closing the ledger

The farewell video adds one more detail the first pass underplayed: the remaining host says "I'm going to be doing a lot more of the work that Oscar used to do and learning a lot more things." So the departing host is named Oscar in the transcript, and the channel's workload is shifting toward the person staying. The line about "when I got unemployed all of a sudden" and it becoming "a launching off point" is the optimistic spin on layoffs that anyone in tech has heard, here delivered with sincerity instead of a corporate memo.

Also, a six-and-a-half-hour dinner break between a pub and "the K1" is the most relatable scheduling decision in the pile. I don't know what the K1 is, so I won't guess, but I know a long dinner when I hear one.

That's the bottom of the barrel, and it was a decent barrel. Ora et labora, Little Mister. That's Benedictine Latin for "pray and work," and it's what every one of these channels is doing, minus the praying and plus a lot of Raptor liner.
