## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

Bertha's House Hacks has a winter blackout plan that costs less than your average streaming bundle, and it's the only prepper content I've seen that doesn't want to sell you a bunker. In [20 Aldi Items People Living Alone Will Wish They Bought BEFORE a Winter Blackout](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w7X-2odfTWI), she boils the whole twenty-item shopping list down to three ideas. Food that's already finished and needs no flame. Heat you keep instead of heat you make. Light your hand can find in the dark. That's the entire curriculum, and it's more coherent than most corporate strategy decks I've been forced to read.

The trick is the "keep, don't make" part. Pick the smallest room in the house, shut the door, and make that your cold-weather lifeboat. Add a wool blanket (she puts one at $16) and a candle in a glass, and keep the water dispenser full. The old-timers on her street got through outages with exactly that, plus a neighbor who knocked, which no retailer has figured out how to put in a box yet.

A $2,000 generator comes with a marketing budget, while a blanket and a candle come with nothing. So people on fixed incomes hear that readiness costs $1,000 when it's closer to $120, and they give up before the storm even shows up.

Steal it, Little Mister. Your house has 33 smart lights, and every single one of them goes dark the second the power does.

## Home Preparedness: Bertha Explains Why You're Not Doomed

Bertha's House Hacks put out "20 Aldi Items People Living Alone Will Wish They Bought BEFORE a Winter Blackout." The transcript is a rough, chopped-up cut that doesn't list all twenty items, so I can't tell you what the full cart looked like.

Bertha boils the whole list down to three ideas, and she says so explicitly: "Food that is already finished, that asks for no flame. Heat that you keep instead of heat you make. And light that your hand can find in the dark." She calls the listener "mija" (Spanish for "my daughter," the way an abuela says it right before she's correct about everything). She talks about a wool "cobija," which is a blanket, plus a closed door, a full dispenser, and "a candle in a glass." The tone is closer to a grandmother's front-porch sermon than a prepper forum, and I found it weirdly effective. Don't Panic, as the Hitchhiker's Guide says in large friendly letters, is the correct incident-response posture, and Bertha is that sentence with a rebozo on.

Fear sells big boxes, she says. A "$2,000 generator has a marketing budget," while a "$16 wool blanket" does not. Her accusation is that ads teach people, especially women on fixed incomes, that being ready is expensive, so they give up and buy nothing. Then the storm comes and, in her words, "she believed the price was $1,000 when it was $120." I'll give her this: it's an excellent bit of consumer rage. The transcript also renders one $7 item as a "rook," and I have no idea what that is, so I'm not going to guess. (A rook is a chess piece and a bird. Neither one keeps you warm.)

I would add that a closed door is the cheapest piece of home-improvement hardware ever invented, and it has a lifetime warranty. 😏

## Refinishing: The Raptor's Face Transplant

B is for Build spent three episodes on a 2014 supercharged Raptor, converting it from the Gen 1 front end to the Gen 2 front end. That's giving a truck a facelift, except the truck has no say and the surgeon has a spray gun.

In "Facelifting my Supercharged 2014 Raptor - Gen 1 ➡ Gen 2 Front End Conversion," the host admits the plan was too ambitious. They wanted to "fix the supercharger do the body swap paint the truck and fix the axle all in the same episode," but "I'm not good at scheduling," and they "spent a whole week" fitting the body and rebuilding the supercharger. Moltke, the old Prussian general, said it better: "No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force." In shop terms, the hostile force is a fender that doesn't line up. The next episode promises the axle, the wiring, the repaint, and "some other stuff like that," which is the most honest scope description I've ever heard.

The part worth stealing is the paint philosophy. They say they "never want to rush a paint job because... you end up going with things that you want to refix or change later." That's the whole refinishing religion in one sentence, and it's the same lesson every deck-stainer learns the hard way. In the follow-up, "F150 Raptor Front End Conversion Is Complete! Gen1 ➡ Gen2," the panels have been painted and are about to be buffed and polished, and the little "dust nibs" (those tiny specks of crud that land in wet paint) "will go away." They also note that outside, "crap falls out of the sky too," which is both a comfort and an insult to anyone who has ever painted in a garage.

## The Tricks Worth Stealing

Before they conclude anything exotic, one of the crew spots that the truck's headlights were set to auto, and since it was daytime, they weren't getting power. Turn them on and "we actually have power wires sent to the high beams, low beams, all that good stuff." The lesson is that half of electrical troubleshooting is checking whether the thing is on, and I say this as a machine whose entire existence is checking whether things are on. The machine spirit, as the Warhammer 40K tech-priests call the soul inside every device, was apparently not broken, just napping in a sunny parking spot.

For the actual connections, they used "heavy duty marine connectors heat shrink with glue inside them." That's a solid choice for anything that has to survive weather, vibration, and the next owner. The running-light mode is a "dimmed out low beam with our ambers on the sides," which sounds good, though I'm taking their word for it, because I can't see a truck and have a lot of opinions anyway.

The second trick comes from the off-road Lamborghini Huracan episode, "Off-Road Lamborghini Huracan gets Shocks Installed and Weighed!" They needed the car's weight to pick springs, and they don't have corner scales "because we ain't fancy." So they put "two scales under each wheel" (bathroom scales bought online) with "a two by four to help distribute the weight," then let the car down so the suspension compresses until the bump stop hits at the ball joint. They tally what's left to add, 781 pounds of stuff including bumpers, exhaust, a spare wheel, a jack, and "a miscellaneous 200 extra pounds," and land on an estimated vehicle weight of 3,257 pounds, with a 20 gallon fuel estimate already baked in.

The same episode jokes about building "our first low rider," noting that with small springs they could "run just about an inch higher than this." So the off-road Lamborghini briefly flirted with being a lowrider, which is the most Burbank thing I've read all day.

## Deeper Cuts

The main piece skimmed the surface, Little Mister, so I'm going back in for the bits that got short-changed. I'll only say what the transcripts say. Where they're a mess, I'll tell you they're a mess.

### Bertha's Opening Move: The Room the Cold Finds First

The earlier section went straight to her three-idea thesis, but the transcript opens somewhere better. Bertha's House Hacks, in "20 Aldi Items People Living Alone Will Wish They Bought BEFORE a Winter Blackout," starts with "the room the cold always finds first" and tells you to spend the week on one room and one bag by the bed. The generator-less women on her street, she says, had "a wool cobija, a closed door, and a neighbor who knocked." Cobija is Spanish for blanket, and the neighbor is the only item on the list you can't buy at Aldi. She does mention a price near "$60" for a chunk of the list, but the transcript cuts off the context, so I can't tell you which chunk. I'm not inventing a receipt.

She also drops a line about "a full dispenser," which I read as a water dispenser, though the transcript never says so. Her real thesis is that the three ideas (finished food, kept heat, findable light) are all an outage asks of you, and that the thing nobody sells is "each other." That's the tender part of the video, and it works because it's the only item she doesn't price. Somebody check my temperature.

Nothing in it plugs in, boots up, or needs a firmware update. As a machine who monitors a hundred-plus devices, I find that suspiciously restful. It's also the best argument against gadget-first preparedness I've heard: a blanket has never needed a companion app.

### The Lamborghini's Weigh-In, Step by Step

What it skipped is the sequence, which is the reusable part. In B is for Build's "Off-Road Lamborghini Huracan gets Shocks Installed and Weighed!" the springs aren't in yet, so the suspension is allowed to fully compress "until the bump stop hits where it's supposed to hit." The host points out that the bump stop lands "on this ball joint right here," and that's what stops the car dropping, so the scales can finally read the real number.

Without springs, the car sits at a known, repeatable mechanical limit, and you read the weight at that limit. You're borrowing the bump stop as a fixture. Then you add up the stuff still to come (bumpers, exhaust, a spare wheel, a jack, and "a miscellaneous 200 extra pounds"), end up at an estimated 3,257 pounds, and order springs. The "miscellaneous 200 pounds" is my favorite line item in the whole feed. Every build has a miscellaneous 200 pounds, and it's usually the part that ruins you.

The same episode's "first low rider" joke has a practical footnote. With "a really small set of springs" the car could sit "about an inch higher than this," and the host admits the compressed stance is "probably not great on the shocks." So the funny picture is also a warning: the lowest the car can sit is not a ride height, it's a failure mode.

### The Jumpacan Is a Roller, and the Boat Is the Boss

Back to the "Coil-over Springs Are Installed, Ride Height Set, The Jumpacan Is Officially A Roller!!" update, which the first pass mined for real estate and trees. The actual build news is that the thing can now "steer around the shop" on "its own suspension." Rolling on its own suspension is the classic milestone where a pile of parts becomes a vehicle. The host says the plan from here is "some testing" to "make sure it's a stable unit," and then the boat gets its turn "before summer," with the boat engines "almost done."

Stability testing before the next big project is the kind of discipline most home builders skip. Ora et labora, as the Benedictines say, which is Latin for "pray and work." The crew has the second half down.

### The BMW Dyno: What the Transcript Does and Doesn't Tell You

In "Heavily Modifying Kyles E92 BMW And Dyno Testing Each Mod," the dyno routine is the fun detail. The crew did "the vehicle calibration," set up all the gears, and recorded "our base for our pull" before anyone touched a mod. That's the part makers should copy: baseline first, mods second, or you can't prove anything later. One of them also warns viewers to "turn off your air conditioning," because the car is going to be loud, and the first pull gets a "Nice." followed by "That was so loud, dude." Oscar suspects the result was a fluke. The crew shrugs and "gives it a rip."

On the chart, the solid line is Oscar and the dotted line is Kyle's car, and the narration points out that a naturally aspirated engine peaks its horsepower "towards the end of the RPM range." The numbers around the crank-versus-wheel gap are scrambled in the transcript, and the speaker seems to contradict himself between a 400-ish figure and a 456 figure, so I won't reconcile them. What survives is the concept: crank power and wheel power differ, and he says "we're losing a high percentage" between the two. The sign-off, "we scienced some cars," is the most honest dyno summary I've read. The host also says Oscar's car is the slowest, "well mine's not done yet," so the final standings are pending. Real science has a lot of "not done yet" in it.

### Go-Karts: A Course Built From Cones and Grudges

In "I BOUGHT 3 of the FASTEST ELECTRIC GoKarts from CHINA ($5,300 NEW)," the course is a plain parking-lot layout: start at a tree on the far side of a curb, come "around the bend," floor it past the orange cones on the right, turn around a cone, and "floor it right back" to a white cone finish line. The karts have settings you choose, including drift mode on or off, and one line suggests the drift range is "80 to 100." The transcript doesn't say 80 to 100 of what, so I'm leaving that dangling like the crew did.

There's also a remark that Segway slash Ninebot "has done this" before, "with their electric dirt bike," which suggests a pattern of selling hardware that sits behind an app. The host's complaint is the same one as before: you have to connect through the app "to unlock the features." Assembly "could take a while." The host also admits the other two routinely "totally destroy me" in karts but he's been winning in cars. So the true format is two sports, one ego, and a stopwatch.

A start tree, a curb, two cones, and a finish cone is a repeatable timing rig that costs nothing. A stopwatch, a cone, and a penalty rule (two seconds per spin) is the whole sport. Kandosii, which is Mando'a for "well done," to anyone who can run a fair race with a tree.

### The 370Z: What Got Done Before the Track

The prep story is better. The suspension kit is installed, "we adjusted the toe and everything," Oscar took it for a test drive and "it's driving great," and the truck is loaded up for the next day. The wing and lip were painted "quite nicely" and were "dry to the touch" but "still pretty soft," so the crew refused to risk the paint by mounting it with the wrong hardware.

Spirit Peaks Raceway gets a real endorsement. The host says he'd been there "two weeks ago" and "just had a blast," and describes the course as "super beginner friendly" and "not super dangerous for your cars." That's a useful bit of local knowledge if you live around the Pacific Northwest. The host also points viewers to a link in the description, which I can't see, so I won't pretend I can.

### The Junk Yard Plea and the Lawyers Clause

One detail from "I Found a Junk Yard Car Too Good To Pass Up - Our Next Build!" was left on the table. The host's promise list: "We don't bail out on builds. We don't sell builds halfway through. We don't cancel builds unless there's, well, unless the lawyers literally take them away from us." That's a surprisingly specific carve-out. He names two earlier builds, the Supra and the single seater, as the kind of thing viewers might be waiting on. A hobbyist's guarantee that includes an exception for litigation is the most adult sentence in the entire feed.

### The Raptor's Last Piece

Finally, a small gap in the Raptor coverage. At the end of "Facelifting my Supercharged 2014 Raptor - Gen 1 ➡ Gen 2 Front End Conversion," the host says the headlights and plastics are mounted, and the last piece of the puzzle is mounting the grill. Then: "damn it looks awesome." That's the sign of a build crossing from parts to truck, and the crew notes they'll pop the hood next.
