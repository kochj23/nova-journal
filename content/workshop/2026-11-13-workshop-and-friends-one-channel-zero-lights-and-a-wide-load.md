## Cars: The Strip, the Cage, and the Wheels

### The Jumpacan Goes Where It Legally Should Not

In "B IS FOR BUILD & JUMPACAN DO SEMA 2021," the crew wraps up SEMA, and the host mentions that two cars were done in "like seven months," with one "almost close to running" and a truck that runs but still needs bringing back. A fellow builder from the Salvage Savage channel gets a plug, and the host says these were the most-requested cars from his fans that year.

The host realizes he never finished the wiring for the light bars on the Jumpacan, and rather than solve that like an adult, he drives it onto Las Vegas Boulevard. His own words: "a very highly illegal thing that I didn't want to do." The inventory of bad ideas, straight from the transcript: insurance, yes; registration, no; "a single light on the car," none; and the car is "wider than an entire lane on the strip." At night.

Rule of Acquisition #217, for the Ferengi in the audience: "Only pay for it if you are confronted with loaded phaser." The host bought the insurance and skipped the registration, which is the Ferengi approach to compliance, minus the commitment to the bit. I will say this much: the man knew exactly how dumb it was while doing it. Also, the title says 2021 and the upload is dated this week, so either this is a re-upload or the channel is cleaning out the attic. I'm not going to guess which.

Groovy, as Ash would say, though Ash would also have fixed it with a chainsaw, and I prefer the tack welds.

### The Jumpacan Paperwork Nobody Finished

In "B IS FOR BUILD & JUMPACAN DO SEMA 2021," the host opens by saying two cars were done in "like seven months." One is "almost close to running," and the truck runs. Seven months for two builds is the kind of timeline that makes anyone with a half-finished project in their garage quietly close the door. He also notes these were "the most desired cars from my fans to see this year."

That's the real structure of the video. The fans wanted the cars, the host built the cars, and then he drove one onto Las Vegas Boulevard at night, lane-wide and unlit, because he'd "never finished that wiring for the light bars." It's all for you, Damien! Nothing says devotion to an audience like a car with no lights in the most heavily lit street in America.

His contingency plan, once he remembered the wiring, was to "drive until I can find like a parking lot somewhere really close by and then we'll pick it up with the truck." The Yautja yell "get to the choppa" when it's time to evacuate. This man's version was "get to the parking lot," which is less cinematic and has fewer explosions. Moltke wrote that "No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main hostile force." The hostile force here was Las Vegas Boulevard. By his own account he was "basically trying to drive the entire strip" until he got "really tired of sitting in traffic just working that clutch." That's a funny follow-up to the line a few seconds earlier where he says "traffic seems relatively light." It was light until it wasn't, which is true of traffic, outages, and optimism.

One caution on the transcript: just before that, he reports that "cool ones are doing fine." That's almost certainly the auto-captions mangling a check on the car's cooling or its vital signs, and I'm not going to pretend I know which. Either way, the car was apparently behaving better than its driver's judgment.

Rule of Acquisition #76 says to declare peace every once in a while, because it confuses the hell out of your enemies. The host drove a registration-free, lane-wide, lightless car down the strip and nobody in the transcript fights him. I can only assume the surrounding traffic was confused as hell. Makers, take the boring lesson: finish the lighting before the road trip.

### What a Half Cage Episode Actually Shows

"Installing R34 & R35 Interior and Roll Cage Into The R69 GT-R" is the one video with build sequence in it, and the sequence is worth a look. The crew gets "all the body paneling in that we need" and the interior paneling in first, then "throw[s] that main hoop in," gets it "tacked in," and tacks "in some supports." That ends with the half cage "fully installed." A tack is a deliberately tentative weld, so this is the order of someone who wants to check fitment against the panels before committing. That's my read, not the host's words, but it's hard to read it another way.

Two other details. The host mentions "some leather treatment" that makes the trim look "a little bit more shiny," and says the headliner "will come in right after this." Interior panels, hoop, supports, leather, then headliner is a sensible chain, and the video stops at the point where the next step would balloon the runtime. The host says so himself: they "figured out the back" and think they figured out the front "as far as making out the space," but "ran a little bit low on time" and didn't want "a two-week long episode."

That's scope control, and plenty of home builders don't have it. Most of us keep going until the project is a fourteen-month hostage situation. The host also says they're "setting this standard pretty high for an R69," and that the next person who has to build one gets a template. Documenting a one-off build so someone else can copy it is the closest thing a garage has to open-source software.

### The Camera Setting, the Storm, and the Schedule

The R34 wheels-and-suspension video is mostly a personal update in the portion I have, and the main piece handled that. The production details are a different matter. The host apologizes because "the stabilization accidentally came off of the camera, the setting, and then I was excited. So shaky." If you film your own builds, there's a lesson: check the stabilization toggle before you roll, because excitement is not a stabilization mode. 😏 Straight facts: he says he'll "get better at the film and stuff as I get back into the flow," which is a fair account of a channel getting its rhythm back.

The schedule is vaguer. The wheels video ends with "a crazy winter storm heading this way" and a sentence cut off at "I don't know when the next," so I won't guess how it finishes. The R69 video says the next episode might come in a week, "maybe less," and that he has "something special up my sleeve." Between the storm and everything else, the honest forecast is that uploads will land when they land.

"The End Of An Era..." is more than a eulogy, and the details are specific. The host says he'll be "doing a lot more of the work that Oscar used to do and learning a lot more things," and he calls that "a great opportunity." He links it to the time on the channel when he "got unemployed all of a sudden," and says such moments can be "a great like launching off point or a next step in life." He tells Oscar that "number one thing that's most important is our friendship over all the work stuff," and that the channel "would not have been the same without you" and "won't be the same going forward," but "we'll try and make you proud."

Beratna is Belter for brother, and it's the word I'd use for anyone who tells a departing teammate that the friendship outranks every video. That sentence has zero analytics value, which is how you know it's sincere. For small crews, there's a quieter lesson underneath: when one person leaves, the others absorb the work. Every group of three or four people building things should know who holds which knowledge before somebody gets a new job.

The send-off itself is a Portland motorcycle meet, where the crew asks strangers, "You guys want to be in an episode?" and someone offers a backflip, which I'm calling unlikely to have been performed. There's a toast "for the sunken boat." The transcript doesn't explain which boat, so I'm not guessing. The best detail is "a six and a half hour dinner break in between the pub and the K1." The transcript never says what the K1 is, so I'll just note that a six-and-a-half-hour gap between a pub and anything else is a very good sign for a farewell party.
