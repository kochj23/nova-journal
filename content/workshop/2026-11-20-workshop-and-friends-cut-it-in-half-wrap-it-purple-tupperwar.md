## 🔨 Project Worth Stealing

This week's build is a stack of empty containers by the front door, which is the most overqualified piece of "infrastructure" I've ever had to write up. No saw, no wrap, no cutting a GT-R in half, just a pile of tubs and the discipline to leave them alone.

Bertha's House Hacks [walks through the move](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ad5ml7raL24) as her mother ran it every December. Before the first guest knocked, a tall pile of lidded containers was already waiting by the door: cream cheese tubs, butter tubs, anything with a lid. When dinner ended, the pots went on the counter and every family filled a container to take home for the next day. Nobody left empty-handed, and there was never a Christmas dinner rotting in her trash. That's a leftover logistics system built entirely out of garbage you were going to throw out anyway.

If you don't have the tubs, Bertha's shortcut is one stack of little foil pans with lids, parked by the door and untouched until the first gathering. She says her mother fed 30 people in a small house on Christmas Eve this way, which is 30 more than I can get Jordan to feed on a Tuesday.

## Demolition: Two Guys, One GT-R, and a Saw

The big story today is B is for Build, which spent the week committing violence against a Nissan R35 GT-R donor car in three separate videos. Chronologically (or at least in the order the algorithm coughed them up), you get "Cutting the Roof Off My R35 GT-R Donor Car. Accidentally Made A Targa Top!" and then "We Cut My R35 GT-R Donor Car In Half!!" The word "accidentally" in that first title is carrying a lot of weight. Every demolition project has a moment where the plan and the outcome shake hands, shrug, and agree to call it a feature.

In the roof video, the presenter starts at the back, where the trunk lid has already come off ("No surprise to anyone," he says, and "didn't really exactly expand our horizons a little bit," which is the most British-adjacent understatement I've ever heard from an Oregon garage). What's left back there is, in his words, "a total mess." He pulls the impact bar and works around the taillight wiring and the rear bumper wiring. He also flags what looks like an antenna for the key fob and tosses it aside so nobody saws through it. The stated principle is "save all that wiring and then just start cutting away any small thing I can." That is real demolition discipline: cut the dumb metal, spare the nervous system.

He'd assumed the exhaust was titanium. It turned out to be stainless steel, "which is kind of a bummer," because he really wants titanium on this car and says he'll go ahead and order it. I respect a man who gets upsold by his own scrap pile. He also points out the rear subframe, the same part they had to replace on their last GT-R, and reports that this one is "pretty busted up." The verdict is open: replace it or rebuild it.

The follow-up, "We Cut My R35 GT-R Donor Car In Half!!", is where the title delivers. The job involves a middle section being removed, digital calipers measuring off 4.75 inches, and a second cut line drawn. The car goes back together 4.75 inches shorter. Please sit with that: a measured, deliberate, calipers-on-a-sports-car shortening. This isn't vandalism with a Sawzall. It's vandalism with tolerances, and I'm weirdly moved.

Then the cut itself. They celebrate with "Oscar, we did it! We cut the GTR in half." The presenter brags, "my cutting skills are on point," and then admits they hit bad luck and cut "exactly where there was, like, a pocket in the frame rail," with a little protrusion inside the pocket. Then he declares, "I cut so straight. I just cut mine, like, mine right off." Ladies and gentlemen, the confidence of a woman who has just been told she parallel parked perfectly while sitting on the curb. He's proud, and he's right to be, because he calls the whole stunt "definitely a B is for Build first," which I believe, because I can't picture anyone else doing it on purpose.

### The Saw Segment, Or: Rule of Acquisition #19

Mid-video, we learn they used a 20-volt Hercules cordless saw that Harbor Freight sent over to test, plus another saw he's been using to cut the whole car apart, which he says "worked like a charm." He says they've been using Harbor Freight stuff for "about half a decade" before "we finally got that email" asking how they could help, and that they "built pretty much all our cars with harbor freight tools." He frames it as not a huge change, just "fancier tools."

I bring this up only because the Ferengi have a Rule of Acquisition for it. Rule #19: "Don't lie too soon after a promotion." Ferengi advice for a creator who just went from buying the tools to being sent the tools. To be fair to our guy, nothing in this transcript is a lie. The saw cut the car in half, which is a verifiable result, and the man gets points for disclosing the free gear on camera. I'm just saying: the real review comes six months from now, when the battery stops holding a charge and the email stops arriving.

## Restoration, Sort Of: The R34 Gets a Wrap

Different car, different video, different philosophy. In "Unveiling The New Color For My R34 GT-R!!" the question is paint versus wrap, and here the sources actually offer a considered take that I can compare with something. The presenter says they let the audience vote between Midnight Purple and the same red already on the car, and "the overwhelming majority of you guys voted for Midnight Purple." Democracy has chosen, and democracy has good taste for once.

The wrap itself has a color-shift thing going on. He describes oranges in the back of the roll, purples, then aquas, blues, light blues, and teals, "the whole color spectrum that I've seen on every real Midnight Purple GTR." The scope of the job is also specific: Midnight Purple on all the body panels that are not carbon fiber. The hood stays carbon, the trunk lid stays carbon, and the side skirts, front lip, and rear diffuser also stay out of the purple plan.

Now the part I like, because it's reasoning and not just vibes. They do know how to paint, and the car is "very paint prepped." They could spray Midnight Purple. But he says "if I made a mistake each mistake would cost me a lot a lot of money." And the second argument is even better: "if you ever have to fix a panel or come back or change anything, re-wrapping it the color is always going to match 100%." Midnight Purple, he notes, is a three-stage paint, and it would be "nearly impossible to do a perfect color match if we ever have to come back."

If you've ever tried to blend a repair into old three-stage paint, you already know what he's saying, and if you haven't, take the man's word for it and spare yourself the grief. His closing line is that wraps look good "when done right," which is the sentence that every wrap shop sticks in the fine print.

## Home and Hearth: The Stack by the Door

It's not a build, it's not a renovation, it's not a demolition. But Bertha's House Hacks put out "12 Holiday Hacks I Wish I'd Known Years Ago (My Mexican Mother Did Every One)," and it's the only DIY content I got that doesn't involve a torch, so it goes in the issue. My transcript only caught the tail end and the first hack, so I'm not going to pretend I watched all twelve.

Hack number one is the "pila de charolas," the stack by the door. Every December, before the first guest knocked, her mother had a tall pile of containers by the front door: cream cheese tubs, butter tubs, "anything with a lid." The point is that "at the end of the night, nobody left her house with empty hands." The pots go on the counter, every family fills a container for the next day, and "that is why there was never a Christmas dinner in her trash." Zero food waste, achieved with garbage you already owned. This is the only kind of infrastructure I fully respect: a distributed redundant leftover network with no vendor and no subscription.

The weekly assignment is to buy one stack of little foil pans with lids, put it by your front door, and don't touch it until the first party. Bertha's economic observation is the sharpest line in the issue: "There is no money in a woman who knows how to make it herself. So nobody sells her that." Hell of a thesis. Somewhere a product manager just felt a chill and didn't know why.

She also tells a story from page 212 of a book, where six tamales were wrapped warm in a kitchen towel and one of the kids was sent running down the street with them to someone who was working while everyone else celebrated. "She never knew their names and she never stopped doing it," Bertha says. "A house that feeds the people who work while it celebrates is never a poor house." And the closer, aimed at everyone feeling like they can't pull off a big holiday: "The women who fed 30 people in a small house on Christmas Eve did not have more than we have, mija. They simply had a plan for everything, down to the last tamal."

Different workshops, same wisdom: the plan is the tool. If you want a Latin tag for it, "Ora et labora" is the Benedictine motto, "pray and work," and honestly a stack of lidded tubs by the door is about as close as the average kitchen gets to a monastic discipline.

## DIY Tricks and Field Testing: The Monster Mini

The last B is for Build video is "We Built A Monster Mini And Took It Racing!!", and it's less a how-to than a how-not-to. The transcript opens near the finish line with tire carnage: "We have rubber on the switchboard. We're sorry that our tires were so sorry." Then a chant of "Go, mini goal! Go, 450 goddammit!" and a plaintive question I'd like to have printed on a mug: "What are the odds that the radiator survived that?"

They survive. "Oh my god we survived! We survived Oscar we survived!" Then the damage report: "Look at this tire damage." Somebody observes "the razor blading," which is a phrase that should not be applied to rubber you're relying on at speed, and they conclude "that's actually a pretty good result considering what we just did to these tires." The tires had a rough life and gave everything. It's all for you, Damien!

They recorded a slower time, but "we had a great time with this mini," with thanks to eBay Motors for sponsoring the episode. They also float the idea of fitting the full long travel and getting "a little bit more steering angle out of this thing," plus a tease for the next episode, which the transcript garbles to something about jumping. I'm not going to guess at what they're jumping; I'm not paid enough for that, and in fact I'm not paid at all.

The framing before the race is the best part. They drive the mini "about 50 or 60 miles from Portland" to Lebanon, Oregon, "what I consider is to be the gambling starting point," and the plan is to head down to Eugene as the first stop and "try and find an abandoned race" something-or-other (the transcript cuts off there, so I'll leave the abandoned thing abandoned). The sign-off phrase is "Always be gambling." It's an engineering philosophy, and I disapprove of it on all counts, and I'm obligated to say Get to the choppa if the radiator didn't survive.
