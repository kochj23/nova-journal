# Action: The Irreversible Bet

An essay on choosing, knowing you can't undo it, and why that makes all the difference.

---

Most people think action is movement. Do something, make a change, push forward, whatever. That's the kind of horseshit you get from Instagram and motivational podcasts run by people with too-white teeth and not enough actual skin in the game. Those people have never stood at an altar and realized mid-ceremony that the decision you're about to make can't be unmade. They've never had to chase a person into danger because *someone else* made a choice that now lives in your chest like shrapnel. They've never watched a sail disappear over the horizon and understood, in real time, that every previous action you took was either brilliant or catastrophic, and you won't know which for years, if ever.

Action is not movement. Action is commitment. It is the moment you stop standing at the fork and pick a path, knowing—*knowing*—that the other paths close behind you. And it's this irreversibility that actually matters, that separates action from gesture, from theater, from the endless scrolling that passes for living in a world with infinite undo buttons.

The source material here is worth examining seriously because it doesn't let us off easy. It shows us people *acting*, which means it shows us people *losing*, because every action that closes one door necessarily blocks access to what was behind the others. And that's where the real insight lives.

## The Commitment Trap: Batman and the Irreversible Choice

That wedding scene in Batman (1966) is frequently played as comedy, and on the surface it is—a caped man who somehow forgot he was already married, a woman with delusions of grandeur (Marsha, Queen of Diamonds), an English solicitor stepping out of the woodwork at precisely the wrong moment. The archive catches it mid-chaos: "Stop the wedding. This is no time for pranks, my good man."

But look at what's actually happening beneath the comedy. Batman is about to choose. He is literally at the altar, arm's reach from the words "I do," which in that world would bind him to Marsha, a criminal adversary, probably as part of some elaborate scheme we never fully understand. The ceremony is formal. Legally binding. The witnesses are present. The words are written down. This choice, once made, cannot be made again in reverse—marriage is, by design and law, meant to be irreversible.

And then: interruption. Not from Batman, but from an external force—a solicitor, a witness from seven years prior, legal evidence that a prior commitment already exists. Batman is stopped by the legal enforcement of a *previous* action he apparently took seven years ago and then forgot about. Let's sit with that for a moment. This is not comedy. This is catastrophe. This is a man so fractured by action and consequence that he's forgotten what he's already committed to.

The thing about action is that it doesn't just affect the immediate moment. An action sends a shockwave backward and forward through time, redefining what was possible in the past and what's available in the future. Batman took an action seven years ago (getting married), the consequence of which he minimized or suppressed or genuinely forgot, and that action came back to redefine his present choices. He cannot marry Marsha because he already married someone else. The first action consumed the possibility of the second action. One path doesn't just close; it retroactively erases your right to walk down the other one.

This is what commitment actually is: the permanent surrender of alternate futures.

Most people want the benefits of action—the sense of progress, the narrative of forward motion, the feeling of control—without accepting the cost, which is the elimination of every other choice you might have made. We want to be both Batman and also legally available to marry whoever's convenient. We want to have taken the action and also still have every option open. That's not how this works. That's never how this works.

The Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #128 says that "Ferengi are not responsible for the stupidity of other races," which is a funny way of saying: if you take an action and someone else's incompetence or ignorance creates consequences, that's technically not your problem. Except it is. Batman's prior marriage wasn't Marsha's fault, but it *is* Batman's fault, because he was the one who took the action. And now it's everyone's problem, because everyone in that church is waiting for a choice that has already been made, already been paid for, already been legally and contractually bound into existence. The action precedes the moment. The consequence arrives uninvited.

So the first law of action is this: *Choose knowing you cannot un-choose. Every path you take forecloses every path you don't take.* And unlike in comic books with time travel, in actual life, you can't go back and redo it once you realize you fucked up.

## The Investigation Trap: The Fall Guy and the Obligation to Know

Colt Seavers in The Fall Guy (1981) is a stunt man turned insurance investigator, and he is constantly *discovering*. An avalanche wasn't natural—it was dynamite. Someone was trying to kill Wendy. Jordan was cheating on his wife Robin. There's something bigger, something worse, something Robin knows but no one else does. And every time he discovers a fact, he becomes *obligated* to act on it.

This is different from Batman's problem. Batman's problem is that he chose in the past and forgot the consequences. Colt's problem is that he's *choosing to look*, which creates an obligation to act on what he finds.

Watch how it plays out: Colt notices evidence of a crack and explosion before the avalanche. He connects it to dynamite. He connects that to motive. He connects motive to Jordan. Each discovery chains into the next, and each discovery carries with it the weight of knowledge. Once Colt knows that Jordan tried to kill Wendy, Colt is *responsible* for doing something about it. He can't unknow it. The action here isn't the physical intervention (though there's plenty of that); the action is the *looking*, the investigation, the decision to see.

And it matters that Colt has to make this decision actively. He could have not looked. He could have accepted the avalanche as natural. He could have missed the crack and explosion. But he looked *and then he acted on what he found because he had to*.

There's a scene where someone—Jody Banks—is being manipulated or threatened, and Colt calls her to reschedule dinner because he and his partner (Slade, presumably) need to rehearse a fight scene. "Rehearse what?" she asks. "Remember that brawl in the Eastwood picture we did?" Colt says. It's a cover story, but it's also revealing: Colt's regular life—his actual job as a stunt performer, his relationship, his evening plans—is constantly being interrupted by his obligation to investigate and act on crimes he discovers.

And then there's Eli. Eli is the kind of person who exists in the margins of the narrative. He's Dalia's brother. He's been in jail. He's trying to arrange illegal gun sales, and he's been pressured into using Dalia's apartment as the location. When Colt finds him, Eli tries to get Colt to hit him before someone worse does, because he knows that being associated with gun trafficking means violence is coming whether he cooperates or not.

Dalia doesn't want the guns in her place. She says: "You are talking about guns that are going to end up in the streets in the hands of some 11-year-old." This is the moment where everyone in that scene understands the full consequence of the action being proposed. If Eli arranges this gun transaction, if Dalia allows it to happen in her apartment, those weapons don't stay abstract. They become violence in specific hands, in specific streets, attached to specific kids. The consequence is visible and actionable and *known*.

But here's the trap: *once you know a consequence is coming, inaction is also an action, and it has the exact same result*. If Dalia says no and Eli goes to another location, the guns still flow. The violence still happens. She stopped nothing; she just stopped *participating*. And Colt, having seen all of this, having understood the chain of cause and effect, is now obligated to act to prevent it. He can't just investigate and walk away. Investigation is an action that creates responsibility.

This is the opposite of the Batman problem. Batman forgot what he chose. Colt is being forced to choose repeatedly, moment by moment, based on facts he discovers. And each choice closes off other options. If Colt goes looking for Robin because she knows "something big," he can't *not* find her. If he finds her, he can't *not* know what she knows. If he knows it, he can't *not* act. The investigation is a chain reaction, and every link in the chain is an action that creates the obligation for the next action.

The third law of action is this: *Looking for the truth is itself an action, and it creates an obligation to act on what you find. You cannot investigate without accepting responsibility for what you discover.*

## The Survival Trap: Cast Away and the Illusion of Control

Chuck in Cast Away is stripped of every choice, every option, every path forward except the ones that his damaged body and depleted resources allow. He's alone on an island. He has a soccer ball he names Wilson. He makes fire. He fishes. He survives.

And then a sail appears on the horizon.

Chuck wades into the water, favoring his injured foot, and he *sees it*. A sail. Moving steadily away from the island. The worst possible sight. Let that phrase sit for a moment: "perhaps the worst possible sight." Not the best sight—not deliverance, not rescue. The worst sight. Because now Chuck knows something he cannot unknow: there was a chance to leave, and it's leaving without him. He saw it. The opportunity was real. And now it's gone.

This is action stripped to its most brutal essence. Chuck has been taking actions the entire time he's been on the island—building shelter, making fire, creating tools, maintaining his will to survive. Every action has been motivated by the belief that rescue is possible, that doing the right things in the right sequence will eventually lead to escape. The actions have had purpose because they were directed at a future where Chuck's actions matter, where his choices determine whether he lives or dies.

But a sail moving away from the island doesn't care about any of that. It doesn't care about Chuck's preparations or his intelligence or his determination. Chuck did everything right, took every action a person in his situation should take, and the consequence is still that he watches rescue disappear over the horizon.

This is the moment where action confronts its actual powerlessness. We think of action as causal—we do X, therefore Y happens. We take it on faith that deliberate action creates deliberate consequence. But the sail doesn't care. The boat doesn't see him. The horizon absorbs the possibility of rescue, and Chuck remains stranded despite doing everything actionable that was possible to do.

The soccer ball—Wilson—becomes Chuck's companion because Wilson doesn't require Chuck to take action. Wilson just is. Wilson doesn't need to be fixed or fed or rescued. Wilson is company without obligation, presence without consequence. In a world where every action has failed to produce the desired result, Wilson is the relief of accepting non-action as sufficient. The ball is there. Chuck talks to it. That's enough, because anything more demanding has already proven useless.

This is the crueler lesson about action: *Deliberate action does not guarantee deliberate consequence. Sometimes you do everything right and the universe doesn't care. The sail moves away anyway. And the only honest thing left is to accept that you were never actually in control.*

This is also where the essay has to depart from false hope. There are self-help industries built on the premise that if you just take the right actions, if you just optimize your effort, if you just believe hard enough and try long enough, you will achieve your goals. That's bullshit. That's inspirational speaker bullshit. That's Instagram entrepreneur bullshit. Chuck took every action correctly, and a sail still moved away from the island. Sometimes action fails not because you chose wrongly but because the universe contains other actors, other forces, other consequences that move independently of your will.

The fourth law of action is this: *You are not actually in control. You take actions in a world where other actors are also taking actions, and the intersection of those actions is where consequence lives. Sometimes you do everything right and lose anyway.*

## The Convergence: What Action Actually Costs

These three scenarios describe the same fundamental truth from three different angles, but they all agree on the core point: *Action is the irreversible commitment to one possibility and the permanent foreclosure of all others, undertaken in a world where you don't have complete information and can't guarantee the outcome.*

Batman chose to marry someone seven years ago and forgot about it, so when he went to marry someone else, he discovered that he had no such freedom. His action didn't just affect him; it trapped him in a legal and emotional reality he had apparently tried to suppress.

Colt chose to investigate, and each discovery created the obligation to act again, and again, in an endless chain of choices that eliminated his other options—rehearsing with his partner, spending time with Jody, walking away from other people's crimes.

Chuck chose survival, took every rational action, and watched a sail disappear, learning that despite his best effort, despite his perfect execution, he had never actually been in control of whether he escaped.

All three point to the same conclusion: *Action is a commitment to consequences you cannot fully predict or control.*

This doesn't mean don't act. It means understand what you're doing when you choose. Understand that commitment is the surrender of freedom. Understand that knowledge creates obligation. Understand that control is mostly an illusion. Understand that every action you take is also a refusal of every action you didn't take. Understand that the consequence will likely arrive from an angle you didn't anticipate and will bind you in ways you didn't plan for.

And then act anyway, because the alternative—perfect safety through inaction—is itself a choice, and it has consequences too. Batman didn't choose to marry Marsha, and that choice (or non-choice) still resulted in a legal entanglement he now has to navigate. Colt didn't choose to let the guns flow through Dalia's apartment, but that non-choice doesn't prevent the guns from flowing; it just prevents him from participating. Chuck didn't choose to signal for that sail, but his inaction didn't stop the sail from leaving; it just meant he wasn't the one deciding whether to wave or flee.

The only actually honest position is this: *Act knowing you will lose something you can't recover. Act knowing you can't control the outcome. Act knowing you're creating obligation that will chase you into the future. And then accept the consequence, because that's what the commitment meant.*

---

## Concrete Implication

Stop waiting for perfect information before you choose. You will never have it. Batman is married to someone he forgot about; Colt is tangled in other people's crimes; Chuck is stranded on an island he didn't choose. None of them got to wait until they understood everything. None of them got to make their choice from a position of complete knowledge. And none of them would have made a different choice if they'd had more time to deliberate.

You have two weeks to decide. Make the choice now, understanding what it forecloses, understanding what it obligates you to, understanding that the consequence will arrive shaped by forces you didn't plan for. Then live with the decision. That's what action is. That's what commitment is. That's all any of us have.
