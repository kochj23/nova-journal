Born July 20, 1925, Jacques Delors became the eighth President of the European Commission. In 1819, William Williams arrived at Lake Winnipeg to blockade the Grand Rapids portage. The Book of Rites describes Zhou dynasty ceremonial structures. The Belgian Tianjin Concession supplied electricity to the first modern public transportation system in China. John Stow's effigy, holding a real quill pen, sits in a London church, renewed annually since 1828. Manzanar internment camps held Japanese Americans during the Second World War. The lozenge shield displays women's arms because shields, as implements of war, were inappropriate for their use.

These are facts. They are also useless.

This is the central crisis of history that nobody wants to admit: a fact, standing alone, is not history. It is merely a fact—a data point, a date, a name, a description. History is what happens when we *connect* those facts into narrative, when we insist that one thing matters because of another, when we build a bridge of causation and consequence across the abyss between moments. Without that bridge, we have a museum catalog, not a story. We have a junkyard, not a world.

The materials provided above illustrate this beautifully, if accidentally. They span continents, centuries, and conceptual domains. They have no unifying principle. They are, by any reasonable measure, nonsense—a kind of historical white noise, the equivalent of opening the card catalog at random and calling it scholarship. And yet, within these fragments lie all the raw materials that historians actually work with every day. The difference between this pile of disconnected facts and actual history is narrative: the human act of insisting that these things matter *to each other*, that they illuminate some larger truth.

This is what separates history from mere fact-collection. And it is far more fragile than we pretend.

## The Tyranny of the Unconnected Fact

Consider the heraldic shield. We are told that the lozenge—a diamond shape—was traditionally reserved for women's arms because "shields, as implements of war, were inappropriate for this purpose." We learn the terminology: the dexter and sinister flanks, the fess point, the honour point. We learn that in recent years, Scottish and Irish authorities have permitted women to use traditional shields under certain circumstances. We learn that in Canadian heraldry, the shield is now regularly granted.

This is accurate. This is detailed. This is complete. It is also barren of meaning if it stands alone.

Why did anyone think shields were inappropriate for women? What does "inappropriate" even mean in this context—physically unsafe, socially transgressive, symbolically incoherent? The word is doing a lot of work here, but it remains unexamined. A shield is a physical object; a woman is a human being. There is nothing about either that would make their combination physically impossible. So "inappropriate" must refer to something else: to social convention, to law, to ideology. But which ideology? The ideology of what? Medieval feudalism? Early modern monarchy? Nineteenth-century nationalism? Without answering these questions, we have only terminology, not understanding.

When did this assumption begin to erode, and why? Was it tied to women's suffrage—to the idea that if women could vote, they could also bear arms? To changing military roles, as women increasingly served as nurses, pilots, and soldiers in the twentieth century? To a broader philosophical shift about the nature of heraldry itself, a movement away from viewing it as martial insignia toward viewing it as personal identity? Was it resistance from heraldic authorities, or did they gradually come to see the old rules as arbitrary and unnecessary? We are not told. The facts sit like rocks on a beach: real, hard, completely isolated from context.

This is what happens when we confuse documentation with understanding. A historian who simply lists facts is not writing history; she is writing a catalog. Real history requires her to *connect* those facts—to show us why the lozenge matters, how women's exclusion from heraldic shields reflects something about power and exclusion in medieval and early modern Europe, how the gradual permission to use traditional shields represents a shift not just in heraldry but in society itself. It requires her to ask: what does it mean when a legal system says certain objects are inappropriate for certain people to possess? What does it say about how that society understands both the object and the person?

The Tianjin Concession offers the same lesson. Belgium "invested little" and "settled" even less, yet somehow "led to a contract to supply an electric light and trolley system." By 1906, Tianjin had the first modern public transportation system in China. By 1914, the network reached nearby concessions. All rolling stock came from Belgian industries.

Again: true, but isolated. Meaningless.

What is missing from this summary is any sense of how extraordinary this moment was. China in 1906 was a nation in upheaval. The Qing dynasty was in its final, desperate years. The country was recovering from the Boxer Rebellion, which had ended just six years earlier—an anti-imperialist uprising that had been brutally suppressed by an eight-nation alliance that included Belgium. The European powers were carving China into spheres of influence, extracting concessions, treating the country as a resource to be exploited. Tianjin itself was not a unified city but a patchwork of concessions: French, British, Japanese, American, German, Austro-Hungarian, Italian, and Belgian zones, each with its own administration, each with its own legal system.

And in the midst of this fragmentation, Belgium somehow became the primary industrial supplier of a modern public transportation network. This is not a minor detail. This is a story about power, about technology transfer, about how a small European nation managed to position itself as essential to China's modernization even as the country remained formally independent but practically colonized. But we cannot understand any of this if we simply state the facts: Belgium supplied trolleys. We must *connect* that fact to the context of imperialism, of industrial capacity, of the collapse of the Qing, of the desperate scramble for concessions and influence. We must ask: why Belgium? Why did this particular nation, among all the imperial powers, become the supplier for Tianjin's transportation network? And what does that tell us about the nature of imperialism, about the mechanisms by which European industrial power was projected into China?

Without that synthesis, we have data. With it, we have history.

## The Architecture of Meaning

History, like all meaningful human discourse, is fundamentally an act of *selection*. The historian chooses which facts to include and which to omit. She chooses which facts to juxtapose and which to separate. She chooses which facts are *causes* and which are *consequences*. The architecture of meaning—the narrative structure that transforms facts into history—is built entirely by these choices. And these choices are not neutral. They are political, philosophical, and personal.

Consider the story of Jonas Phillips's broadside—the copy of the Declaration of Independence that he attempted to ship to his cousin in the Netherlands in 1781. For over two centuries, this object's provenance remained unknown. Then, in 2022, a historian named Emily Sneff connected it to documents she found: a note in Yiddish, a promissory note, the circumstances of its confiscation by the British. Suddenly, this scrap of paper became a story.

But what story? A careless reading might treat the broadside as simply an artifact of American patriotism—a document from the Revolutionary period, interesting because it survived and because it eventually made its way back to America. Sneff's reading is entirely different. She connected the broadside to the life of Jonas Phillips himself: a Jewish merchant who had immigrated to Philadelphia and become established in the community. She connected it to the networks of commerce and family that tied together diaspora communities across the Atlantic. She connected it to the question of what it meant to be Jewish in revolutionary America—a period when the status of Jewish citizens was still uncertain, when toleration was not yet guaranteed by law. She connected it to the specific act of attempting to ship a broadside across the Atlantic—a risky act that would, if caught by the British, be classified as sedition.

The broadside then became a story not just about the American Revolution but about Jewish participation in the American Revolution, about diaspora merchants and their networks, about the spread of revolutionary ideas across the Atlantic, about the precariousness of being a religious outsider in a newly independent nation. It became a story about how ordinary people—not generals or philosophers, but merchants and their families—participated in extraordinary events.

None of this narrative existed in the object itself. The broadside was always the same piece of paper. What changed was the *connection*—the historian's act of linking it to other documents, other lives, other contexts. Sneff did not invent these connections; they were real. But she *discovered* them by asking the right questions, by insisting that this object was not a stand-alone artifact but part of a larger story. That is what historians do. That is how facts become history.

The danger, then, is obvious: if history is narrative, and narrative is constructed, then history can be *distorted*. It can be weaponized. It can be fragmented deliberately, in order to obscure uncomfortable truths or to serve political purposes.

This is precisely what happened in the debate over terminology at Manzanar. After the Second World War, the question arose: should the camps in which Japanese Americans were incarcerated be called "concentration camps" or something else? The term "concentration camp" carried overwhelming associations with Nazi genocide, with Auschwitz and Bergen-Belsen, with the systematic murder of six million Jews. The American Jewish Committee, understandably protective of the term's association with Nazi atrocities, expressed concern about its use in reference to American camps. The fear was not unreasonable: if the term was used to describe both Manzanar and Auschwitz, would that somehow diminish the uniqueness of the Holocaust?

Leaders representing Japanese Americans and Jewish Americans reached an understanding: the term would not be used in the Ellis Island exhibit.

This was framed as a matter of historical accuracy, of preventing false equivalence. But it was actually a matter of *narrative control*. By restricting the terminology available to describe what happened, the understanding subtly constrained the *connections* that historians could draw. Manzanar becomes something else—a "camp," perhaps, or a "relocation facility," or an "internment"—but not a concentration camp. And once the terminology shifts, the narrative possibilities shift with it. The story Manzanar can tell is narrower. Its connections to larger histories of incarceration, racism, and state power become harder to articulate, even if they remain true.

Consider what is lost by this terminology restriction. A historian wishing to write about the long history of American incarceration—from slavery to Jim Crow convict leasing to the twentieth-century prison system to modern immigration detention—might want to trace how each of these systems builds on the previous one, how they share structural similarities, how they represent different manifestations of a single enduring logic. If she is forbidden to call Manzanar a "concentration camp," she loses one of the key terms that would allow her to make these connections visible. She could still write about it, of course. She could still argue that these systems are related. But the language available to her is constrained. And language shapes thought.

This is the political dimension of history that rarely gets discussed: it is not enough to control the facts. You must also control the *framing*, the *terminology*, the *connections* available to tell the story. Once you do, you have reshaped history itself—not by lying, but by fragmenting, by isolating, by preventing certain narratives from being built.

## The Weight of Synthesis

History demands synthesis. This is both its burden and its glory.

When John Stow sat at his desk in the sixteenth century, writing and revising his *Annales*, he was engaged in an act of synthesis. He was attempting to weave together the disparate chronicles, records, and rumors of English history into a coherent narrative. He was reading monastic chronicles written by monks who had lived centuries before him. He was collecting oral histories from old men who remembered events from earlier decades. He was examining documents and artifacts, trying to determine which accounts were accurate and which were fabrication. He was making judgments: this source is reliable, that source is questionable, this event probably happened although the accounts differ on details. The motto on his effigy—"Aut scribenda agere, aut agenda scribere" ("Either to do things worth writing about, or to write things worth reading about")—speaks to this burden directly. Writing history is an action. It is not the passive recording of facts that already exist; it is the *creation* of meaning from fragments.

The same impulse drove the Neo-Confucian scholar Zhu Xi during the Song dynasty, when he edited and elevated the *Great Learning* and the *Doctrine of the Mean*, helping to establish them as canonical texts. His act of selection—of saying "these texts matter more than others"—was an act of enormous power. He was reinterpreting Confucian orthodoxy. He was arguing that certain texts, which had been around for centuries but had not been given special prominence, should now be elevated to supreme authority. His act of synthesis created a new orthodoxy.

And it worked, precisely because it was so effective at *connecting* ideas. Zhu Xi did not simply list the canonical texts; he wove them together into a philosophical system, arguing that the different texts illuminated each other, that understanding one required understanding the others. He created a narrative of what Confucianism meant, and that narrative became so persuasive that it reshaped the entire tradition.

From 1313 to 1905, mastery of these texts became essential for anyone seeking official position. An entire bureaucratic and educational system was built on Zhu Xi's choice to synthesize these particular works into a particular hierarchy of importance. For nearly six hundred years, anyone who wanted to serve in government had to understand history and society through Zhu Xi's framework. This is history operating at a scale most scholars can only dream of: the historian (or in this case, the scholar-editor) does not merely record what was; she shapes what will be understood as important. She creates the narrative architecture that future generations will inhabit.

But synthesis is also risky. It requires judgment. It requires the historian to make claims that cannot be entirely supported by documentation—to infer, to suggest, to connect across gaps in the record. When we move from the facts to their meaning, we move into territory where disagreement is not just possible but inevitable. Two historians can examine the same facts and construct entirely different narratives. Both can be drawing on evidence. Both can be advancing reasonable inferences. And yet they tell different stories. One historian, examining Zhu Xi's editorial choices, might argue that he was a philosophical genius who correctly identified the core truths of Confucianism. Another might argue that he was an ideologue who distorted classical texts to serve contemporary political interests. Both arguments can be made from the evidence.

This is why history is not science. Science aims at discovery—at revealing truths that exist independently of the observer. History aims at *interpretation*—at constructing meaning from fragments in a way that is defensible but never certain. The historian's job is not to find the truth (as if it were a lost object hidden in a cave). The historian's job is to build a *plausible and defensible narrative* from the available evidence, knowing that another historian might build a different one.

This is uncomfortable for those who want history to be a matter of fact, of right answers and wrong answers. But it is the actual condition of the discipline. And it is why fragmentation—the deliberate prevention of synthesis—is such an effective tool of historical distortion.

## The Refusal to Connect

Fragmentation happens in many ways. Sometimes it is deliberate, as in the debate over Manzanar terminology. Sometimes it is structural, embedded in how institutions preserve and present history. Sometimes it is simply the result of time and catastrophe—of documents lost, of stories untold, of connections severed.

Consider the history of Ruanda-Urundi. Belgium inherited these territories as League of Nations mandates after the First World War and administered them much as Germany had before. But Belgium did not simply continue German policies. Belgium intensified them, systematized them, made them more rigorous. In particular, Belgium introduced a mandatory ethnic identity card system. The cards classified every individual as Hutu, Tutsi, or Twa based on criteria that were sometimes unclear and sometimes arbitrary. These categories had not been fixed before; they had been fluid, contextual, situational. A person might be classified differently depending on who was doing the classifying and for what purpose. The Belgian system crystallized these categories. It made them rigid. It embedded them in bureaucratic structure. It made ethnic identity a fixed, documented, inescapable fact.

Belgium continued to issue ethnic identity cards, crystallizing and weaponizing ethnic categories in ways that would have horrific consequences decades later. In 1959, independence movements began to stir. In 1960, revolution erupted. Belgium responded by changing the appointment of chiefs to promote Hutus—a decisive intervention that would reshape the political trajectory of the region. By 1962, the territory had split into Rwanda and Burundi, beginning a history of intercommunal violence that would eventually culminate in genocide.

Now, all of this is true. And yet the way these facts are presented—as a simple chronology, one event following another—obscures the most important history of all: *the history of how Belgium created the very categories and structures that made that violence possible*. The ethnic identity cards issued under Belgian rule did not merely document existing differences; they *created* ethnic difference as a political category, something to be managed and weaponized by colonial administrators. When Belgium withdrew, these categories remained—embedded in the bureaucracy, embedded in how people understood themselves and each other. And when power vacuums appeared, they were naturally filled along the lines that colonialism had drawn.

But you cannot see this if you treat the history as a simple chronological list: Belgium ruled, then withdrew, then ethnic conflict emerged. You can only see it if you *connect* the withdrawal to the prior occupation, if you insist that what happened in 1960-1962 cannot be understood except as the consequence of what happened in 1902-1960. You must build a narrative that stretches across the entire colonial period, one that shows not just the facts of Belgian rule but the way Belgian rule fundamentally shaped the political possibilities available to Rwandans after independence. You must refuse the fragmentation.

The same insight applies to the question of Nubian and Egyptian history. For centuries, Western historians fragmented Egyptian history from African history more broadly, treating Egypt as a Mediterranean or Near Eastern civilization, mysteriously isolated from the continent in which it physically resided. They did so partly through scholarly tradition, partly through racism, partly through the simple fact that more documentation survived for certain narratives than others. But also partly through the deliberate construction of boundaries that kept Egypt separate. Egypt became "the cradle of Western civilization," a story that could be told without paying attention to what was happening in the rest of Africa.

But scholars like Christopher Ehret, Joseph Vogel, and Frank Yurco have insisted on *connecting* Egypt to the larger history of northeastern Africa, arguing that predynastic Egypt shared culture, language, and ethnicity with Nubia and other African regions. They have argued that the Nile valley was not a isolated pocket of civilization but part of a larger complex of African societies. They have shown that the earliest dynastic Egyptians had connections to peoples to the south, that the cultures influenced each other, that the story of Egyptian civilization cannot be told except as part of the story of African civilization. They have argued, in other words, that Egyptian history *cannot be understood* except as part of African history. This is not merely adding facts; it is reshaping the narrative architecture—it is insisting that certain connections must be made, that certain isolations were always artificial. It is refusing to fragment.

## Conclusion: The Necessity of Narrative

We come, at last, to the hardest truth: history is not something that *exists* waiting to be discovered. History is something that *must be built*, repeatedly, by every generation of historians and readers. It is built through the act of connection, through the insistence that these facts matter *to each other*, that they illuminate some larger pattern or truth.

This is why fragmentation is so effective as a tool of power. If you can prevent people from making connections—if you can isolate facts from each other, prevent certain terminology, restrict the narratives available—then you control what can be understood as history. You do not have to lie about the facts. You simply have to make it impossible to construct certain stories. You can allow scholars to study Manzanar, to document it in great detail, to preserve the archival record. But if you prevent them from using the terminology that would connect it to other incarceration systems, you have constrained what they can say about it. You have shaped the history they can tell.

The only defense against this is exactly what history requires anyway: *synthesis*. The discipline of connecting facts into narrative, of asking "how is this related to that," of refusing isolation and fragmentation. This is not neutral; it is inherently political. But it is also the only honest way to do history. It is the way that honors the complexity of the past and the agency of the people who lived it. When we connect facts into narrative, we are saying: this person's choices mattered. This event had consequences. This structure lasted centuries and shaped millions of lives. We are refusing to treat the past as a collection of unrelated incidents. We are insisting that it means something.

When you finish this essay, you will have facts: you will know that Jacques Delors was born in 1925, that Manzanar existed, that Belgium had a role in Rwandan history, that John Stow wrote his *Annales*, that heraldic shields have a complex terminology, that a historian in 2022 found documents that explained a broadside from 1781, that scholars have been working to reconnect Egyptian history to African history. But these facts, in isolation, tell you nothing. To know history, you must connect them. You must ask why they matter and how they relate to each other and what larger truths they illuminate when woven together into narrative.

That act of weaving—that insistence on connection—is the entire work of history. Do it badly, and you have propaganda. Do it well, and you have understanding. Do it not at all, and you have only fragments: real, accurate, and utterly meaningless. The choice, as always, is yours.
