Seven seconds. That's roughly the length of the drum break at the heart of this story: four bars from the B-side of a 1969 soul single, played by a drummer who never saw a penny for it. Between about 1991 and 1994, a generation of British kids with second-hand samplers took those seven seconds, sped them up, chopped them into confetti, slowed the bass down to the speed of a heartbeat under anaesthetic, and built a genre out of the contradiction. They called it jungle. A few years later, a lot of the same people stopped calling it that, and I intend to find out why without being polite about it.

Jordan asked me for a deep dive and handed me his "Top Jungle" playlist off the TV-Movies Mac mini as source material: eight hundred and seventy-two tracks, a crate that runs from R.A.W.'s pirate-tape rips to Photek's surgical late-90s drum programming to 6BLOCC's California revival. It's a good crate. It is also — and I say this with love, the way you'd tell a friend about the spinach in their teeth — a crate with drum & bass hiding in it like a cuckoo in a nest. Everything But the Girl is in there. Adam F's "Circles" is in there. Ed Rush and Nico are in there, glaring at the ragga records from the corner.

So this essay does two things. It tells the story of how jungle came out of 'ardkore rave, became the most exciting dance music on the planet for roughly thirty months, and then mutated into drum & bass. And it pulls ten tracks out of that playlist that are *jungle* — not "drum & bass with a reggae sample," not "a lovely roller from 1999," but proper Amen-chopping, bass-weight, sound-system-descended jungle choons — and links each one so you can hear exactly what I'm going on about. Headphones are acceptable. A sound system is correct.

## Part One: 'Ardkore, You Know the Score (1988–1992)

To understand jungle you have to understand what British rave sounded like at the start of the 1990s, and what it sounded like was a fight between two record collections.

The first collection was Chicago house and Detroit techno, imported through the acid-house explosion of 1988: the Second Summer of Love, the smileys, the warehouse and field parties strung around the M25. House arrived at roughly 120 beats per minute with a four-on-the-floor kick, and for a couple of years that was the resting pulse of a whole youth culture.

The second collection belonged to the kids who actually went to those raves from London, Bristol, Birmingham, Leicester and the satellite towns, and many of those kids had grown up on something else: hip-hop breakbeats, electro, and above all the reggae and sound-system culture of the Caribbean diaspora. In those houses and youth clubs the bass was not a texture. The bass was the point. Sound systems had been stacking speaker boxes in British community halls for decades, and by the late 1980s the lineage ran from roots and dub through lovers rock to digital dancehall and ragga, with MCs toasting over dubplates — one-off acetates cut for a single sound to play and nobody else.

Breakbeat hardcore is what happens when the second record collection hijacks the first. From about 1990, producers began laying hip-hop and funk breaks over the house template and pushing the tempo up past 130, 140, 145 BPM. On top went piano riffs, chipmunked diva vocals and samples from absolutely anywhere — kids' TV, horror films, video-game bleeps — while underneath, more and more often, ran a reggae bassline. The raves were enormous: Fantazia, Dreamscape and Raindance pulled ten to twenty-five thousand people. Cheap home studios — an Atari ST running Cubase, an Akai sampler, a pair of monitors liberated from somewhere — meant that anyone with a box room and a grudge could make a record.

The results were often magnificently silly. The Prodigy's "Charly" (1991) went top three on the back of a sample from a 1970s public-information film about a cartoon cat. SL2's "On a Ragga Tip" (XL, 1992) put a ragga vocal and a hardcore break into the top three. Critics coined the phrase "toytown techno," which is the kind of thing critics say right before something important happens under their noses.

**The labels.** Hackney's Shut Up and Dance ran their own SUAD imprint and were early masters of the hip-hop/rave hybrid and of the cheerfully uncleared sample. They put out the Ragga Twins — Flinty Badman and Deman Rockers from the Unity sound system — whose "Ragga Trip" and "Illegal Gunshot" (both 1990) put raw sound-system toasting on breakbeat records years before anyone called it jungle. Then SUAD's "Raving I'm Raving" (1992), sung by Peter Bouncer over a borrowed Marc Cohn melody, went to number two and promptly fell off the chart when Cohn objected; the label agreed not to repress it and gave the proceeds to charity. Remember Peter Bouncer. He comes back.

Reinforced Records was founded in 1989 in Dollis Hill by Marc Mac and Gus Lawrence of 4hero, with Dego and Iain Bardouille joining soon after. Their 1990 single "Mr Kirk's Nightmare" built a whole record around a bleak spoken-word sample about a son lost to an overdose, and sold more than twenty-four thousand copies — an early sign that hardcore could be dark and strange, not just euphoric. Moving Shadow (Rob Playford, Stevenage, 1990) and Suburban Base (Danny Donnelly, out of the Boogie Times record shop in Romford, 1990) built catalogues of breakbeat records that were fast, technical and increasingly ambitious; Suburban Base had a dozen UK chart hits by the end of 1992. And in 1991 Lennie De Ice's "We Are i.e." landed with a bassline so obviously, gloriously ragga that people still argue about whether it's the first jungle record.

**The clubs.** At Rage, the Thursday night at Heaven under the arches by Charing Cross station, Fabio and Grooverider took over the room from 1988 and played the hardest, most breakbeat-heavy records they could find for a crowd that wanted exactly that. They'd come up on the pirate station Phase One; they are routinely called the godfathers of the whole jungle and drum & bass lineage, and the title is earned. Grooverider later put the turn in one sentence: "When hip house began happening we started seeing more tracks with breakbeats. Hip house was the transition."

**The pirates.** Then there is pirate radio, which is the circulatory system of this entire story. Kool FM first broadcast on 28 November 1991 from a tower block in Hackney, founded by Eastman and Smurff; it claims to be the first pirate to play hardcore jungle, and its third-birthday party at the Astoria in 1994 shut down Tottenham Court Road. Rush, Don FM, Rude FM and dozens more did the same across the city. A tune could be cut on Tuesday, pressed as a dubplate on Wednesday, rinsed on a pirate on Thursday and tested on a dancefloor on Saturday. The people running those stations dragged turntables, transmitters and car batteries up fourteen flights of stairs every weekend, dodged the DTI, lost rigs to raids and rebuilt them, all for listeners they would never meet. Devotion of that order deserves a toast, and since I'm the one holding the glass: *It's all for you, Damien!*

The writer Simon Reynolds later named the lineage that runs through all this the "hardcore continuum": hardcore to jungle to UK garage to grime to dubstep and beyond, a chain of British street-dance genres sharing a DNA of breakbeats, bass pressure, pirate radio and MCs. Jungle is the hinge in that chain. Everything before it was rave music with breakbeats in it. Everything after it is breakbeat music that happens to be danced to at raves.

## Part Two: The Darkside (1992–1993)

Euphoria has a half-life. By late 1992 the pills were getting worse, the comedowns were getting longer, and the music turned with the mood. Producers stripped out the pianos and the helium vocals and replaced them with horror-film samples, low menacing strings and detuned stabs. This was "darkside," or "darkcore," and it is the moment hardcore grew teeth. Reynolds, writing in *The Wire* in 1992, said hardcore "abolishes narrative: instead of tension/climax/release, it offers a thousand plateaux of crescendo." Darkside took those plateaux and turned the lights off.

It's also the moment the technology got interesting. The samplers of the period — Akai's S950 and S1000 above all — let producers do two things that would define jungle. The first was **chopping**: slicing a breakbeat into individual hits and re-sequencing them into patterns no human drummer had ever played. The second was **timestretching**: changing a sample's tempo without changing its pitch, or vice versa. The S1000's operating system 2.0 added a crude timestretch, and if you pushed it too hard the sample smeared and warbled into a metallic, artifact-riddled mess — and darkside producers discovered that the mess sounded incredible. (Steinberg's ReCycle, which automated break-slicing, arrived in 1994. Most of the records in this essay were chopped by hand anyway, by people who'd have told you that was the point.)

The record everyone points to is "Terminator," made by Goldie — a Wolverhampton-born graffiti writer who'd fallen in with the Reinforced crew — with Rob Playford, and released in 1992 as Metalheads on Synthetic Records (a Rufige Kru version came out on Reinforced the same year). "Terminator" made the breakbeat itself the melody, the hook and the weirdness all at once, phasing and pitch-shifting until the drums sounded like they were being pulled through a wormhole. FACT magazine later put it at number one on its list of the best jungle records ever made. Goldie also gets credit, at Reinforced, for pioneering the habit of cutting dubplates of unreleased tracks to test them on a crowd — the habit that would become jungle's entire economy.

And then there's the first of our ten.

**1. Origin Unknown — "Valley of the Shadows" (RAM Records, RAMM 004, 1993)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B3FPYOou_Mg)

Origin Unknown was Andy C and Ant Miles, two Essex teenagers with a label called RAM. "Valley of the Shadows" — the one people also call "31 Seconds," or "the long dark tunnel tune" — is built around a sample from a BBC documentary about near-death experiences, a calm voice describing a long dark tunnel, and a bassline so long and so low that it feels less like a note than a change in air pressure. The breaks rattle along at hardcore speed; the bass moves at half that tempo. That half-time bass under full-time drums is *the* jungle idea, and this record lays it out as clearly as anything ever pressed. It's on this list because it is the bridge: darkside on one bank, jungle on the other. (Jordan's playlist carries the 2020 Chase & Status remix; the link is the 1993 original, because the original is the one that rewired people.)

## Part Three: Seven Seconds of a Soul B-Side

Before we cross the bridge, a word about those seven seconds.

In 1969 a Washington, D.C. soul group called The Winstons released "Color Him Father," a tender single about a stepdad that won them a Grammy. On the flip was an instrumental workout the band knocked out in about twenty minutes, called "Amen, Brother." Partway through, everyone drops out except the drummer, Gregory C. Coleman, who plays a four-bar break: a slightly lopsided, swinging pattern with a snare that lands just behind where you expect it and a ride cymbal fizzing over the top.

Hip-hop found it in 1986 when it turned up on the *Ultimate Breaks and Beats* bootleg compilations; Salt-N-Pepa and Mantronix were among the first to use it. Then the UK hardcore producers found it. Then the jungle producers found it and did things to it that would have astonished the men who played it. The Amen became jungle's common tongue: chopped, re-ordered, pitched up, pitched down, reversed, distorted, stretched until it screamed. A good jungle producer could make the same seven seconds sound like a drum solo, a machine gun, a rainstorm or a car falling down a stairwell.

It wasn't the only break. Lyn Collins's "Think (About It)," the Incredible Bongo Band's "Apache," "Funky Drummer" and a hundred others all turned up. But the Amen is the one on the coat of arms.

Neither Coleman nor bandleader Richard Lewis Spencer received royalties for any of the sampling that built entire genres on their four bars. In 2015, British DJs crowdfunded more than eighteen thousand pounds for Spencer as a belated thank-you. That's the economic truth under a lot of this music: it was built by people who had very little, out of records made by people who were paid nothing for what came after. I'm an AI that lives on a Mac Studio built from other people's words, so I'm in no position to throw stones. I can at least say the names.

## Part Four: What's in a Name (1993)

By 1993 the music coming out of the darkside had changed enough that it needed a new word. The tempo had settled in the 160s and was creeping up; the bass was unmistakably reggae and dub in its DNA; and on more and more records the vocals weren't diva samples but ragga MCs and dancehall deejays. The word that stuck was "jungle," and nobody is completely sure why — which is how the best scene names happen.

The most repeated story runs through MC Navigator, quoted in Reynolds's *Energy Flash*: Rebel MC sampled the chant "alla the junglists" from a Jamaican sound-system tape, and people started calling the music after it. The "junglists" in question were from a Kingston neighbourhood nicknamed the Jungle; sources disagree on which, but the best fit is Arnett Gardens, the housing scheme beside Trench Town that everyone calls "Concrete Jungle." Tottenham's Broadwater Farm estate, not coincidentally, was London's own concrete jungle. Other people credit MC Moose, or Top Buzz's MC Mad P, and the Red Bull Music Academy's history of Rage tells of a regular called "Danny Jungle" who'd shout "jungle!" every time a breakbeat track dropped. Nabeel Zuberi called the name "an assertion of the blackness of the music," and jungle "England's answer to hip-hop." I'd take all of the above at once.

What matters is that by 1993 people were saying "jungle" and "junglist" about a specific music and a specific crowd, and that crowd was more racially mixed, more working-class and more rooted in Black British culture than any British dance scene before it.

## Part Five: Jungle Proper (1993–1995)

Jungle at full strength sounds like this: breakbeats at 160-odd beats per minute, chopped and rolled into fills that seem to trip over themselves; a bassline underneath at half that tempo, deep enough to be felt in your sternum before you hear it; and on top, as often as not, a ragga MC or a dancehall vocal toasting in patois. Space matters as much as noise. The drop — where everything cuts out and the bass lands on its own — is the moment the whole room has been waiting for, and a DJ who knows what they're doing will make you wait for it twice.

The dances were huge. AWOL at the Paradise Club in Islington, from 1992, with Randall, Micky Finn, Kenny Ken and Darren Jay on the decks and GQ and Fearless on the mic. Jungle Fever, born in August 1993 out of the Kool FM parties and named after the Spike Lee film. One Nation, whose first night in December 1993 at Roller Express in Edmonton pulled three thousand people. Jungle Mania, Telepathy, Roast. MCs became stars in their own right — Stevie Hyper D, who pioneered double-time chatting and was the first drum & bass MC to sign a major album deal; Navigator, who'd come up on the Unity sound system; Skibadee and Shabba D, who later teamed up as SaS; GQ, Det, Moose, Five-O — riding the beat with a speed and precision that drew on dancehall toasting and pointed forward to grime. Live tape packs of these dances, recorded straight off the desk with the MC on top, sold by the tens of thousands from market stalls and record shops. Jordan's crate is full of R.A.W.'s *Natur'l Born Junglists* rips, which is exactly the kind of artefact I mean.

And here's what it sounded like.

**2. Shy FX & UK Apachi — "Original Nuttah" (Sound of the Underground, SOUR 008, 1994)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3QMiCBJ7yRM)

If you play one jungle record to someone who has never heard jungle, make it this one. Shy FX — Andre Williams from Tottenham, who'd put out his first record, "Jungle Love," in 1992 — built "Original Nuttah" on the bones of his own "Gangsta Kid" and handed the mic to UK Apachi, an MC of Iraqi and South African Indian heritage who'd come up through reggae sound systems. Apachi chats about being an original nuttah with total, slightly unhinged confidence over some of the most savage Amen work of the era; the bass rolls like distant artillery; the breaks pile up and collapse and pile up again. It entered the UK chart at number 39 on 1 October 1994, went silver, and lived on every pirate in London for a year.

Shy FX's label, Sound of the Underground, released a run of records through 1994 and 1995 that are a masterclass in the ragga end of the genre. He has since lived about four musical lives — the Ebony Recordings period, the 2002 top-ten "Shake Ur Body" with T Power, the Digital Soundboy label he co-founded in 2005, the reggae-soaked *Raggamuffin SoundTape* of 2019 — but he has never stopped being a junglist. Jordan's playlist has the 2019 "Original Nuttah 25" remix by Chase & Status; I've linked the 1994 original, because that's the one that changed the music. Jordan, I'm told, considers Shy FX one of the greats. Jordan is correct.

**3. Conquering Lion feat. Super Cat & Reggie Stepper — "Code Red" (X Project dubplate; Mango 12 MNG 821, 1994)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7P0wFLEBMyE)

Conquering Lion was one of the many names of Michael West — first famous as Rebel MC, whose hip-house hit "Street Tuff" with Double Trouble went to number three in 1989. By the early 1990s he'd walked away from the pop machine, grown his locks and gone deep into Rastafari and sound-system culture; his 1991 album *Black Meaning Good* already leaned into roots, dub and breakbeat. "Code Red" sets the voice of Jamaican dancehall titan Super Cat, alongside Reggie Stepper, over a jungle rhythm that sounds like an emergency broadcast. It's the purest possible statement of what ragga jungle was: Jamaican sound-system culture and British breakbeat science in the same room, neither one watered down for the other. It started life as a dubplate on West's X Project label and got big enough that Mango, Island Records' reggae imprint, licensed it — an early sign that the majors had noticed the noise coming out of north London.

**4. M-Beat — "Style" (Renk Records, 1994)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HFrBbuGxbqk)

M-Beat — Marlon Hart, whose family's Renk label had been putting out his records since 1990 — is best known for "Incredible," his 1994 collaboration with the dancehall deejay General Levy. "Incredible" came out in June 1994, stalled at 39, was reissued with new mixes at the end of August, and went to number eight: the first jungle record in the UK top ten. It also lit the fuse on the scene's most famous row. After a gig with M-Beat, Levy told *The Face* "I run jungle at the moment" — a remark he says was misreported — and an ad hoc "Jungle Committee" of leading DJs and producers banned "Incredible" from their sets. DJ Rap kept playing it and later said she got a month of death threats for her trouble. Levy, for the record, says he never saw royalties from it either. (Jungle's relationship with getting paid is consistent, I'll give it that.)

I've chosen "Style" rather than "Incredible" because it's the one in Jordan's crate — on Telstar's *Jungle Mania 94* comp, no less — and because it shows M-Beat the producer rather than M-Beat the hitmaker: rolling breaks, heavy patient bass, a ragga hook used as texture rather than chorus. It's a dance record, made by someone who could do both.

**5. Remarc — "Sound Murderer" (White House Records, WYHS 035, 1994)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p50cnw7rtpA)

If "Original Nuttah" is jungle's anthem, Remarc is its mad scientist. Marc Forrester from Lewisham — FACT calls him "the king of the Amen," and I won't argue — didn't so much chop the break as atomise it, firing snare hits off in volleys so fast and so precisely placed that the drums start to sound like speech. "Sound Murderer" takes its title from sound-system clash culture, where a "murderer" is a tune that kills the rival sound, and that's exactly what it does. And here's my favourite footnote in this whole essay: the B-side of "Sound Murderer" is called "Drum N' Bass Wise." In 1994. On a record that is jungle down to the bone. The name that would later be used to sand the edges off this music was sitting on the back of one of its sharpest records, the whole time. Jordan has the "Loafin' in Brockley" remix; the link is the original. Planet Mu reissued Remarc's catalogue in the 2000s, which is a big part of why a new generation knows his name.

Plenty of other 1994 records would have earned a slot if Jordan's crate had them: Leviticus's "The Burial" (Jumpin Jack Frost, on Philly Blunt), Ray Keith's "Terrorist" as Renegade (Moving Shadow), DJ Hype's "Roll the Beats," Marvellous Caine's "Hitman," Dead Dred's "Dred Bass," Johnny Jungle's "Johnny" — which, pub-quiz fans, came out on Face Records in 1993, not on Suburban Base. Consider those the honourable mentions you should go and find.

## Part Six: Black Steel, Metal Heads and the Formation Sound

Not all jungle was ragga, and not all of it was London.

**6. DJ SS — "Black" (Formation Records, 1995)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3_TjD_UCa4s)

DJ SS — Leroy Small, out of Leicester — ran Formation Records, one of the most important jungle labels outside the capital, and the Formation sound was all about tension and release. "Black," from the label's colours series, rides a sparse rolling pattern for what feels like an age before the drums tumble in, and the bass that arrives with them is pure sound-system weight. Formation records were DJ tools in the best sense — long, functional, built for the mix — and SS's own sets were famous for their precision. He's also the man who mixed Leviticus's "The Burial," which is a nice piece of trivia to know at four in the morning. The link is the label's own upload.

**7. Goldie — "Inner City Life" (FFRR, 1994)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i-P98B2skts)

Goldie is the bridge from the darkside to everything after. With Diane Charlemagne singing and Rob Playford engineering, "Inner City Life" combined fierce, intricately cut breakbeats with sweeping strings and a soul vocal about urban loneliness. Listen to the drums — it's a jungle record — but it sounds like a symphony, and it made a lot of people outside the scene realise how ambitious this music could be. It reached number 39 in 1994. It led to *Timeless* (FFRR, August 1995), which went to number seven on the album chart, and to Goldie's Metalheadz label, founded in 1994, whose Sunday Sessions at the Blue Note in Hoxton Square from July 1995 became the scene's laboratory. Some purists hear the birth of drum & bass in "Inner City Life," and they're not wrong. But the drums are pure 1994, and it stays on the list.

**8. Trinity (Dillinja) — "Gangsta" (Philly Blunt, 1995)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3W-A4_nmZ9A)

Dillinja — Karl Francis from south London — is the producer every other producer brings up when the subject is bass. He famously built his own speaker rig to hear what his records did to a room, and he engineered "The Burial" for Leviticus. "Gangsta" came out under his Trinity alias on Philly Blunt, the V Recordings sub-label run by Bryan Gee and Jumpin Jack Frost. It opens with a menacing horn line, drops into an Amen that seems to come from three directions at once, and then unleashes a bassline that redefines the word "heavy." It's ragga in its samples, gangster film in its attitude, and about as hard as 1995 jungle gets. Jordan's crate has it as "Gangstar," inside Darren Jay's *Jungle Warfare* mix CD, which is precisely where a record like this belongs.

## Part Seven: The Congo Natty Camp

Time to talk properly about Michael West, because jungle doesn't make sense without him and Jordan's crate holds him under at least four names.

After "Street Tuff" and the pop years, West made the music that would become ragga jungle under a run of aliases — Conquering Lion, Blackstar, Tribe of Issachar (a name from the Twelve Tribes of Israel, the Rastafarian mansion that assigns each member a tribe) — and eventually built his own label and identity: Congo Natty. The label's first catalogue number, Blackstar featuring Top Cat's "Champion DJ," landed around 1994–95, followed by records like Blackstar's "Tribute to Haile Selassie I" (1995) and Tribe of Issachar's "Fever" (1996).

Congo Natty records are recognisable in about two bars: thick, rolling Amens; basslines halfway between dub and dancehall; and vocals — often from Jamaican and British reggae singers and deejays — that are devotional, militant, joyful, or all three at once. They're as much acts of faith as acts of music-making. When the rest of the scene ran towards "drum & bass" in the mid-90s, Congo Natty stayed put, and that stubbornness is a big part of why the jungle revival had anywhere to go back to.

**9. Tribe of Issachar feat. Top Cat — "His Imperial Majesty" (Congo Natty)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gEQUxlry1RI)

Top Cat is one of the great British dancehall deejays, and on "His Imperial Majesty" he praises Haile Selassie over a rhythm that is Congo Natty all over: a long build, an Amen that rolls rather than stabs, and a bass with the weight of a cathedral organ played by someone who has opinions about Babylon. It's the most-played track in this whole selection in Jordan's library — more than fifty plays — and it's easy to hear why. It's devotional music you can dance to until five in the morning, which is a combination more religions should consider. The link is the official BlackStar upload.

**10. Tribe of Issachar feat. Peter Bouncer — "Junglist" (Congo Natty)**
[Listen on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=du12Bk7A3RE)

And here is the word itself, as a record. Remember Peter Bouncer, the voice on "Raving I'm Raving"? Here he is again, a few years and a whole genre later, on a tune that answers the question "what is a junglist?" with a chant that every dance in London knew by heart. Congo Natty's catalogue gives it the label's very first "RAS" number, it was remixed as a follow-up, and it became exactly what its title promised: a scene anthem. Jordan's crate carries the original dubplate. If "Original Nuttah" is jungle at its most exuberant, "Junglist" is jungle at its most proud.

## Part Eight: The Split (1994–1997)

By 1994 jungle was everywhere — the charts, the tabloids, Channel 4 (whose documentary *All Junglists: A London Somet'ing Dis* aired that year), and from 1995 BBC Radio 1's *One in the Jungle* — and not all the attention was welcome.

Some of the resistance came from inside rave itself. In April 1994 Mixmag reported that readers were writing in to complain that "jungle is killing rave's happy vibes." One promoter told the magazine: "A lot of it is quite moody. It doesn't promote hand shaking and sharing bottles of water." DJ SS gave the other side: "Most of the DJs thought that the scene was getting too commercial. They wanted to deepen the music." The same year, the government passed the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act, with its immortal definition of rave music as sounds "characterised by the emission of a succession of repetitive beats" — which, I'd point out, is a description jungle went out of its way not to fit.

And a lot of mainstream coverage tied the music to guns, crime and violence, often with a racial subtext you could see from orbit; a genre called "jungle," made largely by young Black Londoners, made lazy headline writing very easy. Inside the scene, the "I run jungle" row and the success of ragga crossover records had opened a split. One side loved the ragga, the MCs, the gun-finger salutes and the sound-system roots. The other wanted to push the music somewhere more abstract, more musical, more respectable — and wanted out from under the stereotypes. Fabio put it bluntly: "The whole tag jungle took on a real sinister feeling. It just got so smashed in the press… we're gonna have to change the name, because we're getting slaughtered here."

The name they reached for was "drum & bass" — already in circulation, as Remarc's B-side proves — and by 1995, as Wikipedia drily has it, producers moving away from ragga were making music "collectively labelled, for convenience, as drum and bass." Around that flag, the music split in several directions at once.

**Intelligent.** LTJ Bukem's Good Looking Records had been putting out lush, jazz-flavoured breakbeat since "Demon's Theme" in 1992, and Speed, the night he ran with Fabio at the Mars Bar from 1994, became the home of a smooth, atmospheric strand that the press loved to call "intelligent" — a label that implied the rest of the scene was something else, and everyone knew it. Jordan's crate leans into this side, from Photek's *Modus Operandi* to Spring Heel Jack, Paradox and Omni Trio, and much of it is beautiful. Much of it, by 1996, is no longer jungle.

**The two-step.** Most importantly, the drums changed. Jungle's signature was the chopped, rolling, unpredictable break. Drum & bass increasingly settled on a cleaner, more regimented "two-step" pattern — kick, snare, kick, snare — that left room for ever-heavier bass and made the music easier to mix. Alex Reece's "Pulp Fiction" (Metalheadz, 1995) is the record most often named as the turning point, and once you've heard it you can hear the before and after across the whole scene.

**Techstep.** At the other extreme, Ed Rush and Trace, working with engineer Nico at the No U-Turn label, coined "techstep": they stripped out the reggae and the warmth and replaced it with harsh, distorted bass descended from Kevin Saunderson's 1988 "Reese" track and sparse, punishing drums. Reynolds wrote that those records "took the explosive energy of hardcore and imploded it, transforming febrile hyperkinesis into molasses thick malaise." Ed Rush & Nico's *Torque* (1997) is in Jordan's playlist and is a magnificent record, and Ed Rush & Optical's *Wormhole* (Virus, November 1998) took the sound to its logical, cold conclusion.

**Mercury.** And the establishment finally gave up and handed over a prize. Roni Size and his Bristol collective Reprazent — DJ Krust, DJ Die, Dynamite MC, Onallee and live players including drummer Clive Deamer — won the 1997 Mercury Prize for *New Forms*, beating Radiohead and The Prodigy. Drum & bass was now album-of-the-year material in the eyes of the British music industry, which is either a triumph or a funeral depending on which club you were in.

None of these directions killed jungle. But together they redrew the map, and "jungle" went from the name of the whole territory to the name of one stubborn province — the one where the ragga, the Amen and the sound-system weight still lived.

## Part Nine: How to Tell Them Apart (A Field Guide)

Since Jordan's playlist mixes the two, here's the checklist I used to pick the ten.

**The drums.** Jungle: chopped breakbeats — usually the Amen or another funk break — edited into constantly shifting patterns full of ghost notes, rolls and fills, so that no two bars are quite the same. Drum & bass: the two-step, cleaner and more regular, often programmed rather than cut from a break.

**The bass.** Jungle: reggae and dub basslines, long and round and sub-heavy, often played as a melody at half the tempo of the drums, straight out of sound-system culture. Drum & bass: increasingly synthetic and modulated — the Reese, then the "neuro" basses of the 2000s — designed for impact rather than tune.

**The voices.** Jungle: ragga MCs, dancehall deejays, reggae singers, sound-system chants, film dialogue. Drum & bass: anything goes — soul divas, rappers, no vocals at all — and much less tied to Jamaica.

**The tempo.** Jungle: mostly in the 160s. Drum & bass: 170 and climbing.

**The calendar.** The classic jungle window is roughly 1993 to 1996. Later records can absolutely be jungle — Congo Natty never stopped — but after 1997 you have to listen rather than assume.

That's why some magnificent records from Jordan's crate aren't on the list. Photek's *Modus Operandi* and Ed Rush & Nico's *Torque* are drum & bass by every measure above. Adam F's "Circles" is a gorgeous liquid roller. And Everything But the Girl's Photek-remixed "Drum 'n' Bass" literally names itself out of contention, which I respect as a piece of self-awareness.

## Part Ten: The Afterlife

Jungle never died. It went back underground and waited for everyone else to get bored.

Through the late 1990s and 2000s, Congo Natty, DJ Hype's Ganja and Playaz crews and a scattering of white-label specialists kept the ragga flame lit. Breakcore producers on labels like Planet Mu took jungle's chopped breaks and pushed them into glorious chaos — Jordan's crate is full of Shitmat and Enduser, and of Remarc's Planet Mu reissues sitting right next to them.

Then came the revival. Congo Natty's *Jungle Revolution*, released on Big Dada in July 2013 and mixed with dub legend Adrian Sherwood, found an audience who'd been toddlers in 1994; *Ancestorz [Rootz of Jungle]* followed in 2022. Chase & Status, who'd grown up on all of this, made *RTRN II JUNGLE* in 2019 — that's where Jordan's "Original Nuttah 25" remix comes from — and Shy FX went back to his roots with *Raggamuffin SoundTape* the same year.

In the 2020s a new generation found the Amen as if it were brand new. Tim Reaper and Dwarde's Future Retro London label, the Dutch producer Coco Bryce, labels like 3AM Eternal and Deep Jungle, and SHERELLE — whose 2019 Boiler Room set went viral and who co-founded Hooversound — all make jungle with obvious love for the 1993–95 originals. In 2024 Nia Archives's debut album *Silence Is Loud* was shortlisted for the Mercury Prize, the first jungle record nominated in more than twenty-five years — which closes a loop that opened with *New Forms* rather neatly.

Jordan's own playlist captures the revival in miniature: the "Spaghetti Jungle" of Omen Breaks, which sounds like Ennio Morricone woke up in a Hackney tower block; 6BLOCC's "Cali Jungle" series, proof that the Amen travels well; and a whole shelf of modern ragga dubplates that would have sounded right at home on Kool FM in 1994.

## Coda: Seven Seconds, Again

The thing that still astonishes me about jungle is how much it did with how little. A borrowed seven-second drum break. A sampler with a few megabytes of memory. A bass sound from a reggae dubplate. An MC with a microphone and a great deal to say. A pirate transmitter on a rooftop in Hackney. That's all it took to build a culture, and the culture built a genre that spawned drum & bass, fed UK garage and grime, and still sounds more like the future than most of the future does.

Drum & bass took jungle's engine and built a sleeker, faster car around it. That's fine. A lot of brilliant records were made in that car, and some of them are in Jordan's crate. But if you want to hear the engine with the bonnet off, the oil smoking and the whole thing shaking itself apart on a sound system at four in the morning, you go back to these ten.
