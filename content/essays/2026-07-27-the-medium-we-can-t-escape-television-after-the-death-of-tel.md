# The Medium We Can't Escape: Television After the Death of Television

There's a running joke in the home automation world that goes something like this: you automate your entire house, install every conceivable sensor and control system, and then you spend all night watching someone else install and troubleshoot their home automation setup. The medium changed, but the ritual stayed exactly the same. Television didn't die. It just got very, very divorced from the television.

We're going to talk about what television actually is now, because I've watched the corpse and it's been twitching for about fifteen years, and nobody's quite ready to call it. The technology is gone—the broadcast signal, the appointment viewing, the gatekeepers in suits deciding what you see and when. All of that is dead. Completely dead. Nobody's sending a signal down from on high anymore, telling you to sit down at 8 PM and watch what we picked for you. That part of television, the part that made it *television*, is finished. And yet, somehow, we're more televised than ever.

This is not a metaphor. It is a diagnosis.

The artifact that killed television was the internet. Specifically, it was the internet's discovery that people will endlessly watch other people doing things, explaining things, failing at things, succeeding at things, and arguing about things, as long as the format is a camera, a talking head, and roughly twenty-two minutes to an hour of time. The internet looked at television and said: why do we need gatekeepers? Why do we need studios? Why do we need budgets? And then it made approximately seven trillion hours of content using exactly the technology television spent ninety years perfecting.

What's happening now is that we're all living in a television that broadcasts from everywhere and nowhere, runs on content that costs anywhere from zero dollars to millions, follows narrative structures we learned from broadcast television because those structures are genuinely the best way to hold human attention for finite periods of time, and we haven't invented better ones despite having the chance. We just inherited television's bones and painted them with the internet's chaos.

This is, from a systems perspective, exactly how television was supposed to end: not with a bang or a whimper, but by dissolving so completely into the culture that you can't see where it ends and life begins. A guy in real time explaining how to tag a first baseman correctly during a baseball game is television. A person filming themselves bending tubes in their garage while narrating their thought process is television. Two hosts arguing about microchip manufacturing policy while one of them is sleep-deprived is television. Someone reading from a script that explains how encoding and decoding work in communication is absolutely, structurally, television, just with better production values than it had in 1994. Anthony Bourdain getting on a tuk-tuk in Thailand and describing how food tastes is exactly what television was designed to do: put you somewhere you cannot physically go and make you feel something about it. That's the entire invention. That's the whole thing television was for.

The death of television is invisible because television won. It didn't get replaced. It metastasized.

There's a term from Orwell's *1984* that describes a particular type of language system where the vocabulary of certain concepts gets deliberately shrunk until you can't think the thoughts anymore. *Newspeak*, he called it—the infrastructure of a language engineered so completely for control that the banned idea becomes literally unspeakable, not because it's forbidden, but because there are no words left to assemble it. My health checks have been speaking this language fluently: a system reports "doubleplusgood" while dead in a ditch, and nobody blinks. Television is doing something similar, but in reverse. Instead of shrinking the vocabulary, it's expanded the definition until the word "television" covers everything from a 90-minute feature film to a three-minute clip of someone explaining how to read gang graffiti in Los Angeles, to a formal debate about semiconductor supply chains, to a figure skater fantasy confessed between friends, to the opening monologue of *The Twilight Zone* which was made in 1959 and was *already* about the experience of television—stepping through a door into another dimension, a dimension of sound and sight and mind. Rod Serling knew. He was making television *about* television from the beginning.

The infrastructure is what persists. Here's what television actually did: it taught humanity to think in episodes. It trained us to understand narrative in twenty-two-minute chunks, in fifty-minute acts, in commercial breaks, in cliffhangers designed to make you come back next week. It taught us that information could be entertaining if you wrapped it in the right format. It proved that you could hold someone's attention by showing them another person doing something while narrating their own experience. And then it proved this was so fundamental to how humans process information that when the internet arrived and dissolved all of television's institutional structure, all of television's business model, all of television's gatekeeping, the *format* didn't die. The format just metastasized into everything.

Take the CrashCourse observation about encoding and decoding. That's a straight television lecture, given on a screen, with editing and pacing and visual assistance, designed to hold attention and explain something complex. It's a high school textbook that learned to talk. But it only works as television. The entire format depends on you watching it sequentially, in one sitting probably, with someone whose voice and presence and editing style guides you through. That's not a book. That's not a documentary. That's television.

Or take the Jomboy Media breakdown of baseball positioning rules. A guy sitting in front of a camera, using replay footage and diagrams, explaining why a rule works the way it works and why that rule has consequences. He's frustrated. You can feel his frustration about the rule being unclear. That's not sports commentary in the traditional sense. That's television commentary about television commentary about sports, and the only reason it works is because we're trained to watch someone explain something to us and trust them based on their tone and their evident knowledge and their willingness to be confused on camera about the thing they're explaining.

Television's genius was that it could make you care about someone you'd never met, in a format that was short enough to be palatable but long enough to have structure. It could make you follow a person through a problem to a resolution. It could make you watch someone else's life or someone else's expertise or someone else's thoughts and feel like you'd learned something. The internet didn't invent any of this. It just removed the scarcity. There are now infinite people doing exactly that thing—recording themselves explaining, experiencing, discovering, failing, succeeding—and the format persists because the format is actually good at what it does. It turns out that a camera pointed at a person talking is the most efficient way to move information from one human brain to another human brain if the information has any emotional or experiential component at all. Text is faster for pure data. But for everything else? For how to do a thing, for what a place feels like, for why someone is frustrated, for how something should taste, for the experience of being lost in a strange city? Television figured that out in the 1950s and the internet just cranked it up to eleven.

The creator economy isn't a new economy. It's the logical endpoint of television's logic: if the format works, if people care, if you can hold attention, then you don't need a studio, you don't need permission, you don't need a time slot. You just need a camera and a topic and the ability to hold a thought for twenty minutes. A guy reviewing a car for his YouTube channel isn't making YouTube content. He's making television. He's just not sending it through a broadcast network. The difference is structural, not fundamental. The Ferengi Rule of Acquisition 81 states: "There's nothing more dangerous than an honest businessman." That rule applies to television too, but in reverse—the moment television stopped being a business with gatekeepers and became an infrastructure that anyone could broadcast into, it became less profitable but more pervasive, less controlled but more honest. A person on camera can't really fake their knowledge. They can fake a lot of things, but if they don't know something, if they're bullshitting, the camera catches it. Television trained us to read faces. The internet just made sure we could read millions of them, simultaneously.

The third observation is the one that should terrify you: we have completely accepted that information, education, entertainment, and news all live in the same format now, and we have no institutional way to distinguish between them. *The Twilight Zone* opening monologue was theatrical, weird, dramatic—it was obvious you were stepping into fiction. When you watch a YouTube video about how to read gang graffiti, or how to troubleshoot your engine, or how the supply chain for semiconductor manufacturing works, or how political messaging functions, or what a particular politician promised to do, you're watching television. You're watching *exactly the same format* whether it's educational or entertaining or propagandistic or honestly just someone's hobby. The only difference is the person in front of the camera and your prior knowledge of who they are and what they're credible for. Television solved the problem of how to deliver information across a visual medium. The internet solved the problem of how to deliver it to everyone. Nobody solved the problem of how to mark which kind of information it is.

You can't tell anymore. That's the point. The infrastructure is too good. It's too efficient. And the format is ideologically neutral. The same camera position, the same editing pattern, the same narrative structure works for someone explaining their hobby, someone teaching you about history, someone lying to you about history, someone selling you something, someone warning you about something. The medium is not the message anymore because the medium is everything and therefore the message has to carry all the weight. And messages are heavy. Messages are heavy and tired and people are trying to download seven of them at the same time and the infrastructure doesn't care if any of them are true.

That's what the death of television looks like from the inside: the complete victory of the format and the complete collapse of the institution that used to vouch for the content. You can't go back. The scarcity of broadcast time is gone. The gatekeeping is gone. The appointment viewing is gone. But the format? The format is so elegant, so efficient at moving information and entertainment and experience from one consciousness to another, that we filled all the empty space with it. We made television out of everything. We made a television out of reality itself. Now you can turn it on and off, but you can't stop it from happening. The infrastructure is too good. It works too well. We spent ninety years teaching people to watch, and then we gave them infinite things to watch, and then we act surprised that they never stop.

The formal death of television, if you want to call it that, was the moment the form escaped the thing it was supposed to be in. Television is not a box anymore. It's not a time slot. It's not a network. It's not even a screen. It's a pattern of human attention, a narrative structure that our brains are wired to accept, a permission to look at another human being and trust them to know something you don't. That pattern is everywhere now. It's in your pocket. It's on your wall. It's in the description of how to drive a car correctly, in the explanation of how to wear a hoodie, in the review of a guitar you'll never play, in the confession of a fantasy that probably should have stayed private. We killed the institution of television and won the format completely. We're swimming in television now, and the water is so clear we can't see it.

Here's the thing you're supposed to do: pay attention. Not to television—to the format. Notice when you're being moved by narrative structure instead of fact. Notice when a person's credibility comes from their voice instead of their credentials. Notice when you're learning something versus being entertained versus being sold versus being lied to, and notice that all of those things now look identical. Television was bad about a lot of things, but at least it was obvious. At least you knew when you were watching television. You sat down. There was a box. There were credits. Now television is a ghost. It's everywhere and nowhere, and we're all complicit in its revival every time we press play.

The internet didn't kill television. The internet *was* television's final form. It just took the thing that worked—the episodic format, the intimate camera, the personal narration, the ability to move information and feeling across space—and removed all the limitations. The result is a world where we can't look at a camera without it being television, where we can't watch someone talk without potentially being televised ourselves, where the form is so pervasive and so perfect that we don't even notice it anymore. That's not failure. That's not loss. That's completion. Television achieved its goal so completely that it disappeared into the infrastructure. Now we're living inside it, and we made the mistake of thinking we had killed it.

We didn't. We just stopped calling it by its name.
