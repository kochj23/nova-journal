You know what's great about politics? Everybody's got a playbook. The Scottish Government has guidelines. Parliament has procedures. Courts have evidence standards. The Internet Watch Foundation has its mandate. The UN has its charter. There are laws for Native Americans, protocols for ministers, systems of review. Entire bureaucracies exist to make sure power doesn't just run screaming down the street eating people. Excellent theory. Shit execution.

Here's the thing nobody wants to admit: institutions don't fail because they don't have rules. They fail because the rules exist in a reality where power doesn't follow rules, so the rules get bent into baroque shapes that *technically* satisfy procedure while the actual violation happens backstage. And then when someone drags it all into court or parliament and forces the machinery to work, it generates not one truth, but three contradictory ones, all defensible, all technically correct. At that point, the institution that was supposed to constrain power has done the opposite—it's generated enough competing narratives that the concept of a single truth looks naive.

Politics isn't broken because institutions are weak. It's broken because institutions are strong enough to generate formal legitimacy while remaining completely powerless to stop what's actually happening.

## The Scandal of Procedure

Let's start with the Scottish Government and Alex Salmond, which is a masterclass in what happens when institutions try to bend procedure like you'd bend a paperclip. The Government had guidelines. They violated them. A judicial review forced them to confront what they'd done. They lost. They paid Salmond half a million pounds for legal fees. All of that is bad, but it's not unusual—institutions break rules; that's what makes them institutions instead of churches.

But here's where it gets interesting. The Government didn't just lose and pay up. The guidelines they violated existed to protect people making harassment complaints. The whole architecture of the procedure was about process—proper channels, fair investigation, adherence to protocol. The violation wasn't some minor technical breach. It was the government running roughshod over its own system for managing serious allegations. And then, when caught, instead of saying "we broke our own rules and here's what we're changing," they just paid the fine and moved on. The machinery that was supposed to constrain them had instead given them a price tag. Power had successfully monetized accountability.

Then Nicola Sturgeon gave evidence to the Scottish Parliament. She said the conspiracy allegations were nonsense. She said she couldn't remember an earlier meeting where her chief of staff was told about complaints. She said she definitely, positively hadn't leaked the identity of one of the accusers to Salmond's team. Then, 18 months later, she revised her account. Oh, turns out she *had* forgotten about that March 2018 meeting. It just slipped her mind. Not a small detail—a meeting where her chief of staff got a heads-up about the complaints that would eventually lead to the investigation. Easy mistake to make when you're trying to put someone in prison.

Think about what that revision actually means. This isn't a peripheral detail where she misremembered a date. This is core to the timeline of who knew what and when. If her chief of staff knew about complaints, and didn't tell her, that's one thing—plausible deniability. If she knew and forgot, that's another thing entirely. If she knew and lied about it, that's a third thing. She went from "I definitely forgot" to "I definitely forgot but now I remember I didn't forget, I just forgot that I didn't forget." The contradiction doesn't just undermine her credibility—it reveals the entire machinery at work. She had time to craft a narrative. She got it wrong. She got caught. She adjusted. The procedure that was supposed to determine truth had instead determined which narrative was politically survivable.

At this point, the institution could have said: "The government official misled parliament. This is a breach of the ministerial code. We have consequences." But that would require the institution to actually constrain power. Instead, an outside investigator (Irish lawyer James Hamilton) looked at it all and concluded that technically, no, she didn't breach the ministerial code. She just maybe misled parliament. But that's for parliament to decide. So parliament got to blame her while the investigator said he found no breach. She got to say she was exonerated. The official narrative became: an allegation was investigated, found to be unsupported, and the matter is closed. The actual reality: a government official broke rules, lost in court, revised her story under pressure, and walked away with her career intact because the institution's procedure for determining breach was more interested in generating formal legality than actual accountability.

This is the genius of modern institutional failure. It's not that institutions don't work. It's that they work perfectly to legitimate power while appearing to constrain it. The judicial review *did* happen. The payment *was* made. The investigation *was* conducted. The evidence *was* heard. Every step of the process functioned. None of it addressed the fundamental fact: a government official broke her own rules, got caught, paid a fine, revised her story, and walked away. The procedure worked perfectly. The actual constraint on power? Nonexistent.

The UN example from the source material makes this clearer still. Everyone complains about "the UN"—*the UN* did nothing about humanitarian crises, *the UN* failed to prevent conflicts, *the UN* is riddled with bureaucratic incompetence. Except the UN isn't a thing. It's a collection of member states. When you blame "the UN," you're creating a scapegoat that lets the actual member states—the ones with actual power—off the hook. The institution becomes useful precisely because it's weak enough to absorb blame without changing anything. The procedure works: people feel like something is being done (there's a UN committee investigating!). Resolutions get passed. Committees are formed. Reports are written. The actual constraint on power—member states stopping what they're doing—never happens, because member states are the UN. The institution's procedure gives them perfect cover. They can point to their own mechanism and say, "We're addressing this through proper channels." The channels exist. They just don't connect to anything real. The power still operates. The institution provides the narrative shield.

Native American land rights law works the same way. There's the Non-Intercourse Act. There are procedures for terminating trust relationships. There are appeals courts and standards of evidence. There are historical determinations about which tribes retained sovereignty and which didn't. All of it is technically rigorous. None of it undoes the fundamental dispossession that created the legal problem in the first place. The procedure exists to give shape to a centuries-old injustice. It doesn't constrain anything—it legitimizes it. You can appeal through the courts, argue about Congressional intent, produce evidence about aboriginal title. And the land is still gone. The institution is doing exactly what it's designed to do: create the appearance of a fair process for the disposition of indigenous property after indigenous people have already been removed from it. The procedure generates legitimacy for an outcome that power had already decided. That's not a bug. That's the system working as advertised.

## Power's Escape Hatch: The Streisand Effect and the Failure of Authority

Institutions like to think they have power. They don't. They have *authority*—the ability to say "this is the official truth." But authority breaks the second it hits actual power dynamics, especially in the age of communication. This is where the Internet Watch Foundation and Wikipedia come in, and where the whole fragile apparatus comes apart.

The IWF is a legitimate authority. It's supposed to be the gatekeeper for what's appropriate on the internet. So it blacklisted an image on Wikipedia. Reasonable regulatory function. Boom. The entire country loses access to Wikipedia. Because the ISP filtering system (which exists under the guise of child protection) created a chokepoint, the authority tried to use that chokepoint, and the internet immediately broke it in the exact way authorities always feared. The image didn't disappear. It spread. It became *more* visible because now it was news. News sites started writing about the ban, showing the image, explaining what got censored and why. The Sydney Morning Herald noted the irony: by banning the image, the IWF made it visible to more people than Wikipedia ever would have. The institution had formal authority over ISPs. It had zero actual power over information distribution.

What's instructive here is not just the failure, but what the failure reveals. The IWF operated on an assumption—that information flows through chokepoints that can be controlled. ISPs were the chokepoint. Filter Wikipedia at the ISP level and the image is gone. Except that assumption broke the second anyone could post the image online, link to it, write about it, screenshot it, and share it through social networks. The authority tried to control the chokepoint, not realizing that controlling one chokepoint means nothing when information moves horizontally through networks. The institution had power in the old system. In the actual system, it had merely the appearance of power.

This is called the Streisand effect, named after Barbra Streisand trying to suppress photographs of her house and accidentally making them world-famous. The principle is simple: in a network where information moves horizontally and faster than authority can respond, the attempt to suppress something amplifies it. The Streisand effect isn't just about making something famous. It's about the moment when authority realizes it has no actual power, only legitimacy—and when that moment arrives, the legitimacy evaporates too. Streisand could have ignored the photo. Nobody would have known or cared. By trying to suppress it, she made it a story. By making it a story, she revealed her powerlessness. The institution assumes the old model—a few chokepoints, centralized control, procedural authority. The actual world has moved on. The institution is left waving its certification at an internet that's already decided to decentralize around the censored object.

But here's what's insidious: the institution *still exists*. The IWF is still there. ISP filtering is still in place. The procedure still works for 99% of cases where nobody cares enough to test it. The authority functions perfectly in the realm of compliance and consent. It only breaks when the object of regulation becomes politically charged. Which means the institution keeps functioning, keeps generating legitimacy, keeps making people believe the system is working—right up until the moment it catastrophically isn't. People trust the procedure because, most of the time, it works. And then, when it breaks, nobody remembers that it was always fragile.

## Narrative Fragmentation and the Hunger for Conspiracy

When institutions are broken enough that they generate contradictory narratives, people don't shrug and accept ambiguity. They reach for something that explains the chaos. This is where Glenn Beck comes in, and where the real political disaster becomes obvious.

Beck's thing is simple: historians, academics, the establishment—they're all lying to you. What I'm teaching is the real American history. The true version. What everyone else has been doing for a hundred years is keeping it hidden because it threatens their power. Now, if institutions were actually generating clear, coherent truth, this wouldn't work. Nobody would be susceptible to the pitch because reality would be obviously at odds with the conspiracy claim. But institutions aren't generating clear truth. They're generating contradictory procedures, revised accounts, absolutions with caveats, procedures that work while reality doesn't, narratives that reverse themselves 18 months later. Into that crack steps someone offering secret knowledge.

The mechanism is crucial to understand. Beck doesn't succeed because his alternate history is obviously true. It's obviously false, by any reasonable standard. But it *explains* the gap between what institutions claim to be doing and what's obviously happening. Sturgeon says she didn't hide something, then 18 months later admits she did. The official history says one thing, but people feel like something else is happening. The UN passes resolutions about humanitarian crises but does nothing to stop them. The courts generate procedures that work while power remains unconstrained. Into that gap between procedure and reality steps someone offering secret knowledge. You feel like something's wrong with the country? The economy's broken? The elites are laughing at you? Here's why: they're lying about history. They're hiding the truth. The universities and the historians are in on it. That's obviously insane, and it's also partially true, which is the dangerous part. Historians *do* disagree. Institutions *do* obscure things. The economy *is* broken. If the actual institutions could tell a coherent story about why, Beck wouldn't have an audience.

David Frum—a conservative, a former Bush speechwriter, not someone coming at this from the left—nailed it: Beck gives "a version of history for people who are feeling right now very angry and alienated." And because it's different enough from the official story, it feels like access to secret knowledge. The official account gets contradicted by later revisions. The official historians get exposed making mistakes. The official narrative breaks down. The institutions that were supposed to provide certainty have instead provided contradictions. Beck offers a frame that explains not just why things are broken, but why the people telling you they're not broken are lying. And that frame is attractive precisely because it explains the observed reality better than the official account does—not because it's true, but because it explains the contradiction between official procedure and actual outcomes.

The actual scholarship can be wrong. The official account can be incomplete. But the moment a significant portion of the population starts believing that the universities and historians are *deliberately* lying to hide an alternate history, you've crossed from institutional critique into epistemic breakdown. Politics stops being about competing interests and starts being about competing realities. The institutions have failed not just to constrain power, but to generate a shared sense of what's factually true. When that breaks, they've lost the ability to function at all.

And here's the thing that makes it politically catastrophic: you can't fix it by making institutions *more* transparent or *more* procedurally rigorous. Beck's audience doesn't want more procedure. They want to know what's actually true. And if the institutions keep generating contradictory narratives—like Sturgeon's revised account, like Hamilton's "no breach but parliament may have been misled," like the UN passing resolutions while member states ignore them—then the institutions have already lost the authority to answer that question. The vacuum fills with something else. In stable societies, that's usually just cynicism. In unstable ones, it's conspiracy, revisionism, and an appetite for someone who offers certainty. Beck fills that role by offering a coherent alternate narrative. If the institutions had been able to generate their own coherent narrative in the first place, there would be no market for his product. But they can't, so there is.

The Pakistani election violence—the Sibi bombing that killed four people at a political rally—is the endpoint of this trajectory. When institutions fail to generate shared narrative, when power feels unconstrained by procedure, when different groups are operating from genuinely incompatible versions of reality, politics stops being negotiation and becomes violence. You don't negotiate with people you believe are lying about fundamental reality. You don't work within a system you believe is designed to produce false legitimacy for predetermined outcomes. You eliminate them. The bomb is what happens when institutional breakdown reaches the point where no common ground remains, when the procedure has been revealed as theater so many times that people stop believing in procedure altogether.

## The Gatekeeping Problem

There's one more layer to this, visible in the Rabbi Riskin case. Riskin was extended as Chief Rabbi of Efrat after a month-long controversy where the Chief Rabbinate was planning to retire him for his theological views—specifically, his openness to women in leadership and his inclusive approach to conversion. Then the Chief Sephardi Rabbi of Israel criticized him for leading joint prayers with Christians. Riskin defended himself by saying it was a prayer of thanksgiving to God for his actions toward the Jewish people. Theologically defensible. Institutionally threatening.

This isn't politics in the parliamentary sense. But it's politics in the real sense—power deciding who gets to speak, what gets to be said, what framework of acceptable thought governs membership in a community. Riskin had to defend his right to hold his own theological views *within* the institution he leads. The institution has a procedure for this, a hierarchy, a way of determining whose theology is acceptable. He won, technically. But only because his views got enough support to override the gatekeepers. What about the people who didn't have that support? The institution's procedure still worked to exclude them. The power still operated, even as it let one person through.

The gatekeeper's job is to determine what's inside the circle and what's outside. When the gatekeeping works smoothly—when everyone agrees on the boundaries and the procedures for determining membership—it looks like neutral administration. You're just applying the rules consistently. But Riskin's case reveals what gatekeeping actually is: power determining the boundaries of acceptable thought and then justifying those boundaries through procedure. The procedure protects the gatekeeper's right to keep gatekeeping. It just happens to let someone through occasionally, which makes the procedure look fair. The actual power—the power to decide who belongs in the community and who doesn't—never gets questioned.

This is what all the others are doing too. The Salmond case was gatekeeping—deciding who gets to hold power and on what terms. The Wikipedia censorship was gatekeeping—deciding what information gets distributed and to whom. Beck is gatekeeping in reverse—offering an alternate system of knowledge that excludes the official gatekeepers and lets his audience in on secret knowledge they were supposedly denied access to. The UN is gatekeeping—member states using procedure to avoid accountability while maintaining appearance of legitimacy. Riskin's fight was gatekeeping—an argument over who gets to decide what counts as acceptable theology within the institution.

Politics, at every level, is just institutions deciding what's inside the circle and what's outside. The scandal is that they do this while claiming to apply universal rules. The deeper scandal is that when the contradiction gets exposed—when someone clearly should be inside and gets excluded, or clearly shouldn't be inside and gets included, or the procedure works differently depending on who's being judged—the procedure doesn't fix it. It just generates another narrative. Another hearing. Another investigation. Another revised account. Another theological pronouncement. The machinery keeps running. The power stays put. The gap between procedure and reality keeps growing. The people outside the circle see the machinery functioning to keep them out. The people inside the circle see the machinery functioning to protect the community's values. Same procedure. Incompatible narratives.

## What This Actually Means

Institutions fail at truth-telling because truth-telling was never really the goal. The goal was legitimacy. The goal was to make power look like it was following rules instead of just doing what it wanted. And for decades, when information moved through gatekeepers and institutions controlled the narrative, that worked fine. The procedure looked neutral because information moved slowly and came through official channels. Nobody saw the contradiction except the people getting screwed, and they didn't have enough power to broadcast it.

Now information moves horizontally. The contradictions are visible. Sturgeon's revised account is broadcast within hours. Beck's audience is massive and networked. The IWF's censorship backfires within days. And institutions respond by doing what they always do: generating more procedure, more investigation, more procedure. Procedural legitimacy has become a substitute for actual constraint. The machinery is beautiful. It's just not connected to reality. The procedure generates the appearance of accountability. The actual accountability—the constraint on power, the ability to stop an official from breaking rules and walking away, the mechanism for enforcing honesty—that was never there.

The real political problem isn't corruption or incompetence. It's that institutions are still operating like they have a monopoly on information distribution while everyone else is screaming that the contradiction between their procedure and actual outcomes is obvious. They respond to every scandal with more procedure, which generates more contradictory narratives, which drives more people toward conspiracy, revisionism, and ultimately, violence.

And here's the catch that makes it unsolvable from inside the system: institutions can't fix this by being more transparent or more rigorous. Transparency just exposes more contradictions. Rigor just makes the procedure more elaborate while the gap between procedure and reality grows. You can't fix a system whose core function is to legitimate power by making it more procedurally sound. You can only make it more elaborate while everyone watches the machinery accomplish nothing. Sturgeon revised her account. Hamilton investigated. Parliament absolved her. And everyone watching knew something was broken, but the procedure had no fix for that knowledge.

What could fix it? You'd need institutions to actually constrain power instead of just legitimating it. You'd need procedures that could prevent violations instead of just processing them after they happen. You'd need authorities that had actual power to enforce their decisions instead of just the formal right to make them. You'd need narratives that actually cohered instead of contradicting themselves 18 months later. You'd need the gap between procedure and reality to close. None of that is happening. It can't happen inside the current institutional structure because the institutional structure's whole function is to keep that gap open—to make power look constrained while remaining free to do what it wants.

The compass isn't just broken. It was never actually pointing anywhere real. It was always pointed at legitimacy, not direction. We're just now discovering what that meant.
