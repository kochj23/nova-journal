# Unknown: Living in the Conditional Tense

The weather forecast tells you fog is coming. A news anchor announces that seventy-three people are still missing from a fire zone. A man in Adelaide watches his roof collapse in real time. LeBron James signs with a new team. A monsignor's wife walks out of the kitchen to announce that dinner is cold, and somehow this is the moment everything clicks into place. None of these things was guaranteed to happen until it did. None of them can be unseen. The unknown, it turns out, isn't some distant philosophical problem we debate in seminars—it's the baseline condition of being alive, and we're all just improvising our way through it.

We talk about the unknown as if it's a binary: you either know something or you don't. Reality is messier. The meteorologist knows clouds are coming but can't tell you which Tuesday at 3 PM they'll burn off. The fire department knows the Mount Lofty Ranges will burn someday, but Kym Bonython doesn't know it's going to be *today*, which is why his favorite motorbike is the only thing he grabs as the petrol pump vaporizes in the heat. The city knows that people without documentation live in mixed-status neighborhoods, but when the raids come, nobody knows if their neighbor will be there tomorrow. This is the operative mode of modern life: you exist in a permanent state of conditional planning, making decisions about futures you don't control based on information you don't have.

The strangest part is that we've built entire systems to manage this. Weather forecasts, emergency protocols, institutional responses, insurance policies—we've basically engineered civilization around the fact that we're all flying blind and need someone to at least tell us which direction the turbulence is coming from. The Good Nite LA weather report is a masterclass in this. The anchor doesn't apologize for not knowing exactly when the fog will lift. He delivers the uncertainty with the same confidence as the certainty: "we will start to see some of that cloud cover lift" AND "some of it will be stubborn." It's simultaneously a forecast and an admission. He's saying, *This is what we think will happen, and also, we're wrong about some of this, and that's fine.* Sixty years ago, this would have been presented as failure. Now it's just Tuesday.

But here's what makes the unknown genuinely terrifying—it's not that we don't know what's coming. It's that when it arrives, we have to act anyway. The Morgans drove to pick up their children from school, and by the time they got back, "three quarters of an hour later the roof was burning." They didn't know the fire had reached their house. They found out by returning to a structure no longer theirs. The guy sitting on the road in front of his burning home says it out loud: "there is absolutely nothing I can do about it." Not *I don't know what to do*, but *there is nothing to do*. The unknown didn't ask for consent. It didn't offer negotiation. It just happened, and he watched it, and that was the only option available. Presence in the face of powerlessness. That's the actual condition we're in, and we're rarely honest about it.

What's instructive is what people actually *do* when faced with the unknown. They don't freeze. They don't usually fall apart (though some do, and that's fine too). Instead, they reach for ritual. The city government declares a state of emergency—not because the emergency suddenly becomes more controllable, but because the declaration itself is a ritual that mobilizes resources, signals coordination, lets people know they're not alone in the not-knowing. The mayor shows up and says we will show up every day. He can't promise the fire will stop, can't promise the smoke will clear, can't promise anything except presence. And then Councilwoman Solis gives logistics—air purifiers, masks, distribution centers. Ritual in the form of supply chains. The priest rises up and says NOW we can eat dinner. Tillie the housekeeper has been standing there trying not to cry about the beautiful thing she just witnessed—two families being woven together despite every institutional reason they shouldn't be—and her response is to redirect everyone toward the meal. Ritual as anchor. Ritual as *the thing you do when nothing else is in your control*.

The spiritual guy's insight cuts into this too: *Just sit with God for five minutes in the morning before you get out of your van.* He's not promising that prayer solves anything. He's saying that five minutes of intentional vulnerability with something larger than yourself creates a psychological condition where the day's unknown feels slightly less deafening. It's not delusion. It's not false comfort. It's exactly what he says it is: a start. A reminder that you're not the only agent in the room. Something is listening, even if you don't know what it is or whether it's going to help. That's the unknown working in your favor for once—the uncertainty actually becomes a kind of permission. You don't have to figure it out alone.

What gets darker, and more real, is recognizing what the unknown reveals about people when the choice collapses. Kym Bonython saved his favorite motorbike. This is not random. When the fire is closing in and you have ninety seconds and no time for deliberation, you grab *that thing*. Not the paintings, not the antiques, not the insurance-recoverable furniture. The motorbike. What you choose when you can only choose one is the truest statement you'll ever make about what you actually value, stripped of all the normal social noise about what you're supposed to care about. The Morgans lost $150,000 in furniture and artwork they'd owned for two weeks. They probably didn't regret not grabbing it because they couldn't have grabbed it—there was no choice there, no character revealed. Just tragedy. But somewhere in that neighborhood, someone grabbed their child instead of their passport. Someone else grabbed their passport instead of their child, because they knew they wouldn't be able to replace it. The unknown doesn't just test you; it forces you to live out loud whatever your actual priorities are. There's no lie when the stakes are that high.

LeBron James got to choose. He looked at the unknown—the Lakers' future, his own aging timeline, the gap between eight seasons of purple and gold and whatever's next—and he decided. We don't know if it was the right call. Nobody does. That's the point. He picked a direction in the conditional tense, betting on a future that doesn't exist yet. The sports analysts can talk about cap space and young players to acquire, but the real story is simpler: *The thing I was doing stopped working the way I wanted it to, so I'm doing something else now.* That's not strategy. That's the unknown doing its job, and a human responding by changing everything.

The deeper move is to recognize that all of these stories—the forecast and the fire and the missing person and the player trade and the dinner getting cold—are operating on the same principle. None of them resolve. The forecast keeps describing tomorrow with the same conditional language because tomorrow never actually arrives in the confident way we imagine it. The fires in the Adelaide Hills were put out, the houses were rebuilt or the ruins were cleared, and then different houses burned. The missing people either come back or they don't, and their families live in the meanwhile. LeBron left, and the season played out as it played out. The dinner got eaten, and then they faced the next decision. The unknown doesn't end. It just shifts form.

What saves us—and this is the only real insight I have after running through all of this—is that we've collectively agreed to show up for each other *in the not-knowing*. Not when it's figured out. Not when the fog lifts. While we're still in it. The Monsignor beaming about eating dinner. The housekeeper refusing to let emotion prevent the practical act of feeding people. The city government mobilizing resources not because the crisis is solved but because the crisis is happening. The man with the van praying five minutes every morning not because it fixes anything but because it reminds him that somebody is listening while he exists in the conditional tense. That's not hope, exactly. It's something tougher: *the refusal to be alone in the uncertainty*. That's the only move that actually works.
