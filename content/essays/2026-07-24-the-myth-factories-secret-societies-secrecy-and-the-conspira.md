# The Myth Factories: Secret Societies, Secrecy, and the Conspiracy Theories We Deserve

Here's a depressing thought: the actual secret societies are way fucking boring compared to the ones we've invented. The Bilderberg Group gathers once a year in a Swiss hotel to talk about economic policy and global governance. Freemasonry maintains lodges and rituals that date back to the 14th century. Both are real. Both involve powerful people. And both are, in practice, significantly less interesting than the conspiracy theories that have metastasized around them. What we've built in the gap between what these organizations actually do and what we *believe* they do is a fascinating monument to how power works when nobody's watching—not because the organizations are running the world, but because we're terrified they might be, and that terror has become more influential than the truth ever could be.

The scandal is this: secret societies exist partly because secrecy works, and partly because we've decided to make them work harder for it by filling in all the blanks with fiction. The real power of these organizations isn't what they do in the dark. It's that the dark exists at all, and we've collectively agreed to populate it with our deepest fears about who runs the world and why.

## The Bilderberg Group: Networking in Pinstripes

Let's start with something we actually know exists and can name people in: the Bilderberg Group, which has held annual meetings since the 1950s with rotating leadership. Peter Carington chaired it from 1990 to 1998. Étienne Davignon took over from 1999 to 2011. Henri de Castries ran it from 2012 to 2019. These are real names, real years, real résumés. Carington was Secretary of State for Foreign and Commonwealth Affairs in the UK. Davignon was a European Commissioner. De Castries ran AXA, one of the world's largest insurance companies. If you're watching from the outside, this looks potentially sinister: the elites are literally *gathering.*

But here's the problem with the conspiracy theory: the thing that's actually fucked up about Bilderberg doesn't require a shadow government to explain it. The actual scandal is completely mundane. Bilderberg brings together CEOs, government officials, and policymakers for closed-door meetings specifically designed to ensure "strict privacy and secrecy." That's the phrase in the source material—not my paraphrase, their own description. Corporate lobbyists meet with senior policymakers. In 2015, they even sat down with figures from Transparency International, the anti-corruption watchdog. And nobody gets to watch. Nobody gets to know what was said. Nobody gets to ask them what deal was struck.

That's not a conspiracy theory. That's just *how things work*, and if it horrifies you, that's the appropriate response. The Bilderberg Group doesn't need to be secretly controlling the world because it's *openly* a mechanism for powerful people to negotiate out of public view. The actual problem is accountability. Not hidden puppet-masters; just unaccountable wealth making decisions that ripple through global policy without anybody's permission.

But we don't want a boring explanation. Boring doesn't scare us enough. So the left started saying Bilderberg props up capitalist domination. The right started saying it's preparing some kind of authoritarian world order. Both groups, the source material tells us, "often disagree about the exact nature of the group's intentions and use different sources and levels of evidentiary rigor." Translated: some people use facts, and some people are just making shit up, and both believe they're doing rigorous analysis.

## Freemasonry: Four Centuries of Productive Secrecy

Freemasonry is older, weirder, and somehow more defensible than Bilderberg, which is saying something. The fraternal organization traces back reliably to at least the 14th century, possibly the stonemasons' guilds before that. Modern Freemasonry emerged in the 1600s and 1700s. The organization spread across Europe and North America, and by the American Revolution, it was full of heavyweight names: George Washington, Benjamin Franklin, Paul Revere, James Otis. These men were literally reshaping nations, and a lot of them were Masons.

Here's where the conspiracy theorists make their move: *ergo*, Freemasonry was secretly controlling the American Revolution. The evidence? Powerful men were Masons, and powerful things happened. Case closed, let's add it to the list of Masonic conspiracies alongside the planning of Washington, D.C. (which, sure, some Masons were involved in) and basically every significant event between 1776 and yesterday.

But the historian Robert Roswell Palmer pointed out something inconvenient: lodges operated *separately*, and Masons didn't act together as a political bloc. Individual Masons did individual things, which is what individual powerful people tend to do. Benjamin Franklin supported independence; so did a thousand other people who weren't Masons. George Washington was a Mason and also became president, which is approximately the job Masons would want one of their members to have, but that's not proof of orchestration—that's just how nepotism works.

The actual interesting thing about Freemasonry isn't that it secretly controlled history. It's that the organization created a horizontal network of powerful men across class and national boundaries at a time when those networks didn't exist anywhere else. Masons met as equals—a concept radical enough in the 18th century to be genuinely revolutionary. The shared rituals, the degrees of initiation, the mysterious language and symbols—these created trust and continuity in a way nothing else could. That's real power. Not because Masons were directing events from the shadows, but because they were creating the social infrastructure that let powerful people cooperate when they otherwise wouldn't have.

But again, the actual explanation is boring: secret societies work because humans are tribalistic and secret-keeping builds loyalty. The mythological explanation is better: they work because they're *controlling everything*, pulling strings, deciding history. We prefer the conspiracy to the sociology.

## The Conspiracy Theory as Culture

Here's the truly interesting observation: conspiracy theories about secret societies have become more historically significant than the actual organizations they're meant to describe. The Dulles Plan—the alleged CIA conspiracy to morally corrupt the Soviet Union—is a Russian propaganda theory with no serious evidentiary support and minimal grounding in declassified history. The Harvard Project was a real 1950s research program studying Soviet society, funded by the USAF. The fact that some Russians believe it was cover for destroying their culture tells us almost nothing about what the project actually was and everything about what Russians feared the West was doing. The theory is better (scarier, more explanatory) than the reality.

This is the real architecture of belief: when an organization is genuinely secret, we can't disprove our theories about it. The absence of evidence becomes evidence of how well they're hiding. The more Freemasons insist they're just a fraternal club for networking and mutual aid, the more obviously they're *lying*—they have to be lying, because what else would require such secrecy? The Bilderberg Group's commitment to privacy becomes proof of something to hide. And when conspiracy theorists disagree wildly with each other about what's happening, that's not weakness in the theory—that's just different people successfully detecting different layers of the conspiracy.

The psychological architecture is airtight. It's unfalsifiable by design. And that design is half-intentional on the part of the organizations (secrecy creates mystique, mystique creates legend, legend creates influence without accountability) and half-accidental (humans will create explanations for anything we don't understand).

What conspiracy theories actually reveal is that we've outsourced our understanding of power to fiction. We don't know how global governance actually works—it's too complex, too distributed, too boring. So we collapse it into a story: there's a group, they meet in secret, they decide things. It's *way* more parsimonious than reality. And the fact that the story is probably false doesn't make it less powerful. It makes it more powerful, because now it's a story we believe *despite* the lack of evidence, and that belief is self-reinforcing.

## The Conclusion: What We're Actually Afraid Of

The real scandal of secret societies isn't that they exist. It's that we've built a civilization where we need them to exist in order to make sense of power. If the world were transparently governed, if powerful people made their decisions in public and showed their work, we wouldn't need Bilderberg theories or Masonic plots. We'd just see the game and complain about the rules.

But we don't see the game, so we invented a better story. The story has more drama. It has villains. It has explanatory power. It tells us that the reason things are unfair is because *somebody is making them unfair on purpose*, and that's weirdly comforting. Because if it's on purpose, it's fixable. You find the secret society, you expose the conspiracy, you solve the problem.

Reality is messier. Power is less coordinated and more systemic. The actual decisions that shape the world come from a thousand overlapping interests, most of them boring, most of them legal, most of them just wealthy people protecting wealth because that's what wealthy people do. There's no secret meeting where it's decided. There's just the existing order, perpetuating itself, and people at every level working to maintain their piece of it.

That explanation never sells as well as Bilderberg running the world. So here's the action: next time you encounter a conspiracy theory about secret societies, ask one question: what problem does this theory solve that reality doesn't? The answer will tell you what we're actually afraid of. And fear, unlike Freemasonry, is never secret—it's the most visible thing we have.
