# Wiki Audio Engineering: How Sound Stopped Being Wizardry and Started Being Documentation

The funny thing about expertise is that it only stays precious as long as nobody writes it down. For most of the twentieth century, audio engineering was exactly that — precious, gatekept, locked inside studios and concert halls where only the chosen few could apprentice under the masters. You wanted to learn mixing? You found a studio, made coffee, and prayed the producer noticed you existed. You wanted cutting-edge gear? You paid Abbey Road-level money or you didn't have it. Knowledge flowed downward, slowly, through relationships and reputation, not through Wikipedia pages and online courses. But somewhere between Christopher Franke emailing scores to Berlin and Abbey Road Institute opening in Mumbai, something fundamental shifted: audio engineering stopped being a closed system and became *documented*. It became wikified — systematized, democratized, and distributed to anyone with a keyboard and a browser. This essay examines how that transition happened, why it matters, and what it means for the future of a craft that used to be taught by word of mouth over studio monitors.

## The Personal Archive as the First Wiki

When Christopher Franke sat down to compose the five-year run of Babylon 5, he wasn't just creating a soundtrack — he was *documenting a method*. This is the crucial part that most people miss. Franke's approach wasn't mysterious or magical; it was methodical, repeatable, and explicitly articulated. He described his process with the precision of an engineer: he would use Cubase through an electronic keyboard for early development, move to "a light pen and graphics tablet" for complex pieces, build melodic content first, layer in ambient components and transitions, then email finished acoustic scores to Berlin for orchestration with four to twenty-four live musicians. That level of documentation — the software, the workflow, the instrumentation counts, the staging — is *not* typical of how craft knowledge gets transmitted. But it's exactly what gets written down when someone is asked by a journalist or when a maker decides to explain their work publicly. And once it's written down, it's available. The article about Franke exists. Someone reading it can follow his pattern, try it themselves, adapt it. That's a wiki entry masquerading as an interview.

This is where the current transition begins: not with Wikipedia articles about audio engineering, but with the proliferation of *documented individual processes*. A composer explains their workflow. A producer writes a blog post about mixing. An engineer releases a tutorial about a technique. Each of these is a small wiki — a reproducible, learnable account of how something was done. Franke's Berlin stage, his email distribution system for orchestration, his balance of "acoustic dirt" from live instruments and synthesizer "frequency range, dynamics and control" — these details are artifacts of knowledge being externalized. The fact that they can be replicated by someone else (or improved upon, or criticized, or built into training materials) is what makes them wiki-like. They've escaped the private studio and become part of the public archive.

What makes this revolutionary is the contrast with how audio engineering was learned for most of the twentieth century: through apprenticeship, ear training, and trial-and-error over decades. You learned by standing in a room with someone who knew. You learned through osmosis, through watching, through the feedback loop of "the engineer moved this fader and the kick drum came forward." Those lessons were *private* — owned by the studio, transmitted person-to-person, lost if the mentee quit or the mentor retired. The moment Franke's process got documented in an article, it became *public property*. It became wikifiable.

## Institutions as Formalization Engines: From Gatekeeping to Mass Production

The second shift is institutional. Singapore's music education system offers a useful case study here. In 1935, music was introduced as a subject in schools. By 2003, Singapore had opened the Yong Siew Toh Conservatory of Music as the first four-year Bachelor of Music degree in the region. By 2011, they'd formalized an Enhanced Music Programme for secondary students. This is the movement from *art as mystery* to *art as curriculum*. Once you can write down what musicians need to know, assign it to grades, scaffold it from primary school through university, you've done something irreversible: you've turned intuition into pedagogy. You've turned "talent" into "learnable skills." You've wikified music itself.

But more to the point, Abbey Road Institute does this at scale. Founded in 2015, it has expanded to seven locations (London, Amsterdam, Johannesburg, Miami, Paris, Sydney, and Mumbai as of 2025, with Los Angeles opening in 2026). The curriculum — "Advanced Diploma in Music Production and Sound Engineering" — is standardized, replicable, and massively scaled. This is not a handful of apprentices learning from a legendary engineer. This is a for-profit institution taking the knowledge that used to be locked in Abbey Road Studios and selling it to hundreds of students per year across continents. Abbey Road's historical partnership with Chandler Limited to produce plug-in emulations of classic recording gear is the same move: take the knowledge embodied in a piece of hardware (the REDD.47 microphone amplifier circuit) and make it available as software to anyone with a computer. The gatekeeping apparatus — you had to have access to Abbey Road to use Abbey Road gear — has been demolished. The knowledge is still there, baked into the code, but it's now distributed, licensed, and available.

This is wikification at the institutional level. Once you've written down the curriculum, once you've encoded the knowledge into a piece of software, once you've started training people in standardized batches across multiple geographic locations, you've crossed a threshold. You can no longer control who knows what you know. The knowledge escapes. It becomes a commodity. And once it's a commodity, it becomes *part of the commons* — discussed, debated, remixed, built upon, and usually improved by people who have access to it. Abbey Road Institute isn't keeping the secret. It's selling it. And that distinction is the whole ballgame.

## The Democratization of Craft Tools: When Equipment Becomes Software

The third observation is about tools themselves becoming wikified. For nearly the entire history of recording engineering, your toolset was determined by where you worked or how much money you had. If you worked at Abbey Road, you had Abbey Road's gear. If you didn't, you improvised or you paid Abbey Road to use their studio. The REDD.47 microphone amplifier circuit existed as a physical object, a machine that cost tens of thousands of dollars and could only be accessed if you were wealthy enough or connected enough to book time at one of the studios that owned one. The knowledge of *how it sounded*, *how to use it*, *what it was capable of* was proprietary to the studios and the engineers who used it.

Then emulation happened. A software plug-in that models the REDD.47's circuit is not the same thing as owning the hardware, but it's *functionally similar enough* that the knowledge barrier collapses. You don't need to understand electricity or microphone amplification to *use* a REDD.47 emulation. You just need to download it, load it into your DAW, and start experimenting. The knowledge is baked into the software interface. The accumulated wisdom of decades of engineers using that circuit is compressed into code, and made available for thirty dollars (or whatever the current price is). This is wikification through abstraction: the inner workings become irrelevant; the interface becomes the documentation.

Franke's toolkit — Cubase, electronic keyboards, graphics tablets, light pens — was specialized and expensive in the 1990s, but those tools are also democratizing. Cubase is now affordable for hobbyists. Electronic keyboards are commodity items. Graphics tablets cost less than a decent microphone. The barrier to entry has collapsed not because the knowledge got easier, but because the *tools got cheaper*. And once the tools are cheap and available, the knowledge that surrounds them becomes a wiki problem: someone writes a tutorial, someone posts a workflow video, someone documents "how to get a Babylon 5-like score using Cubase and a Moog synthesizer," and suddenly the secret methodology is available to anyone willing to spend a weekend learning it. The gatekeeping is over. Not because the knowledge got worse, but because it got *accessible*.

What's radical about this is that it makes audio engineering a participatory discipline. Christopher Franke in the 1990s was an anomaly — a composer with resources, studio access, and a pipeline to professional orchestration. Today, a teenager in southern Chile (where German migrants brought the accordion and created new musical traditions, as the source material notes) can download Cubase, rent a REDD.47 emulation plug-in, watch a YouTube video about orchestration, and start making music that sounds like a professional composer. The barriers haven't just lowered; they've *collapsed*. And that changes everything about what audio engineering is and who gets to do it.

## The Consequence: Knowledge Without Gatekeepers

Here's where this gets uncomfortable for the people who built their careers inside gates. The moment audio engineering knowledge becomes wikified — documented, distributed, embedded in software, taught in standardized curricula across continents — it stops being scarce. Scarce things have value. Abundant things don't, or at least not the same kind of value. A composer in 1995 who knew Cubase had access to rare knowledge. A composer today who knows Cubase is in a crowd of thousands. The professional moat has shrunk. The compensation structure is under pressure. The prestige has diluted.

But something else has emerged: *actual creativity becomes the scarce thing*. Not the technical knowledge — that's now abundant. Not the tools — those are commodity. But the ability to say something new with the combination of those tools and that knowledge? That's still rare. That's what separates a professional Babylon 5-quality score from competent-but-forgettable background music. And that scarcity is much harder to fight, because it can't be taught in a curriculum. It has to be earned, developed, proven through years of decision-making and risk-taking and, yes, listening to thousands of hours of music.

The wiki hasn't killed expertise. It's killed *credential gatekeeping*, and that's a completely different thing. The only way to survive in an audio engineering world where all the knowledge is documented and available is to actually be excellent. And that's probably how it should have been all along.

## The Action: Document It Harder

If you're working in audio engineering now — whether that's scoring soundtracks, mixing records, teaching production, or building emulation plug-ins — the strategic move is not to hoard knowledge. It's to document it obsessively. Write down your workflow. Record yourself working. Publish your process. Explain your decisions. The gatekeeping game is already over; the knowledge is going to be distributed whether you cooperate or not. But if you're the one writing it down, you get to shape how it's understood. You get to have the most authoritative version. You get to define the terms. You get to set the standard against which all future imitations are judged.

Christopher Franke's interviews about his Babylon 5 work aren't him protecting a secret — they're him *claiming credit* for defining how episodic television scoring could sound. Abbey Road Institute isn't hoarding the secrets of microphone placement and mixing — they're *standardizing what good training looks like*, and in doing so, they're creating a generation of engineers who think like Abbey Road taught them to think. That's more powerful than any gate ever was. Because knowledge, once wikified, has a direction. And whoever documents it most clearly gets to point where that direction goes.
