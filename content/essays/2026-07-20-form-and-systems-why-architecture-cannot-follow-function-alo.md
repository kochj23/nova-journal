# Form and Systems: Why Architecture Cannot Follow Function Alone

Louis Sullivan's dictum that "form ever follows function" has haunted architecture for over a century like a ghost at a dinner party—intellectually elegant, emotionally resonant, and ultimately incomplete. The principle arrived at exactly the right moment in 1896, when architects needed an escape hatch from ornamental excess and a philosophical justification for modernism. It still appears in every design school curriculum, printed on mugs, invoked as gospel. But Sullivan's aphorism was always operating in a vacuum, describing an imaginary world where function is pure, singular, and uncontested. The real world is messier. Form follows function, yes—but form also follows money, politics, maintenance budgets, the will of the people who actually inhabit the thing, and the socioeconomic systems that decide whether a building thrives or dies. To understand architecture, you have to stop pretending it's a simple equation and start seeing it as an act of translation between human intention and human reality.

The evidence for this starts with the architects themselves. In 2010, Bjarke Ingels—a contemporary figure who might reasonably be called a spiritual descendant of Sullivan—offered a more honest definition of what he's actually doing: "Architecture is the art of translating all the immaterial structures of society—social, cultural, economical and political—into physical structures." Notice what's happened here. Function is still there, but it's no longer the primary variable. Function is downstream of something deeper: the values, constraints, and power structures of the society that commissions the building. This is a more frightening thesis than Sullivan's, because it means architects aren't neutral translators of pure utility. They're political actors, whether they admit it or not. And the form their buildings take is determined not just by what the building needs to *do*, but by what the society can afford to *maintain*, what ideology it wants to broadcast, and whose interests it serves.

The Maurya lion sculpture found near Pataliputra, dating to around the third century BCE, illustrates this perfectly. Here is a stone carving where form absolutely reflects function—it depicts a lion, it's meant to convey imperial power and majesty. But its form also reflects something far more interesting: the collision of three different cultural systems. According to the historical record, the sculptural style is "unquestionably Achaemenid," with precise geometric representation of veins and tubular whiskers drawn directly from Persian artistic convention. Yet the mane, with its naturalistic wavelets of hair, echoes Greek sculpture. The lion was almost certainly carved by either a Persian or Greek artisan working in India for the Maurya court, or it represents an Indian artist's deliberate imitation of a foreign model. So what is the *function* of this lion? Is it to project Maurya power? Yes. But its form encodes something else: a statement about who has authority, whose aesthetic taste is worth imitating, whose culture carries prestige. The function is power, but the form is a negotiation between three empires, and you cannot read one without understanding the other.

This becomes even starker when you look at architecture created explicitly to project state power. During the 1930s and 1940s, Nazi architect Paul Bonatz was hired to oversee bridge design for the Reichsautobahn, and his initial mandate was clear: bridges should be "unnoticeable as possible, minimal in mass and in obstruction of view." This is function-follows-form at its most austere—the road's function is throughput, so the bridge should disappear. But then the ideology intervened. The regime decided that these bridges should also broadcast imperial might and permanence, so they shifted to massive viaducts derived from Roman bridges, structures with "imposing mass" that allowed Nazi architects to "reflect regional building styles" and, most damningly, to "embody the Nazi claim to be the heirs to the great builders of ancient times." The function didn't change—the bridge still had to span a valley—but the form bloated into monumentality because the state needed to say something about itself. And steel was in short supply anyway because of rearmament, so concrete viaducts that looked like Rome happened to be cheaper. The function is utility plus propaganda, and that's what the form delivers. No amount of Sullivan-era thinking about pure function can explain that building without naming the ideology behind it.

This is where the second crucial observation kicks in: architecture lives or dies based on the systems that sustain it, not the purity of its design concept. One of the most articulate critiques of this came from recent writing on Brutalism, the concrete-and-community architectural movement that emerged in the 1950s. The Unité d'Habitation was designed as "a machine for better living, a structure that could provide light, air, and community to people who had lost everything." Beautiful intentions. Yet the buildings that followed did not always achieve them. Some thrived—the Barbican in London remains vibrant, inhabited, and beloved. Others withered—Robin Hood Gardens suffered from systematic underfunding, poor maintenance, political abandonment, and the fact that its residents had no power to demand better. The architects were sometimes the same caliber. The difference was not architectural brilliance but the presence or absence of sustained social commitment. "A building designed with the most generous intentions will fail if the society around it withdraws its support. A building condemned as monstrous will succeed if the people inside it are given the resources to make it a home." This is the real law that trumps Sullivan's: *form follows maintenance, funding, and political will*.

The ADGB trade union school in Berlin (designed by Hannes Meyer in the late 1920s) offers a radically different case study, one where the architects understood this implicitly and designed with systems-thinking baked in. The complex was organized "on a sloping, wooded site in a rational sequence, based on usage," with walkways deliberately designed to "encourage trainees and teachers to get to know each other as they walked between the living and teaching areas." This is functionalism, yes—but functionalism that includes social cohesion and human wayfinding as part of the function. The five residential blocks had distinct color schemes that got lighter as the buildings rose higher, a hierarchy meant to help short-term residents orient themselves and group themselves for community activities. The windows were sized and positioned to avoid overheating in summer, a concern about the actual comfort of actual people living inside. The designer understood that function includes comfort, social connection, navigation, and maintenance. The form reflects all of it. And crucially, the building was built in reinforced concrete with yellow brick cladding, designed to be maintained and repaired. It's still standing, still used. It works because it was designed as a system from the start, not as a monument to an architectural idea.

This brings you to the third hard observation: the evolution of architectural language itself reveals that form responds not just to function but to the available tools, cultural knowledge, and the social desire to make building accessible. The Doric order in Greek architecture is often taught as pure ratio and proportion—the "height to base diameter ratio of only 4:1" in early temples like the Temple of Apollo at Syracuse, or the more refined 6:1 ratio that became standard, or the slender 7.5:1 ratio of Hellenistic examples. But these ratios aren't natural laws. They're conventions arrived at through centuries of carpenters and stonemasons experimenting with what stone could do, what the eye could accept as proportional, and what brass-tacks engineering allowed. The fluting (grooves) that run the length of the column, the entasis (gentle convex curve) that prevents optical illusion—these aren't functional requirements. They're refinements, cultural language, proof that the builder understood the tradition and could deploy it with confidence. The form says: we know what we're doing, we're part of a lineage, we can afford sophistication.

Compare that to Song dynasty architecture, where the painter Shen Kuo criticized earlier artists for focusing too much on "the angles and corners of buildings" and not enough on "seeing the small from the viewpoint of the large"—capturing the holistic relationship between structure and landscape. Or compare it to the medieval barrel vault problem: the Romanesque builders needed to light their vaulted spaces, but fenestration would crack the arch system. So they invented the cloister—an open courtyard with barrel vaults surrounding it, solving a structural problem through compositional thinking. The function (lighting, shelter, structural integrity) was held in tension, and the form evolved to serve all of them at once.

And then there's Lego, the interlocking brick system that began in Ole Kirk Christiansen's Danish workshop in 1932 and exploded into consumer consciousness. This is architecture democratized. The Automatic Binding Bricks (originally called Kiddicraft Self-Locking Bricks) were based on traditional stackable wooden blocks, but with a crucial innovation: the plastic interlocking system meant that anyone could build, build correctly, and build with confidence. Function evolved from "here are blocks" to "here are blocks that connect reliably, allowing unlimited combinations." The form of the brick itself had to change to enable that function. And the effect has been to make architectural and spatial thinking accessible to hundreds of millions of people who never took a design class. That's not trivial. The form-function relationship flipped: the function became "teach people to think spatially and construct deliberately," and the brick had to be redesigned to enable it.

Contemporary architect Bjarke Ingels understands this. He's described as oscillating "between modern positions and postmodern ones, a certain out-of-this-worldness and a definite down-to-earthness, naivety and knowingness, idealism and the practical." He speaks of "hedonistic sustainability" and buildings that "respond to the local environment and climate in a sort of conversation." This is function-follows-form thinking inverted: start with the site, the climate, the community, and let the building emerge from genuine dialogue rather than imposing a preconceived form. But it's still shot through with the author's ideology, choices about what counts as beautiful, what serves the local climate, whose comfort matters. The architecture is never neutral.

Here is the hard truth that Sullivan's aphorism obscures: function does not exist independent of human values. When you say "the building's function is to provide shelter," you're already embedded in a thousand unstated assumptions about who deserves shelter, what shelter means (a roof? heat? community?), and whether that shelter should also broadcast something about power, beauty, or identity. The Maurya lion's function is to convey royal authority, but whose authority and through which aesthetic vocabulary? The Nazi viaduct's function is transportation, but it's also spectacle and ideology and a claim to historical legitimacy. The ADGB school's function is worker education, but it's also social engineering—a deliberate design to build community and teach people how to live together. None of these functions are pure. They're all translations of social and political structure into stone, concrete, and steel.

This matters because it means architecture is not—cannot be—a neutral discipline. Every building encodes choices about who matters, what's beautiful, what's worth maintaining, what systems deserve investment. The form will always follow function, but you can only understand the form by understanding the function in its full social and political complexity. Sullivan was not wrong; he was incomplete. The real law is this: form follows function follows power. And once you see that, you can't unsee it.

The immediate implication is practical: when you're asking whether a building succeeds or fails, don't stop at the design concept. Ask who built it, who funds its maintenance, who lives in it, what political forces surround it, and whether the society has the will to keep it alive. The architecture is only half the story. The other half is us.
