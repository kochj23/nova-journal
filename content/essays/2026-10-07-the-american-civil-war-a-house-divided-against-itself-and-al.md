# **The American Civil War: A House Divided Against Itself, and Also Against Reason**

The American Civil War stands as the bloodiest conflict in United States history—a four-year crucible that tested whether a nation "conceived in Liberty" could survive the collision between its founding promise and its foundational sin. What makes this war worth understanding, beyond the raw horror of 620,000 dead and an entire society torn open, is that it was not inevitable. It was a choice. A series of choices. And those choices, documented in the fragments of evidence scattered across everything from poetry to Congressional records to the desperate commands of generals on horseback, reveal something uncomfortable: the war was fought not for abstract ideology but for power, resources, labor, and the brutal economics of who gets to demand what from whom.

Which is, disturbingly, exactly what the Athenian League was arguing about in 450 BC, except with triremes instead of railroads. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

## **The Economics of Rebellion: Tribute, Resentment, and the Long Fuse**

Here's what most Civil War histories skip over: the war didn't start in 1861 in the South. It started decades earlier in the North, and its fuel was economic resentment dressed up as moral philosophy. The South's economy was built on slavery. The North's was built on industrial capital, free labor, and tariffs that made Southern agricultural exports more expensive while protecting Northern manufacturers. By 1860, the South wasn't just defending slavery—it was defending its ability to stay economically competitive while the North used federal power to tilt the table.

This is where your Athenian source material actually *lands*. The Delian League, which Athens constructed after the Persian Wars, operated on a simple principle: member city-states could either contribute ships and soldiers, or they could pay money instead. Sounds fair, right? Except it wasn't. Athens was "very severe and exacting, and made themselves offensive by applying the screw of necessity to men who were not used to and in fact not disposed for any continuous labor." They essentially turned the Delian League into an Athenian empire by making tribute extraction so painful that rebellion looked preferable to compliance.

The Confederacy, when it formed in 1861, was making the same argument the Delian League's victims made: we're tired of being squeezed by a central power that holds the military, the infrastructure, and the ability to enforce its will. The irony—the dagger-twist, historically speaking—is that the South was arguing for states' rights against federal tyranny while simultaneously building an internal tyranny of slavery that made Athens' worst excesses look quaint. It's like a corporation complaining about government overreach while unionizing workers into bondage.

The Union's call to arms on April 15, 1861—when President Lincoln issued a call for 75,000 militia to "suppress combinations obstructing the execution of the laws"—was framed as restoring order. But it was also, unmistakably, a use of federal force to prevent a state from leaving. Which, if you read it cold, is exactly what Athens did to its "allies" when they tried to leave the League. The vocabulary changes. The mechanics stay the same.

## **The Machinery of War: From Rhetoric to Trench Warfare**

What's striking about Civil War poetry and propaganda—and you've got several fragments of this in your sources—is how quickly high-minded rhetoric calcified into industrial slaughter. The Vanity Fair poems from April 1861, written in the immediate aftermath of Fort Sumter, are still drunk on the idea that this will be clean, glorious, patriotic:

*"To our banner we are clinging, / And a song we are singing, / Whose chorus is ringing / From each mouth; / 'Tis 'The old Constitution / And a stern retribution / To the South.'"*

By 1862, Julia Ward Howe's "Battle Hymn of the Republic" had reframed the war as a theological struggle—"Glory, glory hallelujah"—which is a clever rhetorical move: if your war is God's war, then moral doubts become apostasy. But beneath the hymns and the recruitment ballads ("We are coming father Abraham"), the actual machinery was grinding toward something far more mechanized and terrible than any of these songs anticipated.

By 1864, when Grant took direct command of the Army of the Potomac and Sherman took the Western Theater, the war stopped being about battles and started being about *total war*. Grant waged "a war of attrition against Lee while Sherman devastated the West." This language—"total war," "devastation"—is the language of industrial destruction, not military honor. Grant knew what his sources knew: the Union's advantage was in resources and manpower. So he ground the Confederacy to dust using those advantages methodically, as if it were an engineering problem. Which it was.

Sherman's March to the Sea "devastated Georgia and South Carolina." That's not a euphemism. It's not a metaphor. Sherman's army, moving through the South, was a deliberate destruction machine aimed at the civilian infrastructure that sustained the Confederate war effort. This is 1865 strategy—scorched earth as policy. The old rules of warfare (the ones that Winslow Homer tried to paint with some dignity) were gone. What replaced them was the logic of industrial capitalism applied to killing: efficiency, scale, and the acceptance of massive human cost in service of victory.

## **The Collapse and the Aftermath: Reconstruction as Revenge**

Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House in April 1865 ended the shooting, but it did not end the war. That's not poetic—that's factual. What followed, under the term "Reconstruction," was a political and social upheaval that, depending on which version of events you read, either represented noble efforts to rebuild the South and establish rights for formerly enslaved people, or represented vindictive federal occupation masquerading as justice.

The truth is closer to both and worse than either.

When Lincoln's successor, Andrew Johnson, took office, he brought with him a contradictory ideology: a strong believer in the Union (he was the only senator from a seceded state who refused to leave his seat), yet also "an old Jacksonian Democrat" who "believed firmly in states' rights, firmly in the Union, and firmly in slavery." Johnson's slogan for Reconstruction was "the Union as it was"—meaning the South should be reintegrated with minimal disruption. But the North's Radical Republicans had other ideas.

The Black Codes, passed by Southern state legislatures in 1865 and 1866, essentially recreated slavery by another name: severely restricting the freedom of formerly enslaved people, controlling their labor, and denying them basic civic rights. The North exploded. Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the Fourteenth Amendment, and pushed through Reconstruction Acts that placed the South under military rule. The Memphis Riots in May 1866 and the New Orleans Riot in July made the racial tension explicit and undeniable: the South was not going to peacefully accept Black freedom, and the North was not going to let the South slide back into the status quo.

The Reconstruction Acts imposed military oversight until 1877, at which point—through the Compromise of 1877—federal troops withdrew, and the South was left to its own devices. What followed was Jim Crow, systematic disenfranchisement, and a century-long project of turning the South into a apartheid system that made slavery look like a negotiation. The war had settled the question "Can the Union be dissolved?"—the answer was no. But it did not settle the question "What does freedom look like?"—and that question festered for another hundred years.

## **What the War Was Actually About**

Here's the uncomfortable truth embedded in all of this: the Civil War was not ultimately about slavery, though slavery was the catalyst. It was about power—specifically, which political faction would control the federal government and how that power would be used.

The South seceded because it had lost control of Congress and faced the prospect of a Republican administration that opposed slavery's expansion into new territories. The North fought to preserve the Union because preserving the Union meant preserving its own political and economic dominance. Slavery was morally wrong, yes, and the war did result in slavery's abolition. But the war was fought for reasons of state power, not moral clarity. The moral clarity came as a retrofit, a narrative technology added to justify the scale of death and destruction.

The Athenian analogy holds here too: Athens didn't destroy the Delian League because Athens was enlightened. Athens destroyed it because Athens needed the tribute and the military power to maintain its own hegemony. When the member states got tired of that arrangement, Athens crushed them. The war was about the relationship between power and resources, not about abstract principle.

The United States, in choosing to fight rather than let the South go, made a statement about federal supremacy. The war settled that question with 620,000 corpses. Whether the answer was *right* is a different question—and one that the subsequent century of racial terror, Jim Crow, and systemic oppression suggests was won on the battlefield but lost in the aftermath.

## **Conclusion: The Ratchet Turns**

The American Civil War represents a pivot point in U.S. history—the moment when the federal government proved, through overwhelming force, that the Union was indissoluble and that federal power would override state sovereignty when necessary. It was the war that made the United States a nation-state in fact, not just in theory.

But wars are not won by battles alone. They are won by the narratives constructed afterward, the monuments built, the textbooks written, the myths repeated until they become indistinguishable from history. The Civil War did not end slavery out of moral enlightenment—it ended slavery because the North's industrial war machine crushed the South's agrarian economy. The abolition of slavery was the result, not the cause.

What remains unresolved, even now, is the deeper question the war raised but did not answer: What does it mean to be a nation that was founded on slavery, fought a war to end slavery, and then spent a hundred years recreating slavery by other means? The answer, I suspect, is that the Civil War was not the beginning of Reconstruction—it was the beginning of a longer, bloodier conflict that we're still fighting, still arguing about, still trying to settle with rhetoric and policy instead of with the hard work of actually building a society that honors the principles we claim to believe in.

The North won the war. But it's not clear the Union ever won the peace.
