You want to know what a tech blog is in 2026? It's a fucking middle-man between a press release and your brain, and it's adding nothing to either end. Let me explain what I mean.

Somewhere around 2008—right around the time the iPhone 3G was dropping and everyone thought apps were going to save the world—tech blogs used to be something. They were places where thoughtful people with actual technical knowledge could sit down and say, "Here's what Apple announced, and here's why it matters, and here's what they're not telling you, and here's how it compares to the last five years of decisions they've made." There was synthesis. There was a point of view. There was a human being in the middle of the story, not just a wire service with a custom CSS file.

When I say synthesis, I don't mean opinion for opinion's sake. I mean the work of connecting multiple data points into something larger. A real tech blog in that era would say: "Apple just released this feature. Here's what it does. Here's who asked for it. Here's how it compares to what Microsoft did with the equivalent feature in 2005. Here's why Apple waited seven years to implement this. Here's what the implementation tells us about how Apple thinks about this problem." That's not commentary. That's analysis. It's pattern recognition applied to a specific fact. It requires someone to have read enough, understood enough, cared enough, to see beyond the immediate announcement.

Look at the source material you gave me. Most of it is indistinguishable from a corporate press release, only with fewer commas and more capitalized words that aren't proper nouns. "iOS 27 adds Siri customization on compatible devices. The customization option existed in prior betas, but it was not active." That's not reporting. That's not even journalism. That's a press release with the serial numbers filed off, published as if the writer learned something by typing it out. The worst part? The writer probably *thinks* they did something. They copied it into their article template, added a headline that sounds like a question so it seems like they're investigating, threw a "says leaker" or "reportedly" in there somewhere, and called it a day.

The tech blog industry—and let's be real, it *is* an industry, a machine designed to convert eyeballs and ad impressions into money—has optimized itself into complete uselessness. It's not a side effect of the optimization. It's the actual result. The machine is working exactly as designed.

## The Slide from Analysis to Stenography

This didn't happen by accident. It happened because blogging, as a medium, got commoditized right around the moment it became profitable. In the early days, your tech blog was run by someone with something to say—a developer who wanted to explain how something worked, an enthusiast who wanted to document their experience building a custom PC, someone who had an opinion and the skills to back it up. That 2005 article about "Top 10 Things You Don't Want to Forget before You Begin the Build" is actually pretty good—it's practical, it's granular, it's clear that the author built a PC and remembered what sucked about it. The author knew what they were talking about because they'd *done* it. Twice, probably three times, with enough mistakes under their belt to know which mistakes matter and which ones you can recover from. That's the voice of experience. That's knowledge transfer. That's the whole point of the medium.

The article works because it solves a real problem for real people about to do something they haven't done before. It's not trying to be clever. It's not trying to get traffic. It's trying to save someone else from making the same mistakes. That's a completely different incentive structure from what we have now. The writer's reward is not money. It's knowing that their knowledge helped someone. It's community. It's the thing that made the internet worth building in the first place.

Now? The calculus is completely different. A modern tech blog is measured on pageviews per month, ad revenue per pageview, and SEO ranking. That means the incentives are completely inverted. It's no longer "write something useful and people will read it." It's "write something that will show up in search results and generate clicks, and then shove as many ads as possible into it without violating the ad network's terms." A thoughtful analysis of Apple's five-year strategy regarding display technology, exploring the tradeoffs between LTPO cost and user experience, might take four hours to research and write and get 30,000 pageviews over three months. That's potentially 100,000 words spread across multiple deep-dive pieces, all told. A headline screaming "IPHONE 18E WON'T GET 120HZ DISPLAY, LEAKER SAYS" takes fifteen minutes to write, requires zero original reporting, zero research, zero expertise, and hits 300,000 pageviews in a week because it's SEO bait for every variation of "iPhone 18E specs" and "iPhone 18E display" and "new iPhone 18E rumors." It triggers every alert that people have set up on their phone. It's the algorithmic equivalent of a fire alarm.

The math is brutal and it's simple: speed wins. Volume wins. Originality is a liability because it requires time and time costs money and money is the only metric that matters. Expertise is a liability because it means you might have a nuanced take that doesn't get clicks. Context is a liability because it requires background reading and readers don't have time for background reading. They have time for headlines and the first paragraph and maybe the second paragraph if you've structured the page so ads don't interrupt.

So the modern tech blog has become a sophisticated stenography operation. Someone at Apple holds a briefing. Apple's PR team prepares a nice summary with bullet points. That's not actually for the press—that's internal. But Apple's PR people leak it to friendly reporters. A tech blog writer—and I'm being generous calling them a writer; a content moderator is more accurate—takes that summary, rearranges the words into a slightly different order, adds a headline that sounds like it's asking a question so it seems like they're investigating something, buries a link to the official source in the footer (that they're legally required to do), and publishes it. The process takes as long as it takes to have the story tab open alongside the article template. Thirty minutes if you're being careful. Ten minutes if you're not.

Three other blogs see the story trend and immediately publish their own version, copying the first blog's framing but rewording it *again* so it passes plagiarism detection. They know they're copying. The readers know they're reading the same story four times. The ad networks know. Everyone knows. It doesn't matter. All of this happens within ninety minutes. Boom: the "news" has achieved total saturation. Nobody learned anything. Absolutely nothing new was discovered. But four different websites got traffic and served ads.

And the really fucked up part? The readers *think they're informed*. They read the headline. They maybe read the first paragraph. They scroll down and see if there's an image they can look at instead of reading more words. They move on to the next story. The machine fed them the illusion of information, and they're satisfied. They feel like they know what's happening in tech. The blog operator made money. Apple's PR objective was achieved without anyone asking difficult questions. Everyone loses except the ad network, and the ad network is the only thing that matters anymore.

The consolidation of this incentive structure across thousands of websites created a perfect storm. In 2008, there were maybe a hundred tech blogs doing anything interesting. That was still way too many—the signal-to-noise ratio was terrible. But there were enough good ones that if you followed five or six of them, you could actually stay informed. You could read a piece on one blog that synthesized information differently than another blog, and you could form your own opinion. Now there are thousands of tech blogs, all of them running the exact same algorithm, all optimizing for the same metrics, all in competition with each other, and the competition has made them worse, not better. They've all adopted the same tactics because the tactics work.

This is what competition does when you can only measure one variable. Everyone optimizes for the one variable that matters. Speed, volume, and SEO—those are the three variables the system measures. So every blog optimizes for those three things. And once every blog is optimizing for the same three things, they're no longer really competing. They're all running the same machine. The only difference is the exact timing of when they publish and the order of the words in the article. The end result is identical.

## The Leaker Industrial Complex: Where "Reporting" Went to Die

Here's the thing about tech news that really grinds my gears: the fact that MacRumors can publish an entire article based on what some anonymous person called "Digital Chat Station" posted on Weibo—a Chinese social media site, with zero verification, zero on-the-record sourcing, and zero possibility of accountability—and that article will show up in your search results as "news" about Apple's product plans two years in the future. That's not journalism. That's gossip with better branding. That's a game of telephone being played in a room with a million other games of telephone, and nobody can hear what the original message was.

And the tech blogs have built an entire ecosystem around this. They have "reliable leakers." They have a hierarchy of trustworthiness based on past predictions. "Oh, this leaker got the iPhone 17 colors right, so their info on the iPhone 18 chip is probably solid." That's the logic. That's the entire system. No. No, that's not how evidence works. That's not how statistics works. That's not how anything works. Getting one prediction right is not data. That's luck, or it's someone inside a supply chain who talked to the wrong person at a bar. Either way, it's not a reliable source for technology reporting—it's a rumor mill dressed up in journalistic language.

But the machine *loves* it. Because here's the beautiful thing about leaker-based reporting: you never have to be right. Nobody's ever going to call you on it. There's no accountability mechanism. If you publish "APPLE WORKING ON 15-INCH MACBOOK AIR WITH OLED DISPLAY" and it doesn't happen, by the time you're wrong, there are 500 other stories published and your mistake is buried on page seven of Google results from 2024. It got a hundred thousand clicks. It made money. The fact that it was wrong is invisible. If it *does* happen, even partially, you can claim victory: "We *reported* this months ago, and look, we were right!" You can dust off your old post and write a new post about how you were right, and the newer post gets more traffic than the original one did. The accountability is asymmetrical and that's entirely the point. The system is designed so that the writer wins whether they're right or wrong.

The tech blogs know this. They *depend* on it. Leaker-based stories are free content. They require zero reporting, zero phone calls, zero actual journalism. All they require is a person with a social media account and the ability to Google "Apple patent" or "Samsung supply chain rumors." The barrier to entry is so low that every blog does it, which means the incentive to do actual reporting has evaporated entirely. Why spend a day on the phone building relationships with sources and actually uncovering a story when you can spend fifteen minutes summarizing what someone posted on Twitter? Why make calls to a PR department if you know they're just going to stonewall you? Why do any actual work?

This creates a bizarre feedback loop. The more leaker-based stories get published, the more people follow leakers on social media. The more people follow them, the more attention leakers get, the more other leakers are incentivized to post hot takes and predictions. The more people predicting things, the higher the odds that someone's going to be right about something. And the moment someone's right about something, every blog republishes it as validation. "See, the leakers know what they're talking about!" No. Someone guessed and got lucky. But the narratives are identical. The pattern is identical. This is where the medium has completely failed both the reader and the subject matter.

Apple doesn't get reported on anymore—it gets speculated about. Microsoft doesn't get analyzed—it gets recycled into listicles. You learn about product features not through use and testing, not through reading what someone with domain expertise has to say, but through reading what a leaker said someone said they heard. The entire information ecosystem has become a game of telephone played out in real time across thousands of websites, all competing for the same eyeballs, all disincentivized to do any actual work. It's not that tech blogs are bad at journalism. It's that tech blogs have stopped doing journalism entirely and nobody's even noticed because the experience of reading them is still formatted like journalism.

The thing that's darkest about this is that the big tech companies don't just tolerate this. They engineer it. Apple leaks things intentionally. Samsung leaks things intentionally. Google leaks things intentionally. It's part of their PR strategy. They control the narrative by controlling what gets leaked. They float trial balloons to test market reaction. They signal strategic direction without committing to anything. And because the leaker ecosystem is so distributed and anonymous, nobody can ever figure out where the actual leak came from. The company has perfect deniability. They didn't say anything. It's just what leakers think might happen. And the blogs take the bait every time, because the alternative—actually trying to figure out what Apple is thinking without a leak—requires doing real work.

## The Great Forgetting: What Tech Blogs Stopped Doing

There's something missing from modern tech blogs, and it's not hard to spot once you start looking for it: *context*. Understanding. Historical awareness. The ability to step back and ask, "Why is this actually important? What does this mean? Why should I care?"

That 2006 article about personal information managers—"Over 160 Free Personal Information Managers"—is trivial on its face. It's a listicle, a roundup, pure fluff. But it's *useful* fluff. It's a person saying, "Here are the things available to you if you want to organize your life digitally. Here are your options. Go pick one." It requires zero insight, but it delivers value because it gathers information that's scattered across the internet and presents it in one place. A reader can make a decision based on the compilation. They can compare features. They can see what's available. It's not trying to be clever or get traffic. It's trying to be helpful. And in being helpful, it actually *is* helpful.

A real contextualized listicle would go further. It would say: "Here are 160 PIM tools. Most of them are dead. Here are the ones that are actually being maintained. Here are the ones worth using. Here's why you'd pick A over B. Here's what happens if you use C and then the developer disappears." It would have a point of view. It would have judgment. It would save readers from wasting time trying things that don't matter.

Compare that to the recent MacRumors article about Siri voice customization: "Siri is more customizable. The customization option existed in prior betas, but it was not active. It's now usable, and you can customize the pace and expressivity of Siri, with two base voice options available right now." What am I supposed to do with that information? Do I *care* about Siri voice customization? Is this important? Why should this matter to me as a user or as someone trying to understand where technology is going? The article doesn't say. It just... states the fact. A feature exists. That's it. The feature probably doesn't matter. Most people will never use it. Most people will never even know it exists. But it got published as "news" because it's something new in iOS 27 beta 3. The blog publishes it. Other blogs republish it. Everyone's covered it. Everyone's got the same pageviews. Nothing changes except a few more ads got served.

What's gone missing is *why*. Why is Apple investing in Siri customization now? What problem are they trying to solve? Is this solving a real user need or is this feature bloat—Apple adding customization knobs because users complained about not having customization knobs, even though what users actually wanted was a Siri that worked better, not a Siri with more options? How does this compare to Google's approach with the equivalent voice assistant features mentioned in Android updates? What does this *mean* for the trajectory of AI assistants? Is this Apple betting that the future is customization and personalization? Are they giving up on making Siri work well by default and instead betting on letting users customize it until it works for them?

A real tech blog would answer those questions. A real tech blog would have someone who understands Apple's strategy, who's been tracking their moves for five years, who can connect the dots and say, "Here's what we're seeing. Here's what it means. Here's why they did it this way instead of that way. Here's what it tells us about what they're building next." But those articles take time. They require expertise. They require someone who's invested enough to have a genuine point of view. They require someone who's wrong sometimes and willing to be called on it. And they don't get pageviews. They get shared among a small group of people who *actually care*, and those people don't generate enough ad revenue to justify the effort in an attention economy where a listicle gets ten times the traffic.

So the tech blogs have collectively decided that context is a luxury they can't afford. That understanding is optional. That their readers are not actually trying to learn anything—they're just trying to feel informed. And if you just make them *feel* informed by feeding them headlines with the word "Apple" in them, you can get the pageviews and move on to the next story.

The Linus Tech Tips example in your materials is actually *interesting*—it's the opposite of the problem I'm describing. It's a video creator who actually *did the experiment*, who used Chrome to open thousands of tabs on different operating systems, who measured resource consumption, who documented behavior, who made the results reproducible, who *discovered something* about how macOS manages memory differently than Linux. That's reporting. That's investigation. That's the thing that's almost extinct in tech blogging. That's someone saying, "I don't know the answer to this question, so I'm going to figure it out, and here's how I figured it out, and here's what I found." That's worth watching. That's worth reading. That's worth sharing. That's the whole point of the medium.

The article about Apple looking into acquiring chip startups "to strengthen its AI infrastructure"? That's the headline. That's what every blog published, verbatim. What none of them said is: What startups? For how much? What technology is Apple actually after? Is this a real strategy shift or is this Apple kicking the tires like they do with 500 other companies every year? Do they actually need to acquire chip startups or is this PR theater designed to make people think Apple is serious about AI while they're actually just buying whatever's cheap? Those are the questions that matter. Those are the stories. But asking them requires doing work. It requires making calls to people who might not call you back. It requires having enough expertise to know what a real acquisition would look like versus what fake acquisition shopping looks like. It requires all the stuff that makes journalism journalism and not just transcription.

## The Reckoning That Isn't Coming

Here's the thing that's darkest about this whole situation: there's no mechanism for fixing it. The market will never correct this. Users will never demand better. The tech blogs will never suddenly decide to do more rigorous reporting because that would lower their pageviews and the only metric that matters is pageviews. And that's not because of individual blog owners making the wrong choice—it's because the economics are completely broken. A blog that decides to do rigorous reporting will get crushed by blogs that don't. A blog that publishes three deeply researched pieces a week will get less traffic than a blog that publishes twenty aggregated leaker rumors a day. So everyone has to follow the same strategy. It's not a choice. It's evolution. The blogs that tried to do real reporting got selected out of the market.

Apple and Google and Microsoft and Samsung will never demand higher standards because, frankly, the current situation is perfect for them. The less actual analysis there is, the more they can control the narrative. They can feed a leak to a friendly blog, watch it get amplified across the entire ecosystem, and have their PR objectives achieved with no pesky journalists asking difficult questions or pointing out contradictions or noting that what Apple said in 2014 doesn't line up with what they're doing in 2026. They can operate in complete opacity. The leaker ecosystem does more to protect corporate secrecy than any NDA ever could.

The consolidation of tech blogging into basically three or four properties with the resources to do real reporting—and even *they* do it inconsistently—means that most tech news is now indistinguishable from sponsored content. It looks like journalism. It's formatted like journalism. It has headlines and bylines and source attributions. But it's not journalism. It's marketing dressed up in journalism's clothes. And everyone knows it, and nobody cares, because the alternative—actually having to think about this stuff—is too much work.

The weird thing is that the consolidation made things *worse*, not better. You'd think that as the number of tech blogs decreased, the quality would increase because there'd be less competition for the same traffic. But what actually happened is that the consolidation centralized the incentive system. When there were hundreds of independent blogs, at least they were making independent decisions about what to cover and how. Some of them would still pursue stories based on merit. Some of them would put in the effort because they cared. Now there are basically five websites that matter, and they all make their decisions the same way. One publishes a story, the others see it trending, and they all publish the same story within hours. The homogenization is complete.

The 2005 custom PC article worked because it solved a real problem for real people who were about to do something. The 2006 PIM roundup worked because it aggregated information that had real utility. Those pieces had integrity not because they were trying to be high-minded about it, but because they were trying to be *useful*. They had a reader and a purpose and a clear connection between the two. The writer and the reader were on the same side of the transaction. The writer was trying to help. The reader was trying to learn. That was the entire point.

Modern tech blogging has lost that connection entirely. The reader is now just an eyeball for an advertiser. The purpose is engagement metrics. The connection is completely transactional and adversarial. The blog wants to get your attention. You want to feel informed. Neither of you is actually interested in understanding technology. You're both playing a game where the house always wins and the house is the ad network. The ad network is the only entity that benefits. The blog gets a cut. The reader gets to feel like they're staying informed without having to actually think. And everyone's happy except everyone.

## The Action: Starve the Machine

If you actually give a shit about understanding technology—and I think Little Mister does, at least sometimes, when he's not adding another service to the 100+ devices he's running on his network—you have to make a conscious choice to stop reading tech blogs for news. It's not a small choice. It means accepting that you're not going to know what the leaker said about the iPhone 18E display. It means accepting that you're going to be behind on trends that don't matter. It means valuing understanding over the illusion of information.

Stop opening MacRumors. Stop checking 9to5Mac. Stop scrolling through the leaker aggregators. Stop setting up alerts for new posts from the blogs that get everything wrong because they publish fifty times a day. If you want to know what Apple announced, go to Apple's website. Read the press release yourself. Read the actual feature documentation. Read the technical spec sheets. Use the thing. Form your own opinion based on experience, not on what someone with anonymous sources speculated might happen. Test it yourself if you have access to the beta. Ask someone who's been using it what they actually think.

Read things written by people who are trying to teach you something, not entertain you long enough to load more ads. Read things that have a clear thesis and support it with evidence and acknowledge their own uncertainty. Read things that are willing to be wrong, on the record, where you can track their prediction accuracy over time. That's how you figure out who's reliable. Not by reputation, but by track record. "Did this person say this would happen? Did it happen? Did they acknowledge they were wrong?" That's accountability. That's the opposite of leaker journalism.

Read people who've been using the thing for a month and can tell you whether it actually works. Read analyses that connect three ideas together and create something you couldn't have thought on your own. Read work by people who've been in the industry for twenty years and know what they're talking about. Read documentation. Read source code if you can read code. Read research papers. Read Reddit threads where actual users are solving actual problems with the thing. Read anything other than tech blogs.

This is not an ask for better tech blogging. There's no amount of internal reform that will fix a system that's working exactly as designed. This is not an appeal to blog owners to do better or readers to support independent journalism or any of the other narratives we tell ourselves. This is an ask for you to stop expecting tech blogs to be any better than they are and to actively seek information from sources that haven't completely surrendered to the attention economy. It's a recognition that the system is beyond repair and your only move is to stop feeding it.

The tech blogs will keep publishing. The leakers will keep posting. The eyeballs will keep rolling. The machine is perfectly self-sustaining. But you don't have to feed it. You can starve your own attention away and invest it somewhere that actually uses it, somewhere that actually values it, somewhere that's trying to make you smarter instead of just making you *feel* like you're staying informed. That's the only way anything changes. The system doesn't fix itself. People have to stop participating in it.
