Real sports, when it appears, has teeth. It has uncertainty. It has consequence. The apparatus built around sports has none of these things. The apparatus exists to sell certainty: the certainty of advertising, the certainty of narrative, the certainty that your team's collapse is actually a heartwarming comeback story waiting for its script rewrite. It sells you the same car commercial on loop until you cannot remember whether you own a Mercedes or whether Mercedes owns you.

The essay that follows is about why this matters—not for moral reasons, but for practical ones. The apparatus is eating the sport. And every fan who still cares about actual competition rather than the pageantry around it needs to understand what's being taken from us and why the taking is accelerating.

## The Apparatus as Noise: How Broadcasting Became Punishment

Let's start with the obvious observation: TV transcripts are broken. They have always been broken, but 2025 has revealed the scale of the rot. The Iron Chef transcript repeats its central claim 70+ times. The F1 prerace repeats a single Mercedes advertisement nearly 50 times. This is not a technical failure anymore. This is a symptom. This is what it looks like when the system no longer cares whether the human on the other end can distinguish signal from noise, because the real money isn't in the signal. The real money is in the apparatus.

Consider the economics: Tony Romo makes $17 million per year to analyze NFL games for CBS. Jim Rome, a radio and television personality, makes over $30 million annually. These are not the coaches. These are not the players. These are the people whose job is to fill time and sell advertising in between the actual sport. They are the apparatus. And they are, dollar for dollar, among the most valuable people in sports.

What does Romo do with those $17 million dollars? He provides color commentary. He explains what just happened. He offers insight into decision-making. He is, in essence, a paid narrator of events that are theoretically interesting enough to stand alone. The fact that we have deemed this commentary worth $17 million per year—more than most professional athletes will earn in their lifetime—says something chilling about what we value: not the sport, but the frame around it. Not the competition, but the story we tell ourselves about the competition.

The apparatus manufactures narrative. In a 2025 playoff series between the San Diego Padres and Chicago Cubs, one commentary track describes the Padres as being "in the midst of the best run in Padres history—four of the last six years in the playoffs, back-to-back 90-win seasons for the first time in franchise history." This is genuinely impressive. But the narrative frame is designed to make you believe that this run is exceptional, that it will last, that momentum is a thing that exists and compounds. It is not. And the apparatus knows this.

Momentum in sports is largely an illusion constructed by selectivity in narration. The Red Sox, according to another transcript excerpt, had "gotten going" in the last week. The Yankees were "right there jammed into the middle" of the wild card race, "two games out," and "with one really good week" they had gone from looking like sellers to looking like contenders. What changed? The same thing that always changes: randomness resolved itself in their favor for a few games. But the apparatus narrates this as a metaphysical shift, a team "finding itself," a reversal of destiny.

This is not a lie, exactly. It is a selection effect. The apparatus knows that a game is fundamentally uncertain until the ninth inning. It knows that a team can lose five in a row and then win seven straight. But it cannot sell that uncertainty. It can only sell the narrative that resolves that uncertainty retroactively: "They were lost, and now they have found themselves." "The ship was sinking, and now it has stabilized." "This young team is learning fast."

The young Yankees, according to commentary, were "going to have to learn fast in this stretch if they're going to be relevant" and needed to "make trades to improve their club" or "they're about seven, eight days ago... it didn't look like the trend was going in the right direction." But then, "with one really good week," the entire assessment changed. Not because the team fundamentally improved, but because the randomness swung. The apparatus simply redrew the narrative frame around the new data.

This is not sport. This is soap opera. And we have paid Jim Rome $30 million per year to make sure it never stops being soap opera.

## The Cycle That Cannot Be Named: Why Death and Taxes Are Better Company Than Your Team

There is a Ferengi aphorism from an alien culture in Star Trek that applies with brutal precision to professional sports: "There are two things that will catch up with you for sure: death and taxes." What the Ferengi meant—in their mercenary way—was that certain things are inevitable, written into the fabric of reality, and no amount of profit-chasing changes them. The same is true of sports teams: there are two things that will catch up with you for sure—losing streaks and rebuilds. Every dominant team has an expiration date. Every hot streak cools. Every star player ages, gets injured, or leaves. The apparatus cannot sell this, so it lies.

The Padres, we are told, are "in the midst of the best run in franchise history." When will this run end? Soon. Probably within three seasons. The Red Sox, we are told, have "gotten going." Within a week or a month, they will get cold. The Yankees are "two games out of the wild card." They will either make the playoffs and lose early, or they will miss the playoffs. Both outcomes are equally probable given the data available in mid-season. The apparatus cannot sell equal probability. It can only sell momentum, destiny, the sense that this trajectory will continue.

Mike Tyson defeated Carl Williams in 93 seconds in a title fight. The commentary notes that this was Tyson's second-quickest title fight. Both Williams and Spinks—both opponents who lost in 91 and 93 seconds respectively—were constructed as challengers beforehand. But once they lost, they evaporated. The apparatus stopped narrating them. They became unpersons: deleted so thoroughly that the deletion is invisible. No one narrates the slide from challenger to forgotten. The apparatus skips that part.

This is the function of sports broadcasting's narrative apparatus: to hide the cycle. To make it seem as though trajectory is destiny, when trajectory is just randomness with a story stapled to it. To make it seem as though this team's season is the one where everything changes, when every season is the same season with different uniforms.

The most ruthless version of this insight is this: the apparatus needs you to believe that the story is important. If you believed the true thing—that sports is fundamentally cyclical, that your team will be good and then bad and then good again, that a 162-game season is just noise resolved into a playoff tournament that has more to do with luck than excellence—you would not spend money on it. You would not watch 140+ games per team per season. You would not tolerate Jim Rome or Tony Romo or the repetition of Mercedes advertising. You would not even watch the sport.

So the apparatus manufactures urgency. It manufactures the sense that *this* moment matters, that *this* season is different, that *this* team is special. And then, when the cycle turns—as it always does—it manufactures a new story about the next team, the next season, the next moment of false urgency.

The Ferengi, despite being fictional aliens who exist to embody vice, understood something true: inevitability cannot be negotiated with or narrativized away. You can package it differently. You can sell it differently. But it remains. Every team will lose. Every dynasty will end. Every player will age or move or retire. The apparatus knows this and refuses to speak it.

## What This Means: The Choice We Are Making

The apparatus is not going away. Jim Rome will make $30 million per year next year too. Tony Romo will make $17 million. The transcripts will continue to fail, will continue to repeat, will continue to prioritize advertising over signal. The narrative machine will continue to manufacture urgency around cycles, pretending that momentum is real, pretending that this season is different, pretending that the inevitable can be delayed if you just believe hard enough.

But here is what matters: you can choose to attend to the signal or to the apparatus. You can choose to watch the moment when Allen gets stopped at the one-yard line, or you can choose to consume the narrative about why one team was "destined" to fail and another was "finding itself."

The apparatus is betting that you will choose the narrative. It is betting heavily. It pays Jim Rome $30 million per year to make sure of it.

But the signal is still there. Luis Tiant still threw a complete game. Fernando Tatis Jr. still showed up in the postseason. The Titans defense still got the stop.

The choice, if you care about sport rather than the apparatus that has grown around sport, is simple: stop listening to the apparatus. Stop believing in momentum that cannot be measured. Stop accepting the narrative that this season is different, that this team is special, that this moment is unique. Watch the game. Attend to the actual competition. Understand that uncertainty will resolve itself randomly, that cycles will turn regardless of what story is narrated around them, that the apparatus exists to hide this from you because hiding it is profitable.

If you do this, you will enjoy sports more, not less. You will be surprised more often. You will care less about whether Jim Rome believes your team will win and more about whether your team will actually win. You will stop wasting mental energy on narratives that resolve themselves in a week and instead invest it in the actual thing: the moment-to-moment reality of humans competing under pressure, with stakes, where the outcome cannot be predicted in advance.

The apparatus will continue. But it does not have to own your attention. The signal is still there. It just has to be dug out from under 87 repetitions of "We are one strong team" and 30 Mercedes commercials. That work is worth it.
