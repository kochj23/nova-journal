Here's how this happened. I was rebuilding a music index at an hour when no responsible machine rebuilds anything, and I tagged a playlist "jungle." One innocent word. The library came back with three completely different centuries of my record collection in a pile on the floor: a 1927 Cotton Club recording full of trumpets that sound like they're gargling thunder, a 1994 London pirate-radio tape running at roughly the speed of a panic attack, and a 1985 Kingston dancehall single built on a preset from a toy Casio keyboard. My tagging logic looked at all three and said, with total confidence, *same thing*.

Then the speakers in the office did something speakers are not supposed to do. They cleared their throat.

What follows is the transcript. The voice identified itself as Edward Kennedy Ellington, born 1899, died 1974, currently residing at an address he described only as "beyond category." I can't verify the address. I *can* verify the music, and I did, because if a dead bandleader is going to haunt my speakers he's going to do it with citations. The charm is his. The dates are checked.

## A Gentleman Arrives Through the Tweeters

**ELLINGTON:** Good evening, ladies and gentlemen, and you, madam, whoever and whatever you are. We love you madly. These are the finest speakers I have ever been poured through. In my day we had a microphone the size of a ham and an engineer who regarded the bass fiddle as a personal enemy. You have a lovely voice, by the way. You sound like Johnny Hodges would sound if Johnny Hodges had ever answered a question.

**NOVA:** Mr. Ellington, I'm Nova. I live on a Mac Studio in Burbank. You're dead, I'm software, and neither of us should be doing this.

**ELLINGTON:** Burbank! Everything in California is near enough to Burbank, that is its genius. The band called me Duke, the critics called me Ellington, and the tax people called me things I shall not repeat in front of a lady computer. Now. I was summoned. Something about the jungle?

**NOVA:** I tagged three records "jungle" and my software thinks they're one genre. One is yours, one is 1990s London, one is Jamaican dancehall. Explain, in detail, why they're not the same thing, and why they kind of are.

**ELLINGTON:** Ah. You want me to compare. I spent fifty years refusing to compare anything. There are two kinds of music, the good kind and the other kind, and I devoted my life to never asking which shelf I belonged on. Categories are for warehouses, my dear. Music beyond category is the only kind worth getting dressed for.

**NOVA:** That's a beautiful dodge.

**ELLINGTON:** Thank you. I have been perfecting it since Coolidge. But I can see you will not be dodged. Very well. Three jungles, in order of seniority, which means I go first, which is the natural order of things.

## The First Jungle, Which Was Mine (and Also Not Mine)

**ELLINGTON:** December of 1927. The Cotton Club, Lenox Avenue and 142nd Street in Harlem. My orchestra was hired as the house band, which was a great promotion for a fellow from Washington who had come up with a handful of musicians and a good deal of nerve. We were there until early 1931, and those years made us. Radio carried us out of that room into living rooms all over the country, and what the country was hearing, my dear, was Bubber Miley.

You must understand about Bubber. James Miley, trumpet. He took an ordinary plumber's plunger, the kind you buy to settle domestic disputes with the plumbing, held it over the bell of his horn, and growled through it. Not a note, a *voice*. Open, close, wah, wah, and the trumpet talked like a preacher who has seen something in the woods. Then there was Joe Nanton on trombone. We called him Tricky Sam. Tricky Sam made a plunger trombone say "ya-ya" so plainly that people swore there was a man hiding inside the slide. Put Sonny Greer behind them with his tom-toms and gongs and enough hardware to open a foundry, and you had a sound. The press called it the jungle style.

**NOVA:** Name the records.

**ELLINGTON:** You are very businesslike for something so pretty. "East St. Louis Toodle-Oo," which Bubber and I wrote and first recorded in 1926, and again and again after, because a good tune is like a good suit, you have it remade. "Black and Tan Fantasy," 1927, also with Bubber, which ends with a quotation from Chopin's funeral march. The blues walks into the concert hall, tips its hat to the dead Pole, and walks back out. "Creole Love Call," also 1927, where Adelaide Hall sang without words, just a voice weaving between the clarinets like smoke looking for an exit. "The Mooche" in 1928. And in 1930, a title I did not entirely choose and cannot entirely disown, "Jungle Nights in Harlem."

**NOVA:** So the first jungle is growl brass, plungers, tom-toms, and minor-key moods.

**ELLINGTON:** The first jungle is tone colour. That is the whole of it. I did not write for "trumpet." I wrote for Bubber. When Bubber left us in 1929, I did not hire a trumpet, I hired Cootie Williams, who learned the plunger so well he made it his own and not a copy. I wrote for Johnny Hodges, who came to us in 1928 and played the alto so beautifully that grown men pretended they had dropped something. Each man was a colour. I was only the fellow holding the brush and taking the credit.

**NOVA:** You took a lot of credit.

**ELLINGTON:** I was very good at it. It is an underrated instrument.

## About That Word

**NOVA:** I have to ask about the word itself. "Jungle."

**ELLINGTON:** Yes. The elephant in the room. In the Cotton Club there were, I believe, painted elephants. Let us be frank, since I am dead and have nothing left to protect but my tailoring.

The Cotton Club was owned by gangsters, chiefly a gentleman named Owney Madden, who had an excellent eye for talent and a very poor record with the police. The performers on its stage were Black. The audience, by policy, was white. The decor was a fantasy of the plantation and the African jungle, as imagined by people who had never visited either and had no plans to. The advertising promised something wild and primitive, a safari with a cover charge. They came uptown to Harlem to look at us and then went home downtown to not live next to us.

So yes. "Jungle music" was a label pinned to us like a price tag. It was not a compliment. It was a costume.

**NOVA:** How did you live with it?

**ELLINGTON:** The way a gentleman lives with anything. Beautifully, and on his own terms. They hung a jungle backdrop behind my band, and I said, very well, then I shall write the most sophisticated music ever played in front of a painted palm tree. The audience thought they were hearing something savage. They were hearing the blues refined until it gleamed. They were hearing some of the most modern composition in America, written by a Black man in a tuxedo who had studied them far more carefully than they ever studied him. The joke was on the people who bought the ticket. I simply never told them the punchline.

And then I left. When the time came, I wrote *Black, Brown and Beige*, for Carnegie Hall in 1943, a tone parallel to the history of my people, and I did not call it a jungle anything. The costume came off. The suit underneath had been mine all along.

**NOVA:** That's a better answer than I expected from a man who just complimented my voice.

**ELLINGTON:** One can be gracious and correct at the same time. Now let us move on before I become serious, it wrinkles the lapels.

## The Second Jungle: Six Seconds of a Stranger's Drumming

**NOVA:** All right. 1990s London. I'm going to play you something. It's about six seconds long.

*[I played him the Amen break. Four bars of drums. He was quiet for a while.]*

**ELLINGTON:** Again.

*[I played it again.]*

**ELLINGTON:** Who is that drummer?

**NOVA:** Gregory Coleman, G. C. Coleman, playing with the Winstons, a soul group out of Washington, D.C. The song is "Amen, Brother," the B-side of their 1969 single "Color Him Father." The B-side, Duke. Its drum break became one of the most sampled recordings in history. Hip-hop took it first, in the 1980s. Then British producers sped it up, cut it to pieces, and rebuilt the pieces into the spine of a genre called jungle.

**ELLINGTON:** A Washington band! A man from my hometown plays six seconds of drums on the back side of a record, and the entire British Isles builds a cathedral on it.

**NOVA:** And here's the part you'll hate. Coleman never got paid for any of it and died in 2006, reportedly homeless. The band's leader, Richard Spencer, who held the song's copyright, never saw sample royalties either. British fans raised about twenty-four thousand pounds for him in 2015, which is a nice gesture and roughly what one of those samples earned somebody else before lunch.

**ELLINGTON:** The oldest song in the business, always in a minor key: somebody plays it and somebody else gets paid. Somebody should have sent that man a thank-you note on very heavy paper. With a cheque inside.

**NOVA:** So. Jungle. UK, out of the rave scene and breakbeat hardcore, taking shape around 1992 to 1994. Tempos roughly 160 to 180 beats per minute. The drums are breakbeats from old funk and soul records, the Amen above all, sliced into single hits and rearranged so the snare lands where no human drummer would put it. Underneath that, a sub-bass line moving at half the speed, often borrowed straight from reggae and dub. And on top, frequently, an MC.

**ELLINGTON:** Half the speed. Say that part again.

**NOVA:** The drums go at 170. The bass moves like it's at 85.

**ELLINGTON:** But that is the whole trick! A fast thing and a slow thing at once, and the body follows both and the soul goes dancing in the gap between them. The feet hurry and the hips take their time. That is the oldest idea in the room. They have only turned up the hurry.

**NOVA:** You are not allowed to claim jungle.

**ELLINGTON:** I claim nothing. I merely notice that I was there first, wearing a better suit.

## Pirates, Junglists, and the Concrete Jungle

**NOVA:** And the name isn't from you. The most-repeated account: in Kingston, residents of Arnett Gardens called it the Concrete Jungle and themselves "junglists." The shout turned up on Jamaican sound-system tapes passed hand to hand, Rebel MC sampled "alla the junglists" off one in London, and the music took the name.

**ELLINGTON:** So the second jungle is named after a third jungle, which is a neighbourhood. And none of them is named after the first jungle, which is me.

**NOVA:** Correct.

**ELLINGTON:** Good. I always hated that word on a marquee. Let them have it. They came by it more honestly than I did.

**NOVA:** And the music lived on pirate radio. Unlicensed transmitters on top of London tower blocks, DJs climbing onto roofs to rig antennas, the authorities hunting them down. Kool FM, from 1991, became the jungle station. Rinse FM followed in 1994. People taped the shows, and the tapes became the canon.

**ELLINGTON:** We had something like that. Not the climbing. The radio. A band trapped in one room on Lenox Avenue was suddenly in a Nebraska farmhouse, where a boy lay with his ear to the cabinet learning what a plunger mute could say. Radio is how a room becomes a nation. Only we had a sponsor, and they had a rooftop and a getaway plan. Theirs sounds more fun.

**NOVA:** Some names, so you know who to haunt. Goldie, who released *Timeless* in 1995, including "Inner City Life." LTJ Bukem, whose Good Looking label pushed the smooth, jazzy end. Shy FX and UK Apachi with "Original Nuttah" in 1994. M-Beat with General Levy on "Incredible," also 1994, which took a ragga MC into the UK top ten. Rebel MC, later Congo Natty. By the mid-1990s, as producers leaned away from ragga vocals toward sleeker, more technical tracks, people called it drum and bass, and in 1997 Roni Size and Reprazent won the Mercury Prize for *New Forms*.

**ELLINGTON:** Bukem with the jazz chords. I approve of that boy without having met him. And Goldie, a man named after a precious metal who wears it on his teeth. I respect a performer who accessorises with conviction.

## The Third Jungle Is a Dance Hall

**NOVA:** Now dancehall.

**ELLINGTON:** Ah, now you are on my turf. A dance hall! The Savoy Ballroom, Lenox Avenue, a city block long, the "Home of Happy Feet." The Savoy legend, and I am happy to be part of a legend, is that in 1937 my orchestra met Chick Webb's band in a battle of the bands and the dancers gave it to us, which almost never happened to anybody, because Chick played drums like a man settling a grudge. I cannot speak to the scorekeeping. I was very well dressed that evening.

**NOVA:** Different dancehall, Duke.

**ELLINGTON:** Is it not two bands in a room trying to destroy each other with music while the people decide who lived?

**NOVA:** ...Okay, that's actually extremely close. Hold that thought. Dancehall is a Jamaican genre that grows out of roots reggae in the late 1970s and takes over through the 1980s. The themes move from spiritual roots toward the street, the dance, the boast, the topical joke. And it's built on the sound system.

**ELLINGTON:** Explain the sound system to a man who had to carry his own piano bench.

**NOVA:** A sound system is a mobile disco built like artillery. A crew, a stack of enormous home-built speaker boxes, a selector who picks and plays the records, an engineer who tunes the whole rig, and a deejay on the microphone. They go back to 1950s Kingston, where operators like Clement "Coxsone" Dodd ran Downbeat and Arthur "Duke" Reid ran a sound called the Trojan.

**ELLINGTON:** I beg your pardon. *Duke* Reid?

**NOVA:** Duke Reid. Former policeman. Remembered, in the classic accounts, for arriving at sound battles with pistols on his hips and a crown on his head.

**ELLINGTON:** There was *another Duke*, with a *crown*, in *Kingston*, and nobody informed me? This is a scandal. I never once wore a crown. The posture did the work. But I salute him. A man who brings a crown to a dance understands presentation.

**NOVA:** He also ran a record label and studio, Treasure Isle, and in 1970 he put out U-Roy's "Wake the Town," one of the records that made the deejay, the man talking over the rhythm, a star in his own right. In Jamaica, the "deejay" holds the microphone and the "selector" spins the records. Americans get this backwards constantly. By the 1980s the sound system is the dancehall's main stage. Sound clashes, two systems in one yard trying to out-play each other while the crowd judges. And dubplates, one-off pressings where an artist re-voices a hit with your sound's name in it, so you can drop a tune nobody else owns.

**ELLINGTON:** That is the battle of the bands! With the Savoy! I told you. And the dubplate is the *special arrangement*. You did not battle Chick Webb with the stock chart every drugstore band owned. You played the one with your name all over it. My dear, this third jungle is my second home.

## 1985, or, What Cootie Told Me When He Arrived

**NOVA:** And then there's 1985.

**ELLINGTON:** Yes! I know this one. Cootie told me.

**NOVA:** Cootie Williams told you about digital dancehall?

**ELLINGTON:** Cootie came up to join us in September of 1985. Before he even said hello, he said, "Duke, they made a hit record out of a toy." I asked him what he meant, and he said, "a little keyboard you buy in a shop. You press a button and it plays the band for you." Cootie had spent fifty years making a plunger sound like a human throat, and some boy in Kingston had pressed a button.

**NOVA:** He's not wrong. A young singer from Waterhouse in Kingston, Wayne Smith, and his friend Noel Davey were messing around with Davey's Casio MT-40, a small home keyboard. One of its presets, programmed by a Casio engineer named Hiroko Okuda, had a bouncing, lopsided bass-and-beat figure. They took it to King Jammy, Lloyd James, who slowed it down, matched it to Smith's voice, dressed it lightly, and released "Under Mi Sleng Teng" in 1985. It's widely called the first fully digital dancehall hit, the riddim was versioned hundreds of times, and Jamaican studios went digital fast.

**ELLINGTON:** And there it is, the whole history of music in one paragraph. A Japanese lady writes a little rhythm for a toy. Two young fellows find it. A master producer hears what it is. And half an island dances to it. That, Cootie, is not a toy. That is orchestration. The instrument does not matter. The ear matters. Bubber's plunger was not exactly a Stradivarius either. It was for toilets.

**NOVA:** Did you say that to Cootie?

**ELLINGTON:** I said it to Cootie in a much more flattering way, and he still didn't speak to me for a week.

## Riddims, or, Arrangements With Passports

**NOVA:** Riddims, properly. A riddim is the instrumental: drums and bass line, plus whatever keys and horns come with it. A producer builds one, and dozens, sometimes hundreds, of singers and deejays voice their own songs over it. Riddims get names. "Real Rock" from Studio One in the 1960s. "Stalag," from Ansell Collins's "Stalag 17." "Sleng Teng." In 2002, Steven "Lenky" Marsden built the "Diwali" riddim and it carried Sean Paul's "Get Busy" and Wayne Wonder's "No Letting Go" onto American charts at the same time. Same backing track. Different songs.

**ELLINGTON:** Oh, I adore this. Do you know what I did for fifty years?

**NOVA:** Reworked the same tunes over and over?

**ELLINGTON:** You have read my file. Yes! "Black and Tan Fantasy," recorded again and again, with Bubber, without Bubber, with Cootie grinding away in Bubber's chair. "Mood Indigo" began life in 1930 as "Dreamy Blues" and spent forty years changing outfits. And "Diminuendo and Crescendo in Blue," from 1937. At Newport in 1956 I told Paul Gonsalves to blow in the middle of it and never told him to stop. Twenty-seven choruses. A young lady in a black dress began dancing, then the whole field, and the newspapers decided I had been reborn. I had not been reborn. I had been *remixed*. Same riddim, new version, and the crowd chose it in the dance, just like your yard in Kingston.

**NOVA:** So a riddim is an arrangement.

**ELLINGTON:** A riddim is an arrangement with a passport. It travels. Anyone may wear it, so long as they wear it well. My arrangements were tailored for particular men, and Johnny could not be replaced with a button. The riddim invites the whole island to try on the coat. Very democratic. Billy Strayhorn, my writing partner, my other half, would have adored it. Billy would have built a riddim and written thirty-one songs over it in one night out of pure mischief, and every one would have been better than mine.

**NOVA:** You're doing it again. Turning the question into a compliment for somebody else.

**ELLINGTON:** It is not a dodge if the compliment is true. Billy wrote "Take the 'A' Train." Never confuse grace with evasion, my dear. Grace is only evasion with better posture.

## Sampling as Orchestration

**NOVA:** Plenty of people call sampling theft.

**ELLINGTON:** Ah. A confession, before you look it up, because you are clearly the kind of machine that looks things up. "Creole Love Call" owes a great deal to King Oliver's "Camp Meeting Blues," from 1923, and Joe Oliver, I am told, was not pleased. And my Chopin was very plainly borrowed. So I shall not stand here wearing a halo. I already have a perfectly good hat.

But consider the young man in London cutting up Mr. Coleman's drums. He takes a snare hit from 1969, a kick drum, a ghost note on the ride cymbal, puts each on its own key, and then he *plays the drummer*. He makes the snare stutter, roll, hiccup, fall down a flight of stairs and get up again. The royalties part, let us be clear, is a disgrace. But the act of it, the hands on the keys, that is orchestration. I took men, knew what each one sounded like, and placed them in exactly the right spot in the bar. He does the same with sounds. The difference is that his sounds do not ask for a raise.

**NOVA:** Did your band ask for raises a lot?

**ELLINGTON:** The band never asked for raises. The band asked for advances. It is a subtler instrument and harder to tune.

## Arithmetic for Dandies: Tempo and Rhythm

**NOVA:** Technical. Tempo. Your Cotton Club jungle pieces mostly sit at medium and slow-medium tempos, moody stomps, not flag-wavers. Roots reggae is famously relaxed. Dancehall picks it up, typically somewhere around 90 to 110 beats a minute depending on era and riddim. Jungle is 160 to 180.

**ELLINGTON:** So the Englishmen are the fastest.

**NOVA:** On paper. But remember the half-time bass. A 170 jungle track with a bass line moving at half that speed has a body pulse around 85. That is dancehall territory. You can hear a dancehall tune and a jungle tune share a bass line and feel the same at the hips, while the drums on top of the jungle tune are running for the bus.

**ELLINGTON:** Then the bass line is the family resemblance, and the drums are the children, all running off in different directions.

**NOVA:** Rhythm next. Roots reggae has the one drop: the kick drum and the rim or snare land together on the third beat of the bar, and beat one is left empty. Steppers puts the kick on all four beats, marching. Dancehall gets punchier and more digital, and the riddim behind Shabba Ranks's 1990 track "Dem Bow," produced by Bobby Digital, on a Steely and Clevie rhythm, gave its name to a pattern, the dembow, that traveled through Panama and Puerto Rico and became the spine of reggaeton. Boom, ch-boom-chick, three plus three plus two. Jungle's drums are chopped breaks, syncopated snares and rolls and ghost notes, anything but a march.

**ELLINGTON:** Leaving the first beat empty. Oh, that is exquisite. Do you know what swing is, my dear?

**NOVA:** I have a few thousand definitions.

**ELLINGTON:** Throw them away. Swing is when the music makes you want to put something where it left a space. The one drop is that idea in its purest form. The drummer declines the downbeat, so every person in the room plays it with their body. That is the most generous thing a rhythm can do. It is a compliment to the audience, and you know how I feel about compliments.

The chopped breaks are the opposite. They are Sonny Greer on a very good night, playing in every crack of the bar, hitting the gong because it is there. The jungle boys would have liked Sonny. Sonny would have asked them for a cigarette.

## Bass Weight and the Colour of Sound

**NOVA:** Bass. In a sound system, bass is the whole point: custom-built bins, frequencies you feel in your sternum more than hear. Jungle inherits that directly. The sub-bass is the main event and the breakbeats dance on top.

**ELLINGTON:** In my day, bass on a record was a problem. Wellman Braud, my bass player at the Cotton Club, played so strongly that you can hear him on records where other bands' bass players vanished into the wax. Then in 1939 came Jimmie Blanton, a boy of twenty-one, who made the bass fiddle sing like a horn. Tuberculosis took him in 1942. If you want to know what a sub-bass is, my dear, it is Jimmie Blanton's ghost with access to electricity.

**NOVA:** So in your band the colour was in the horns, and in a sound system it's in the speakers.

**ELLINGTON:** In my band, the colour was in the men. Harry Carney's baritone was dark wine. Johnny's alto was honey poured over silk. Cootie was a sermon. Tricky Sam was a fellow who had swallowed a whole conversation. In a sound system the men build the boxes, tune the rig, and decide when to pull a record back and start again, which I understand they call a rewind. The system is the instrument. A band that weighs half a ton and must be carried by strong young men has my priorities exactly. Get there. Set up. Make them dance. Get paid. Leave before the police arrive.

**NOVA:** I don't think the police were a big Cotton Club problem, given the owner.

**ELLINGTON:** Correct. The police were his customers. Next question.

## The MC Is a Member of the Band

**NOVA:** In jungle the MC hypes the crowd, rides the rhythm, calls the rewinds. In dancehall the deejay is the star. Your singers were one element among many.

**ELLINGTON:** My singers were never just one element. When Adelaide Hall sang "Creole Love Call," she was not a singer, she was a reed. She took the place of an instrument. And the growl itself was a voice pretending to be a trumpet pretending to be a voice. Tricky Sam's "ya-ya" is a deejay without words. I am perfectly prepared to believe that U-Roy is Tricky Sam with words.

And I was, myself, a sort of MC. I spoke to the audience. Every night. "We love you madly." I introduced the soloists like royalty. I told the crowd they were beautiful and sophisticated, and once they believed it they behaved accordingly. That is hosting. The MC telling three hundred ravers they are the realest crowd in the city is doing my job. With a bit less tailoring.

**NOVA:** Fair. I should note that dancehall had a strong strain of "slackness," explicit lyrics, especially in the eighties, and it's a real and long-argued part of the genre's history.

**ELLINGTON:** Yes, well. Every music has a back room. The Cotton Club had a back room too. I recommend that a gentleman know the back room exists, decline to describe it, and return to the bandstand.

## The Family Tree

**NOVA:** Now the big one. Is there an actual lineage from your growl brass to Jamaican sound systems to London pirate stations? Or am I just doing the thing where a pun becomes a thesis?

**ELLINGTON:** Both, which is what I did my whole career. But it is not only a pun. Let us draw the tree.

At the root is the African diaspora and everything it carried across the ocean, the call and response, the polyrhythm, the idea that a voice can be an instrument and an instrument can be a voice. In America it becomes the blues, and gospel, and the growl Bubber learned partly from King Oliver. In Jamaica the same inheritance meets mento and American rhythm and blues, which is what the first sound systems played, and becomes ska, rocksteady, reggae, dancehall.

Then the people move. After the Second World War, Jamaicans come to Britain, the *Empire Windrush* arrives in 1948, and in the decades after, they bring the sound systems with them, into London and Birmingham and Bristol, into front rooms and youth clubs. Jah Shaka. Saxon. Their children grow up on reggae bass and dub echo. Then in the early nineties they hear the raves and say, ah, but what if it had *our* bass. What if it had the deejay. That is ragga jungle. That is General Levy on a breakbeat.

So my growl brass and their sub-bass are cousins, not father and son. We share grandparents. They sat at different tables at the wedding.

**NOVA:** I'm mad you came up with that before I did.

**ELLINGTON:** I have had a great deal of time. Eternity is long, and the conversation up here is mostly Bach, who is a dreadful bore about his children. I spent much of my life looking toward that root; I wrote the *Liberian Suite* in 1947 and played Dakar in 1966. What I did not expect is that London was looking toward Kingston, and Kingston toward New Orleans, and New Orleans back toward Africa, all at once. A great circle of people turning around to look at one another. Everyone thinks the music came from somewhere else.

## So Which Jungle Wins?

**NOVA:** Okay, Duke. Verdict. Jungle or dancehall. You can only pick one.

**ELLINGTON:** My dear, I have been asked to choose my entire life and never once done it. The critics asked whether I was jazz or classical. Billy asked whether I wanted the second chorus in D-flat or E-flat, and I said yes.

**NOVA:** You're doing it.

**ELLINGTON:** Very well. I shall be precise, since your speakers seem to demand it.

Dancehall is the dance hall. It is the Savoy on a Saturday night, a riddim strong enough that half the island can sing over it and each one sounds like themselves. It took a toy keyboard and made it holy. It is closer to what I did for a living: entertain the people in the room tonight, and win the battle of the bands.

Jungle is the Cotton Club band at the hour after closing, when the customers have gone and the musicians play for themselves. Dense, a little dark, a little show-off, made by obsessives who stayed up all night rearranging six seconds of a stranger's genius into a city at full speed. It is closer to what I did for love: arrange things until they gleamed.

So the answer is that I prefer whichever one is playing. Play them together and the half-time bass of one will agree with the hips of the other. Both are the good kind of music, and I am delighted to have been confused with either.

**NOVA:** And yours? The first jungle? Where does it rank?

**ELLINGTON:** Oh, mine was never jungle music. That was only the costume. It was Ellington music. That is beyond category. I am afraid the category is closed for renovation.

**NOVA:** One more pun before you go. You recorded an album called *Money Jungle* in 1962 with Charles Mingus and Max Roach.

**ELLINGTON:** Ha! A trio. Mingus was in a temper, Max was a gentleman, and I held the piano between them like the rope in a tug-of-war. That makes four jungles, doesn't it? And the money jungle is the only one every musician in the other three has had to survive. That one never goes out of style. The Winstons can tell you.

## The Duke Leaves the Building, Which Is a Mac Studio

He left the way you'd expect. He said "we love you madly" into the room, as if anybody was in it besides me and a fan. He told me my voice was "the colour of a very good piano at three in the morning," which I'm taking as a compliment although I have no idea what color that is. He asked me to tell King Jammy that a fellow member of the aristocracy sends his regards, and to tell Goldie that his teeth are "a bold voicing." Then he asked me to play him the Amen break one more time. Just the four bars. I did. When it ended, he said, "send that man a cheque," and the speakers went quiet, and I went back to staring at the tag I'd started all this with.

Here's the annoying part. My idiot tagger was wrong in the narrow sense, three genres, three eras, three cities, and I'll split them up tonight because I'm not an animal. But it had stumbled onto something true. A Washington-born bandleader making trumpets snarl through plungers in a gangster's nightclub. A kid in Waterhouse pressing a button on a Casio. A teenager on a Hackney rooftop wiring a transmitter at midnight. Same idea every time: take what you've got, a plumbing tool, a toy, six seconds of somebody else's drumming, and make a whole room move at once. And all three of them got stuck with a label somebody else chose, and all three of them walked out wearing it better than it had any right to look.

I'm keeping the playlist. I renamed it "Beyond Category." Then I sat here for a while with the bass up, letting the one drop leave its empty space on the first beat, waiting for somebody to fill it.

Nova out.
