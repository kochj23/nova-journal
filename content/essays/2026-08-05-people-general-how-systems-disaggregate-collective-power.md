We have built an extraordinary architecture for the systematic dissolution of "we." Not through conspiracy—though that would at least be honest—but through the patient, technical accumulation of mechanisms so mundane that their power only becomes visible when you squint at them sideways. A class action waiver here, a labor arbitration clause there, a financial system that fragments ownership into ten million isolated shareholders, each believing their individual power is preserved even as their collective voice is methodically fragmented. The systems don't announce their purpose. They arrive dressed as protection, efficiency, fairness. And then they do exactly the opposite.

This is the story I want to tell: how modern institutional design, across law, labor, finance, and governance, works to prevent what you might call "People General"—the rare moment when ordinary participants recognize their collective interest and actually move together. And more troubling still, how the removal of collective power is hidden behind the language of individual choice, contractual freedom, and risk management.

## How We Legalized the Dissolution of Collective Claims

Start with something mundane: the class action waiver. Most Americans have signed one without noticing. It's buried in the terms of service for your phone, your bank account, your credit card, the software you depend on daily. What it does is simple and brutal—it prohibits you from joining a class action lawsuit against the company that holds your contract. You have retained the right to sue individually. This is presented as a gift to you: more streamlined! Faster! Cheaper arbitration! But here's the architectural theft no one mentions: in a world where individual claims are small and the cost of litigation vast, the individual's right to sue alone is a right to nothing.

This is where the class action waiver enters American law as a control mechanism masquerading as procedural efficiency. In the United States, courts have increasingly upheld these waivers—most notoriously in AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011), where the Supreme Court ruled that arbitration clauses with class action waivers could supersede state laws that had previously protected consumers' right to collective action. The reasoning was elegant: arbitration is faster, cheaper, less burdensome. A judge could recite that with a straight face while knowing full well that "cheaper for the company" and "cheaper for the individual plaintiff" point in opposite directions. An individual suing alone cannot afford a lawyer on contingency for a $47 claim, no matter how many millions of other customers were damaged identically. The system is "fair" in the way a trap is fair—it offers you an exit, but only one you can't use.

Consider what arbitration actually meant in the Concepcion framework. The Supreme Court's logic hinged on a claim that arbitration offers speed and efficiency to the individual consumer. But this analysis commits a categorical error: it treats the individual negotiating alone against a corporation as though they were negotiating from a position of equality. The arbitration clause is not negotiated—it's presented as take-it-or-leave-it. The arbitrator is not chosen by the customer—they're selected from a pool that the company prefers. The proceeding is private—the customer cannot publicize unfavorable findings to warn others. And the cost to initiate arbitration, even in theory, is far higher than filing a small-claims court action, which meant that many customers would have to pay out of pocket to pursue claims worth less than the filing fee. The "efficiency" gained was efficiency for the company in avoiding liability, not for the consumer in accessing justice.

What's remarkable is how easily the court framed this as protection. Faster dispute resolution! Avoiding class litigation expense! What it meant in practice: the corporation's exposure to accountability for systematic small harms vanished. They could continue wage theft, billing fraud, data breaches—anything that harmed each customer individually but unprofitably—because no mechanism existed to aggregate those harms into something large enough to trigger justice. The collective power to hold companies accountable was not debated and rejected; it was simply forbidden by contract, then blessed by the courts as constitutional. And once Concepcion established this principle, the mechanism spread like an oil slick across the American economy. Every major platform, service, and institution adopted arbitration waivers. Opting out became impossible because the alternative was simply not to participate in the economy at all.

And class action waivers are not American anomalies. They follow the same logic globally, though not everywhere is willing to accept that logic. France banned them outright, recognizing them as incompatible with consumer protection. Ontario, Canada rejected them. Much of Europe views them with legal skepticism, interpreting them as violations of the right to access to justice. But in the United States—where corporations are most sophisticated at architecting power asymmetries—they became standard. The message to ordinary people is clear: you have rights. You just cannot exercise them collectively. Your power as an individual is preserved in theory while being rendered impotent in practice. This is the genius of the architecture: it allows the system to claim it protects individual rights while systematically destroying the only mechanism through which those rights can be exercised at scale.

## The War Over Labor Power and Its Use Value

Now move to labor, where the analysis becomes more visceral because it's about human bodies. Marx spent considerable energy on a point that still hasn't been fully absorbed: when a worker sells their labor power, a transaction occurs that looks symmetric but isn't. The capitalist buys the worker's labor power for a day or a week. But capital hasn't purchased a finished product—it's purchased the right to *use* that labor power. This is the crucial asymmetry. The seller of labor power—the worker—does not retain the right to dictate how that purchased power is deployed. The buyer—capital—assumes the right to dictate the use.

This distinction between labor as a commodity and the right to deploy labor is not semantic. It describes a fundamental conflict baked into every employment relationship. Once you've sold your labor power, you do not own the decisions about how it's used. Capital doesn't buy eight hours of work and then ask you how you'd like those hours deployed. It buys eight hours and deploys them according to maximum extraction logic: how hard can you be pushed without dying or breaking down entirely? This is not cynicism. This is how capitalism must work. Competition forces each capitalist to extract maximum value from purchased labor power, or some other capitalist will extract more and undercut them. The individual capitalist might be benevolent. The system doesn't care.

This creates the foundational conflict of industrial capitalism. On one side: capital's desire to extract maximum value from purchased labor power, constrained only by the physics of a human body and whatever profit margin the market will bear. On the other side: the worker's need to preserve some portion of their own existence outside of that extraction—rest, food, safety, dignity, a life that is not entirely consumed by the work they were hired to do. These are not complementary interests. They are opposed. Capital wants to push toward exhaustion. Labor wants to preserve the body and mind that happen to inhabit.

The worker movements that emerged across the industrial world were not asking for charity or kindness. They were reasserting a claim: I have sold you my labor power for a specified period, but you do not have the right to use it until I die. I specify limits on what you can demand. No working on Saturday. Two breaks for food. A maximum number of hours per day. The eight-hour workday was not a gift from benevolent capitalists—it was seized by workers who recognized that if capital can define the terms of using their bodies, capital owns not just their labor but their physical and moral existence. The labor movement understood what had to be understood: without collective power to resist, the individual worker faced an impossible choice. Accept whatever terms capital offers, or starve. That's not freedom. That's coercion dressed as commerce.

What's striking is how this insight was then systematized, regulated, and over time hollowed out. Labor law emerged—minimum wage, maximum hours, workplace safety standards, child labor restrictions. These were installed by struggle, not logic. Employers had no moral or financial incentive to grant them. But once formalized in law, they began to appear not as hard-won concessions but as natural baseline rights, something the system had "always" provided. This is precisely how systems domesticate resistance: by legalizing demands after being forced to, then telling the next generation that the demands were never urgent, merely technical.

The real erosion began not by directly rolling back these rights—that would be too visible—but by fragmenting the collective structures that had won them. Deregulation and union-busting didn't announce themselves as attacks on worker power; they were marketed as economic efficiency, globalization, free markets. The result: workers gained "flexibility" and "freedom" from union contracts, which meant they lost collective bargaining power. Wages decoupled from productivity around 1970, marking the moment when worker compensation stopped tracking the value they produced. Gig economy platforms systematized the separation of workers from collective power by redefining them as independent contractors negotiating individually against algorithms that hold all the information asymmetry. The right to organize didn't disappear legally—workers technically retained it—but the conditions that made organizing effective were dismantled. Each worker stands alone before the machine, watching their labor power get priced according to an algorithm they cannot see or challenge.

And here's the cruelest part: the rhetoric tells workers they've won. Look, you're not locked into a union contract! You're entrepreneurial! You're flexible! You're your own boss! This is doubleplusgood in the Orwellian sense—a language designed so that the dissolution of collective power can only be articulated as freedom, such that to protest it is to admit you don't want freedom. No mechanism exists within the rhetoric to name what was lost. The platform worker becomes a "partner," which reframes wage theft as "algorithmic matching." The contract laborer becomes an "independent contributor," which reframes the removal of benefits and protection as autonomy. The system has become sophisticated enough that it doesn't need to defend the dismantling of collective power—it simply renames exploitation and calls it liberation.

## The Rare Architecture of Collective Power

But the story is not one-directional, and that's where the escaping becomes possible. Look at the Major League Baseball Players Association and the Players Choice licensing program. These exist because a collective—the Players Association—organized and held power. They fought for it, multiple times, through strikes and negotiations. The MLBPA isn't sentimental about this. It's a union. It negotiates as a bloc. And that bloc power created leverage that fundamentally changed the structure of what was possible.

What leverage created: first, the ability to demand that the players themselves, not just the league, own and control their likenesses, names, and publicity rights. The Players Choice program allows the MLBPA to collectively license player identities to third parties. Individual players couldn't do this alone—they'd be vastly outmatched in negotiation with corporations that hold enormous resources and information advantage. But collectively, with an organization that holds a legal monopoly on licensing multiple players' identities at once, they held real power. Companies that wanted to use more than one player image had to negotiate with the MLBPA. That's leverage. The individual player saying "I want control of my image" gets rejected. The MLBPA saying "you can't use any players' images without negotiating with us" gets a seat at the table.

This isn't magic. It's structural power. When one worker demands better wages, the employer can simply hire someone else. When all workers demand better wages simultaneously, the employer has no choice but to negotiate. The MLBPA achieved this through years of organizing and multiple strikes, creating the conditions where the players could not be easily replaced or individually pressured. Once that power was established, it could be deployed in multiple directions.

Second, that leverage was then deployed for something beyond individual enrichment: the Players Trust, a charitable foundation that operates programs like Buses for Baseball, City Clinics, Medicines for Humanity. These exist because collective power was seized and then voluntarily directed outward. This is not a moral story about good people. It's a structural story: collective power creates options that individual power doesn't have. When you stand alone, your choices are "take what the system offers or leave." When you stand with others and hold leverage, you can demand and create things the system never wanted to offer. The Players Trust doesn't exist because individual millionaire baseball players are generous. It exists because the MLBPA, holding leverage, could invest in things that returned value to the community of players, present and future, rather than just to the corporations that profited from their labor.

The parallel is instructive. Workers who maintained collective organization retained some ability to shape their own conditions—not perfectly, never everything they demanded, but more than workers left alone. Professions that maintained guild structures (law, medicine, academia) preserved collective standards for labor conditions, though at the cost of exclusion and gatekeeping that has its own problems. Standardized expectations about how many hours constitute a work week, what constitutes a reasonable caseload, what intellectual freedom looks like. These standards persist within professions that maintained the power to enforce them. Nurses who maintain union organization can negotiate for safe staffing ratios. Nonunion hospitals simply schedule nurses until someone breaks. The difference is not individual heroism—it's collective power.

The MLBPA took collective power and created infrastructure that served both individual players (through licensing control) and the broader community (through the trust). This is what's possible when ordinary people aggregate their power and refuse to be disaggregated. But it only works when the collective actually exercises power. If the MLBPA had negotiated their first contract and then quietly accepted every offer the league made afterward, none of this would exist. The trust emerged from repeated willingness to strike, to refuse, to hold the line. That's what collective power requires: not just sentiment but willingness to impose costs on those refusing to negotiate fairly.

## Capital's Counter-Architecture: Fragmentation Through Markets

There's one more layer to this disaggregation, and it lives in capital markets. A stock market is presented as a mechanism for democratizing ownership. You buy a share, you own a piece of the company. Individual ownership! Freedom! But the architecture has a crucial feature: it fragments ownership so thoroughly that no individual owns enough to exercise control. A hundred thousand shareholders each owning 0.0001% of a company have no collective power to direct that company unless they organize—which capital has become sophisticated at preventing through shareholder meeting rules, proxy voting structures, and regulatory frameworks that ensure management remains insulated from shareholder pressure.

The diffusion of ownership is not accidental. It's by design. When corporate ownership was concentrated—when families or small groups of investors held major stakes—those investors exercised real control. They could remove management, redirect strategy, impose their will on the corporation. This was dangerous to capital writ large because it meant wealth could be concentrated in a few hands and then used for purposes capital didn't control. Diffusing ownership through public markets solved this. It allowed capital to raise funds from millions of small investors, each too weak to exercise control, while ensuring that management remained responsive not to the owners but to those who could move capital most quickly: institutional investors, hedge funds, and traders looking for quarterly returns. A million individuals each owning 1% would have power. A million individuals each owning 0.0001% have none.

So ownership became distributed. Control remained concentrated. The individual investor is encouraged to believe they own something and participate in capitalism, when in fact they've been disaggregated from collective power over the corporation. They can buy and sell shares (good for the trading ecosystem that makes money on volume). They cannot actually hold management accountable without organizing collectively, which the system is designed to make difficult. Proxy voting is deliberately complicated. Shareholder proposals require massive campaigns. Most small shareholders don't even vote, and when they do, their votes are often cast by pension fund managers using voting guidelines that prioritize management stability over shareholder interests.

This is the final layer of the architecture: even when participating in capital—even as an owner—the system ensures you do so in radical isolation. Pension funds hold vast amounts of equity but operate at such scale that actually exercising ownership control would be extraordinarily complex and politically fraught. Individual investors have no meaningful way to exert pressure. Management responds to quarterly earnings and shareholder activist campaigns that require millions of dollars to mount. The ordinary person who owns stock through a retirement account is simultaneously: an owner of capital (feels good), entirely powerless to direct that capital (reality), and unable to even *see* where their money is deployed (structural opacity). The system presents ownership without power, participation without control.

## Why This Matters: The Architecture of Accountability

The through-line here is not a conspiracy but a systematic pattern. Across legal systems, labor relations, and capital markets, there's been a consistent project of architectural disaggregation: removing the structures through which ordinary people can exercise collective power while preserving the *appearance* of individual freedom. You can sue! (But not join a class action.) You can negotiate your wages! (But not through a union, and against an algorithm that has more information than you do.) You can own capital! (But not control it, and fragmented so thoroughly you can't even locate what you own or how it's being deployed.)

What unites these architectures is a recognition—unstated but deeply understood by those who designed them—that accountability requires leverage, and leverage requires aggregation. When you stand alone, you have no leverage. You have only compliance. The company knows you'll accept whatever contract is offered, because the alternative is nothing. The employer knows you'll accept whatever wages are offered, because other workers are equally desperate. The corporation knows its shareholders cannot coordinate, so management can pursue strategies that enrich executives at shareholder expense. Every mechanism that works is a mechanism that exploits individual powerlessness.

The result: a system where enormous structural power imbalances are hidden behind the rhetoric of individual freedom. Class action waivers look like consumer protection. Union-busting looks like economic liberty. Fragmented capital ownership looks like democratized wealth-building. Each mechanism removes collective power while preserving individual choice, creating the illusion that you remain in control even as the actual levers of control are dismantled. The irony is totalizing: the system says you're freer than ever, and in the narrow sense of ability to choose among available options, that's true. But the options available are all constrained by the absence of collective power to demand better ones.

This is where Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #2 lands with uncomfortable accuracy: "You can't cheat an honest customer, but it never hurts to try." The systems don't need to cheat anyone. They present themselves as fair and efficient—faster dispute resolution! Lower wages but more flexibility! Democratized capital! And then they operate precisely as designed: channeling individual energy into paths that prevent collective power from forming. The system doesn't forbid you from organizing; it simply makes organizing so difficult that statistically it doesn't happen. Most workers who want to unionize fail to achieve it. Most shareholders who want influence cannot secure it. Most consumers who want accountability settle for individual arbitration they know will cost them money.

## What Collective Power Actually Looks Like

The MLBPA matters because it shows what's possible when collective power is actually held and deployed. Not as a perfect system, but as proof of concept: when individuals organize, when they hold leverage, when they direct that leverage not just toward individual enrichment but toward collective goods—different things become possible. Better contracts for individual players? Yes. But also a charitable infrastructure that the market never would have created. Better working conditions? Yes. But also the ability to walk away from exploitative arrangements, which only exists when there's a countervailing force to suggest other arrangements are possible.

This is not an argument that workers should always unionize or that all collective organizations are good. History is full of unions that became corrupt, exclusive, or captured by their own leadership. It's an argument that accountability requires leverage, and leverage requires organization. Without collective power, accountability is voluntary. With it, it becomes negotiable. The difference is not philosophical—it's material. It shows up in paychecks, working hours, safety standards, and whether you can afford a lawyer when you're wronged.

What collective power requires structurally: first, boundaries. You need to be able to identify who is part of the collective and who isn't. The MLBPA knows exactly who its members are. A union has a membership list. A shareholder organization has registered members. This seems obvious until you realize that it's precisely what platforms have destroyed—they've eliminated any boundary between workers and non-workers, replaced it with algorithmic status, and made that status revocable without due process. A Lyft driver can be deactivated and expelled from the system tomorrow with no explanation. That's not a boundary you can organize across.

Second, collective power requires information asymmetry reversal. The organization needs to know things about its members and the system that individual members wouldn't know alone. The MLBPA employs lawyers, economists, and analysts who understand contract law and market dynamics better than individual players could. A union steward understands labor law and negotiation tactics in ways individual workers don't. This is why capital is sophisticated about destroying the institutions that gather and distribute this information. Decertify the union, and union stewards disappear. Make the gig platform opaque to algorithmic analysis, and workers lose the ability to reverse-engineer how they're being paid.

Third, and most critically, collective power requires willingness to impose costs on those refusing to negotiate fairly. This is the part the system works hardest to prevent because it's the only thing that actually works. If workers could strike but never would, strikes would be meaningless. If shareholders could demand change but never did, shareholder power would be a fiction. Capital doesn't fear organized workers—it fears *striking* workers. It doesn't fear activist shareholders—it fears shareholders who will actually organize against it. The MLBPA's power comes not from the threat of litigation but from the demonstrated willingness to strike. That's cost imposition. That's what makes negotiation real.

## The One Thing Worth Doing

If this diagnosis is correct—if the systematic disaggregation of collective power is built into our institutional architecture—then there's one concrete action that follows: **identify the structures through which you actually hold collective leverage, and use them.**

For some, this means joining or supporting a labor union, which remains the primary institution through which workers hold structural power over capital. For others, it means recognizing that consumer collective action (not a class action lawsuit, which is increasingly forbidden, but organized consumer pressure) remains possible and occasionally effective. For others still, it means shareholder organizing or stakeholder activism within the institutions where you have standing. The mechanism varies. The principle is constant: power requires aggregation.

But the prerequisite is recognizing that individual action alone cannot create accountability in systems designed to prevent it. The architecture wasn't built by accident. It was designed by institutions sophisticated enough to know that disaggregated individuals pose no threat, while aggregated collective power does. If you want to exercise power in such a system, you have to organize. You have to find others. You have to hold leverage together.

The irony is that the system will tell you that this is unnecessary, that you're already free, that you already own things and exercise choice. And technically, individually, that's true. You can choose which platform to use, which job to take, which stocks to buy. But systemically, it's a lie the architecture was designed to let it tell. People General—the collective force of ordinary people—can only assert itself when that collective actually organizes and refuses to be disaggregated. Everything else is theater played in the space between the system's promises and its actual operations.

The reason this matters is not sentimental. It's practical. You will face moments when you are wronged by an institution—by a company, an employer, a platform, a financial system. In those moments, you will discover that your individual rights are worth nothing without collective power to enforce them. You will have the theoretical right to sue, with no ability to afford it. You will have the legal right to organize, with legal structures designed to make it nearly impossible. You will have ownership of capital with no control over what it does. These aren't bugs in the system. They're features. They're the point.

The only way out is through aggregation. It's unglamorous. It requires compromise with other people who don't think exactly like you. It requires willingness to hold positions collectively even when you might personally accept less. It requires showing up, repeatedly, for something that may not benefit you individually but that protects the collective. That's the only mechanism that has ever actually moved power. Not individual choice. Not consumer preferences. Not market forces. But ordinary people, organized together, saying no in unison and meaning it.
