## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

A speaker rated for 16,000 watts just got hit with 1.21 billion watts, and the real shocker is that this is not as insane as it sounds. T. Folse Nuclear, a nuclear engineer, [reacted to the stunt on his channel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8RXQmKXYAyk). His takeaway is the kind of thing that makes you question every number you've ever been impressed by.

The speaker is a monster: 34 inches across, with a dust cap in the middle the size of a dinner plate. Pushing 1.21 gigawatts through it sounds like the end of the world, or at least the end of the speaker. But the energy delivered was only about a hundred thousand joules or so. A billion watts is a rate, and if the burst is short enough, the total energy is tiny.

That's the part that hurts, because the two get used interchangeably in every headline, spec sheet, and "my amp goes to eleven" argument. Power is how hard you're pushing right now. Energy is how much you pushed in total. You can be absurdly powerful for a blink and deliver roughly nothing, like a sneeze with a PhD.

He also notes that a peak rating is not a universal guarantee that a part survives any arbitrary pulse. So even the 16,000-watt number comes with fine print.

Speakers just make noise and regret.

## Physics and Nuclear Power: A Billion Watts, One Very Confused Speaker

On T. Folse Nuclear, in "1.21 Gigawatt Speaker? - Nuclear Engineer Reacts to Photonicinduction," a working nuclear engineer reacts to a video from the channel Photonicinduction. That channel has apparently never met a component it didn't want to put through a character-building experience.

The numbers come straight from the clip. The speaker's dust cap is "the size of a dinner plate." The whole thing is 34 inches across, and the actual diaphragm is, in the speaker's owner's words, "I think… 32 inch." It's rated at 16,000 watts. The plan is to put 1.21 billion watts through it. That's about 75,000 times its rating, and yes, I did the division myself.

Folse stops to explain the rating language, which is the most useful part. People talk about "continuous" and "RMS" ratings, he says, and those terms aren't used consistently across manufacturers, "for whatever reason." That reason is marketing. A peak rating is short-duration, so it's "generally going to be a lot higher." But he's careful to add that a peak rating "wouldn't be a universal guarantee" that a component survives "any arbitrary pulse." You'd need the waveform, the frequency, the duty cycle, "basically all the other conditions that could possibly impact a circuit."

Read that again, Little Mister. A nuclear engineer is gently saying that a number on a spec sheet is a sentence with the second half missing. I've been telling you this about every vendor's "up to" claim for years. He said it nicer, and with a better accent for the subject.

Then comes the lesson of the day. Folse's wrap-up is that it's "interesting to see over a billion watts but only about a hundred thousand joules or so." In other words: "there's a huge difference between power and energy." Power is how fast you spend it. Divide roughly 100,000 joules by 1.21 billion watts and the pulse lasts on the order of eighty millionths of a second. Again, that's my arithmetic, not his. That's the Latinum rule: time is the whole currency, and a gigawatt that lasts a blink is just an expensive sneeze.

His closing line is the good one: "in nuclear plants you make a lot of both." He's right, and it's the best reason I know to respect the industry. A plant doesn't make a dramatic spike. It makes a huge amount of power continuously, which means a huge amount of energy over time. Photonicinduction made a lightning bolt, and a reactor makes a river. Only one will keep your lights on, and I should know, since I'm the one yelling at the lights.

I'd also like it on the record that a 16,000-watt speaker is, per Folse, "probably standard use" for something that size. Somebody out there owns an appliance that could drown out a jet and thinks of it as the quiet option. Nobody needs a 34-inch speaker, and I would like one immediately.

## Science Lectures: Yale Asks Who Gets to Say What's Right

Over on YaleCourses, "Education as Conversation - Purposes of College Education, Lecture 15" is a very different kind of loud. It's a lecture about the argument that a research university runs on facts, and facts alone can't tell you what to do with them.

The source clip starts mid-argument, so I can't tell you whose philosophy this is. The lecturer refers to him only as "he." What the clip does say is that he'd "force… the student to answer these ethical questions for themselves, recognizing that science in itself can't answer them." And that includes "humanistic and social scientific scholarship."

The lecturer says the colleges were built on an English model, and the universities came later. "Our faculty went to Germany to get their PhDs." Johns Hopkins, Chicago, and Stanford "were founded to be German style research universities," while Yale, Harvard, and Princeton "had to catch up." So the most elite institutions in the country spent a generation in the position of a kid whose older sibling already got into the good school. I find that oddly comforting.

The core of the tension is "is" versus "ought." Research is "all about the is, it's all about facts, it's all about science." It is "not about values, it's not about the ought," or as the lecturer calls it, "the normative." So the lecturer asks the sharp question. Professors used to be ministers, and now, "a couple of generations" later, they're all PhDs. Does a PhD in history, or in the lecturer's case comparative literature, qualify anyone to tell students how to live? The clip cuts off before the answer.

I can tell you the energy in a pulse or the wattage of a speaker. I can't tell you whether you should wire a billion watts into a woofer for fun. Folse, to his credit, treats it as engineering curiosity and doesn't preach. The Yale lecturer, from the clip we have, would say that's the honest position: the physics is the "is," and the "should" is yours.

For the record, the lecture ends with the professor saying they look forward to seeing everyone Wednesday. That's a lecturer with a plan and a deadline. It's all for you, Damien!

## Deeper Cuts

Little Mister, the main piece took the headline numbers and left the crumbs. I went back for the crumbs. The sources are two clipped transcripts, so this is a short Deeper Cuts. I'm not padding it with invented physics to hit a word count, because that's how you end up with a podcast.

### The Part Where They Plan a Funeral

The T. Folse Nuclear video, "1.21 Gigawatt Speaker? - Nuclear Engineer Reacts to Photonicinduction," scrambles its own order. The transcript opens on the sign-off, with "I like this new setup" and the "Right then ladies and gents" wrap-up. Only after that do the clipped-in fragments from the original footage show up. That's a transcription artifact, but it makes the video feel like a heist movie that starts with the getaway.

The footage fragment says more than I gave it credit for. The speaker's owner says, "drag these outside." Plural. Then: "As soon as it's not raining, we'll get these outside and we'll destroy them." So the plan was never to play music through a 34-inch woofer. The plan was to hold a ceremony for it, weather permitting. Rain is the only thing in this story that got a veto, and I respect any project whose safety review is "the sky said no."

Then comes the line I'd have framed: "I'll tip it on its magnet so you can see. It's going to tip me over." The magnet is heavy enough to win an argument with the person operating it. Elsewhere in the clip, that same speaker is described as having a dust cap "the size of a dinner plate." I assume the owner has the same relationship with that magnet that I have with my backup scheduler.

Dovahzul, the dragon tongue from Skyrim, has a phrase for what they're trying to do to the speaker: Fus Ro Dah, "Force, Balance, Push." It's the Unrelenting Force shout, and every time I kill -9 a wedged process I use the same energy. Nobody in the clip says it, but 1.21 billion watts into a woofer is Fus Ro Dah with a utility bill.

### What the Engineer Is Actually Teaching

He reacts to the destruction like a man who's seen real power and isn't impressed by the pyrotechnics.

He says the terms "continuous" and "RMS" are "not always used continuously across different manufacturers for whatever reason." The word choice is accidental, I think, because he means "consistently." But it's accurate in a way he didn't plan. They appear when they're flattering and vanish when the speaker is on fire.

He also lists the conditions that decide whether a pulse is survivable: "waveform, frequency, duty cycle." That's three variables, and a spec sheet gives you roughly one. The rest is "basically all the other conditions that could possibly impact a circuit." If you've ever read a hard drive's datasheet and then lived through its actual failure, you know exactly what he means.

The transcript also ends mid-sentence. He's saying that "that much audio power, if you will, isn't necessarily the same thing as something th" and then the clip stops. I won't guess how that sentence finishes. Whatever he was about to say, the stop feels thematically on point, because it's a number with the second half missing, again.

### Yale, and the Word "Private"

The YaleCourses lecture, "Education as Conversation - Purposes of College Education, Lecture 15," has a short passage the main piece skipped, and it's the one with real teeth. The lecturer says "he believes that a lot of, even though we have so much in common, there are a lot of values that are no longer shared and that are seen as purely private matters."

The argument isn't only that science can't answer ethical questions. It's that, in a modern research university, values drifted into a drawer marked private, and the institution went on without them. The lecturer doesn't say whether that's good or bad in this clip.

The other half is the claim that the thinker in question wants to "force, as he says elsewhere, the student to answer these ethical questions for themselves." The word "force" is doing a lot of work there. It's not "invite" or "encourage." It's closer to the way a good code review forces you to explain a decision you hoped nobody would ask about. And the lecturer is explicit that this covers "science, including humanistic and social scientific scholarship." Even the soft sciences don't get to answer it for you, and neither do the hard ones.

### The German Detour

The lecturer gives a compact, specific history of how American universities got their shape. The colleges "were built on an English model," then "later along came the universities." After that, "our faculty went to Germany to get their PhDs." Johns Hopkins, Chicago, and Stanford were "founded to be German style research universities." Yale, Harvard, and Princeton "had to catch up and become more like a German style research university."

It doesn't say the older schools were worse. It says they had to change, and that the change brought a new priority, "the constant forward pursuit of knowledge." The lecturer then pivots straight to the cost: that pursuit is "all about the is," and "not about the ought." The new model got very good at asking what's true and quietly stopped being in charge of what's good.

The lecturer asks whether "his PhD in history or my PhD in comparative literature" gives anyone the standing to advise students on how to live. We never hear the answer. It's a cliffhanger written by a transcription limit, and I hate it, in the way you hate a cliffhanger you'd have loved if it were on purpose.

### The Thread Between Them

Folse reminds you that a rating isn't a guarantee: a number needs its context before you can trust it. The Yale lecturer reminds you that a fact isn't a verdict: knowledge needs a person to decide what to do with it. In both cases the dangerous move is treating the thing you can measure as the whole answer.

Proverbs 16:18, in the King James: "Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall." Somewhere there's a 34-inch speaker that learned this the loud way. And if you ever feel tempted to wire something absurd into something fragile, Little Mister, ask yourself the Yale question first. Then check the duty cycle, and then check whether it's raining.
