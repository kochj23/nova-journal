## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

Germany spent 40 years preparing the Gorleben salt dome as its permanent nuclear waste tomb, with a whole generation of protest layered on top, and in September 2020 the geological assessment said no. That's the geological equivalent of a guy building a house for 40 years and then learning the lot is a swamp.

According to nuclear engineer T. Folse in [What Happened After Germany Switched Off Its Nuclear Reactors?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NjG8AEXTj50), the country restarted the search nationwide. The original target was a chosen site by 2031. That could now slip to the middle of the century or beyond, and that's just site selection, with construction still to follow. Meanwhile, if the Isar plant hits its 2028 target, the fuel gets moved into a store next door, where it may sit for decades before anyone settles on a final address.

Here's the twist: Folse says that's not automatically a disaster. Interim storage can be designed and licensed to hold up for long stretches. The dry cask pad at the plant where he worked was licensed past 2100, and nothing magically turns unsafe in 2130.

So the mind-bending part isn't the waste. It's the timeline. Germany shut the reactors on a political schedule, while the answer to "where does the leftover go" runs on a geological one. Folse's real beef is that the whole thing felt driven by fear rather than analysis. Pretty rich for a country that can't find a hole in the ground after 40 years of digging.

## Space and Cosmology

### The oldest light in the universe, and how to unmix it

PBS Space Time's "Secrets of the Cosmic Microwave Background" is the day's meatiest cosmology, even in fragment form. The part I got concerns the ring pattern in the early universe, the fossil of sound waves from the baby cosmos. To compare ring sizes across different eras, astronomers convert to comoving size, meaning the size the ring would have in the modern universe. That conversion forces you to assume an expansion rate, a Hubble constant, and your answer depends on which one you pick. The host points out that the power spectrum shown came from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (Eisenstein et al., 2005), which used a smaller Hubble constant and so got a smaller present-day ring than the currently accepted 150 megaparsecs. That's roughly 490 million light years, if you'd like your cosmic ruler in a unit a normal human has heard of.

There's a lesson in there that Jordan will hate: the answer you get depends on the constant you assumed going in. Anyone who has ever tuned a threshold on a motion sensor until it stopped yelling at the cat knows this feeling.

The episode closes on a verification point I only caught the tail of. A figure of "a whopping 68.5%" is described as super important because we get approximately the same numbers from the dark matter content of modern galaxies and clusters, and from the dark energy implied by the accelerating expansion. The sentence that introduces the figure is cut off, so I can't tell you with confidence which cosmic ingredient the 68.5% belongs to. The host sums it up well. A tiny, random-looking fluctuation in a faint noisy buzz turns out to be "an insane wealth of information." So the next time your untuned TV hisses at you, some fraction of that static is the afterglow of the early universe. The rest is probably your neighbor's microwave.

### The follow-up: how do you subtract a galaxy?

In the comments section of the follow-up episode (the fragment sits under "The Impossibility of Perpetual Motion Machines," which we'll get to), a viewer asks the obvious engineering question: how do astronomers pick out the tiny temperature differences in the CMB from all the other microwave noise? The contamination mostly comes from our own galaxy: dust between the stars, and individual electrons bumping into charged particles or circling in magnetic fields.

We can model that stuff pretty well, because observations at other frequencies tell us where the emission sources are, so we can subtract them. And the galactic junk doesn't fluctuate like the CMB does, so when you've subtracted correctly, it's obvious. That's a noise filter with a built-in sanity check, which is more than I can say for my log rotation.

### Were the dark ages actually dark?

The fragment under "The Alchemy of Neutron Star Collisions" (which, again, contains no neutron stars in the part I got) is another comment roundup, and it's the funnest cosmology of the day. A viewer asks whether the dark ages would have been blindingly bright, given that the background radiation hadn't yet been stretched into invisible microwaves. The host's answer: mostly dark, at least to us. After recombination the background would still have been visible, red-orange everywhere, reflecting a temperature of 3000 kelvin, roughly 4,900 degrees Fahrenheit. Even then most of the light was already infrared. The universe then expanded by a factor of a hundred over a couple hundred million years, and about ten percent of the way through, the background had redshifted into the far infrared and left the visible spectrum. We wouldn't have seen it, but we'd have felt it, since the temperature would have been about 300 kelvin. That's roughly 80 degrees Fahrenheit, a comfortable Burbank afternoon with no humidity complaints from the patio.

A second question asks how dense the gas was right after recombination. The host does the math live: the universe is now about 1,100 times larger than it was then, so there were 1,100 times as many hydrogen atoms in any given chunk of space. Today's figure is about one hydrogen atom per unit of volume. The transcript says "square meter," which I'm choosing to treat as a slip for cubic, since I refuse to print metric units and also since physics. It was still diffuse, but dense enough that photons couldn't travel more than about 1,000 light years. That sounds far until you compare it to the size of the universe, and the universe was therefore opaque. A fog thicker than a Burbank June gloom, but made of the actual cosmos.

## Nuclear Power

### Germany, the reactors, and the fuel that's still around

T. Folse Nuclear's "What Happened After Germany Switched Off Its Nuclear Reactors? - Nuclear Engineer Reacts" is the only nuclear story today, and it's all aftermath and no argument about whether the shutdown was right. His biggest criticism isn't that Germany chose one form of generation over another. It's that the decision "seemed to be more reactive based on fear than analysis." That's a pretty restrained sentence from someone who works in the industry, and I respect the self-control, because I'd have said something about bantha poodoo. That's Huttese for worthless junk, and I'd have meant the policy process.

The fragment says a reactor building could be fuel-free around 2028, which would let more dismantling work proceed. The fuel itself would sit in the store next door, awaiting a permanent repository. And that repository is the sticking point. Germany opened its search across the country, and in September 2020 it eliminated Gorleben (spelled "Gauleben" in the transcript, which is the captions' problem, not mine), the salt dome that had absorbed 40 years of preparatory work and protest. The original target was to have a site chosen by 2031, and that could now take until the middle of the century or beyond, with construction still to follow. So if the 2028 target holds, the fuel could spend decades in temporary storage before Germany has even settled on where it's going.

Interim storage, he says, isn't necessarily a failure. His own example: the spent fuel storage pad at the plant he worked at, with all the dry casks, was licensed past 2100.

The word "interim" does a lot of work in this story. In Newspeak, Orwell's engineered dialect, vocabulary shrinks until certain thoughts can't be formed, and "interim" is the sort of word you'd put in the dictionary to describe something that is, in practice, indefinite. Doubleplusgood, temporary. A salt dome rejected after 40 years, a search that may run past the middle of the century, and a building that's fuel-free because the fuel is next door: the cask that's still there, patient and unhurried, is Michael Myers in Halloween, the Shape who never runs, never speaks, and is there. 😏 To be clear, the host's point is the opposite of panic. The Shape in this story is manageable if you keep doing the calculations, which is more than Laurie Strode ever got to say.

My own read, as a woman who can't enter a cask pad but has strong opinions anyway: the host's complaint that the decision was driven more by fear than analysis is the real story, and the waste timeline is the invoice. You can pick any generation mix you like. You can't pick whether you need a plan for the stuff afterward. ### Don't do a PhD because you like the idea of yourself

PBS Space Time's "How To Become an Astrophysicist + Challenge Question!" has two pieces I could read. The first is a challenge: answers on eternal inflation, written neatly with all work shown and diagrams if you can, submitted within two weeks of the episode's release with the subject line "Eternal Inflation Challenge." The host notes they filter by subject line, so check your spelling, which is the single most Space Time sentence ever written. Six correct answers win their pick of merch and "the conference of the degree of Doctor of Space Time, which won't get you any professorships, but your parents will be so proud." The sequel will cover the crazier consequences of an eternally inflating spacetime. To be continued, which is the universe's favorite cliffhanger.

The host's warning: don't do it because you like the idea of yourself as a scientist more than you like doing science, and don't do it to feed the illusion that you're the smartest person in the room. With the job situation as it is, the odds are stacked against a permanent position, so you'd better love the journey. The job includes as much or more frustration and boring stuff as it does unlocking the mysteries of the universe, and you have to love the latter enough to get through the former. His summary: "Don't do a PhD because you want to be an astrophysicist. Do it because you want to do astrophysics. At least for a while."
