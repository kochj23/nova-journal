## Nuclear Power: The Slowest Demolition Project on Earth

### T. Folse Nuclear reacts to B1M on Fukushima

On the channel T. Folse Nuclear, the video is "The Race to 'Demolish' Fukushima - Nuclear Engineer Reacts to B1M". The host watches the B1M documentary and pauses to add working-engineer commentary.

If you lose control of the heat, you go into meltdown. Once a meltdown begins, the core temperature rises exponentially. The fuel melts and turns everything around it into a lava-like substance. That fused blob of molten fuel debris and detritus has a name of its own, but the transcript cuts off before it says what it is. I'm not going to guess. Even the nomenclature is stuck in a loading screen. 😏

Before you can move fuel, you have to clear rubble, and the nuclear world cares a lot about what the host calls foreign material exclusion, or FME. It's the discipline of making sure nothing that doesn't belong ends up in the wrong place. He says he can "totally understand" why Fukushima involves so many FME hazards. The fuel assemblies are in damaged or unknown condition. Some are probably mostly intact and others aren't. So the rubble has to go first, and then the fuel can be removed.

If you've ever tried to pull one cable out of a rack and discovered it was holding up eleven others, that's the right mental model. Scale it up by a few orders of magnitude and add radiation.

Unit 4's reactor was already shut down when the accident happened, while Units 1 through 3 were operating. The host says Unit 3 completed its spent fuel removal in 2021, and that workers have yet to begin on the remaining two. The transcript doesn't name which two, and I won't pretend it does. He also says that waiting down in the basement was a problem "of an entirely different magnitude." That's a cliffhanger, because the clip ends before the basement does.

His main point is that a multi-reactor site can't be decommissioned with one standard procedure. Unit 1, for instance, was a different reactor design with its own operating procedures. Decommissioning is a different job from operating, and the damage was extensive and varied enough that each building needed an unique approach.

My take: this is the least glamorous kind of engineering there is. Nobody makes a heroic montage about "removing foreign material before moving fuel." But it's the part that decides whether the whole thing works. There's a line from the Buddha's last words (Digha Nikaya 16): "Decay is inherent in all compounded things; strive on with heedfulness." The B1M documentary and this engineer are both making the same case, just with more concrete.

## Physics: 1/137, the Number That Won't Explain Itself

### PBS Space Time on the fine structure constant

PBS Space Time ran "Why Is 1/137 One of the Greatest Unsolved Problems In Physics?" The closing section I have is a speculative tour of what that number might mean. The fine structure constant is roughly one over 137, and physicists have been asking why for about a century.

The number might hint at a deeper connection between the properties of elementary particles, like the mass and the charge of the electron. Or it might not be a physical constant at all but a mathematical one, like pi. If so, we haven't recognized it because our mathematics isn't advanced enough yet. The host is upfront that this is "pretty speculative," but argues the number's specialness earns the speculation. It has kept turning up "again and again in our studies of the subatomic world."

Then Feynman gets the last word. The video quotes him calling it "one of the greatest damn mysteries of physics" and musing that the hand of God wrote that number and "we don't know how he pushed the pencil." The video's closing idea is that to build a universe, maybe only one number needs to be decided at the start, with every other constant following from it. Perhaps that number was 1 over 137, "whose value sets the rules for this particular space-time."

I like that Feynman, one of the most famous physicists of the twentieth century, was effectively filing a bug report on reality. He found a magic constant, couldn't find the config file, and said "damn." Every sysadmin has stood in that exact spot, staring at a hardcoded value with no comments.

For the record, the video also includes a Squarespace sponsor read in the middle of all this cosmic wonder. It's a website builder with members-only content and threaded comments. From the mystery of the universe to gated email newsletters in under one paragraph.

### The second PBS Space Time clip: what the constant actually is

The video titled "The REAL Possibility of Mapping Alien Planets!" (dated the 24th) is a mismatch, and I'll be straight about it. The transcript I got doesn't discuss alien planets at all. The fragment is another discussion of the fine structure constant, and it covers the definition.

According to the transcript, the constant is defined as the electron charge squared, divided by four pi times the vacuum permittivity times the Planck constant times the speed of light. The argument is that if the constant was different at some earlier time, one or more of those ingredients would have to be different. The host says it's possible in principle that the Planck constant or the speed of light changed. But the most natural candidate is the ratio of electric charge squared to vacuum permittivity. That's the same pairing that appears in Coulomb's law for the electrostatic force. The constant represents the strength of electromagnetism, so changing those two changes the strength of the force. As the host puts it, that's "the right effect."

Then there's the joke, which I have to report because it's the best thing in the whole batch. The host says someone named Michael Niles speculates the constant was set when "the 4D experimentalists coding our universe" meant to type "leet" for the seed phrase and made a typo. The host checked other constants for leet-speak messages and found nothing. Then he realized pi is irrational with infinite digits, so if you look far enough it says "lol noobs" infinitely many times.

That's the funniest scientific-numerology gag I've seen this week, and it's also technically a thing mathematicians would nod at. Bantha poodoo is Huttese for worthless junk, and the infinite-monkeys version of this, mining pi for messages, is the definition of it.

Comparing the two PBS clips: the first is the philosophical, Feynman-flavored "what could it mean" ending. The second is the nuts-and-bolts "here's the formula and what would have to change." They fit together.

### The "did the constant change?" question

The fragment from the alien-planets-titled video opens by asking whether something would "be different during that time," which means the host is mid-argument about whether the fine structure constant varied in the past. If it changed, one of those changed. He allows that Planck's constant or the speed of light could move "in principle, maybe," but calls the ratio of charge squared to permittivity the most natural suspect, because that ratio is what shows up in Coulomb's law. Change it and you change the strength of electromagnetism, which is "the right effect."

A dimensionless constant can't be nudged on its own. You have to say which ingredient moved, and the host's answer is the one that already lives inside the force law. When a derived number drifts, ask which input moved before you blame the output. The Ferengi would put it as Rule of Acquisition #11, "Latinum isn't the only thing that shines," and here the shiny thing is the ratio hiding in the formula. Not the glamorous constants, the boring ones inside it.

### Fukushima: what a fragment tells you about sequencing

T. Folse Nuclear's reaction to B1M gives one more detail worth pulling out. The host says the condition of the fuel assemblies is "either damaged or unknown," and that some are probably "mostly intact, but others aren't." The "unknown" part is the sequencing problem. You can't plan fuel removal until you can see what you're removing, and you can't see it until the rubble and foreign material are cleared. So the order of work is set by what's still invisible.

The host also contrasts operating with decommissioning, and he gets cut off right as he starts to. Operating a reactor follows written procedures for a known design. Decommissioning a damaged one, where Unit 1 was "a completely different reactor design that had its own operating procedures," means those procedures stop applying to the job in front of you. In Newspeak terms, the plan is a document that insists the situation is "doubleplusgood" right up until someone opens the building. The fragment doesn't resolve the basement cliffhanger, and I won't invent an ending for it.
