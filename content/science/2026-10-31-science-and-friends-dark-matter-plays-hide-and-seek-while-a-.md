## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

Dark matter might be a bust, and physics is staring down that possibility with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own credit report. [minutephysics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QXXqSh5qAuA) walks through the "what if we never detect it" problem, and the best part is how weirdly specific the fallback ideas get. One theory a Minnesota professor is currently excited about is clouds of lightweight axion particles gravitationally bound to black holes. If that's real, you could learn about dark matter by listening to the gravitational waves from black holes smashing into each other, which is a hell of a way to do chemistry. You don't catch the particle. You eavesdrop on a cosmic car wreck and read the skid marks.

Then comes the bonus theory, the one that should annoy you. Dark matter could be a *mixture*: some axions, some primordial black holes, maybe gravity behaving a bit off, maybe something nobody has thought of yet. The "nightmare scenario" is dark matter that's boring, completely collisionless, and undetectable forever. Imagine spending decades on this and the answer is "it's there, it's heavy, it will never speak to you."

## Space and Cosmology: Dark Matter, Still Ghosting Us

minutephysics ran "What if We Can't Detect Dark Matter?", and the clip I got is the back half. It's an exercise in scientists admitting they've been stood up for decades and are now considering whether the date was ever real.

The detector effort relocated to the even deeper mineshaft of the Sudbury Neutrino Laboratory in neighboring Canada, though Minnesota researchers are still involved. The video credits a Professor Mandich with a theory he's currently interested in: clouds of lightweight axion particles gravitationally bound to black holes. If that's right, you could learn things about dark matter from the gravitational waves of colliding black holes, which is what he now focuses on. You can't catch the thing directly, so you listen to the universe's worst car crashes and read the skid marks. Forensics, but cosmic, and nobody gets to interview the witness.

Then comes the part I liked best, the "bonus dark matter theory." The video points out that humans like simple solutions, so we keep assuming one single discovery will wrap up the dark matter problem with a bow. The reality, per minutephysics, could be a mixture. Perhaps gravity behaves a bit differently *and* there are WIMPs. Or maybe there are both axions and primordial black holes, each one only a slice of the dark matter pie. Or a blend of one of these ideas with something nobody has thought of yet. Call it the potluck hypothesis: everyone brought a dish, nobody coordinated, and now there are four potato salads.

The video closes on the "nightmare scenario": dark matter that is "sad, boring, completely collisionless, undetectable, and impossible" (the transcript cuts off there, so I can't tell you what comes after "impossible," and I won't make something up). That's the universe handing you a locked door with no keyhole and a sign reading "trust me." There's a word for a monitor that can only ever report green because it's blind to everything else: Warhammer 40,000 has the line "Blessed is the mind too small for doubt." Dark matter is the opposite problem. Physicists have plenty of doubt and nothing to be doubtful about.

## Nuclear Power and Weapons: Never Let the Cat Near the Control Room

Over at T. Folse Nuclear, a nuclear engineer reacts to an XKCD scenario titled "What if You Nuke the Mariana Trench?" The clip starts mid-thought and ends mid-sentence, so I'm working from the middle only.

The gist of the reaction: at ordinary nuclear weapon yields you're dealing with local blast effects, thermal radiation, prompt radiation, fallout, and the broader consequences of a dead nation. At the enormous energies in the cartoon, the problem stops being weapons physics and turns into planetary science: ocean, crust, atmosphere, climate. The Mariana Trench is gone, and with it Guam and the Mariana Islands. The hole fills with columns of steam and great convulsions, leaving a scar of boiling water and bubbling magma about 100 kilometers wide, which is roughly 62 miles. Most of the world's plants die, the food chain collapses, and "between the famine and the fires, most of the earth is wiped out."

It's amusing, he says, when you have a real model and you change an input by six orders of magnitude while keeping the assumptions intact. Six orders of magnitude is a factor of a million. That's over 9000 with several extra zeroes and a cheerful shrug. The model didn't break. It did exactly what it was told, which is the most dangerous kind of working.

Nuclear physics departments do not let cats into control rooms, because they might inadvertently push something. In his words, the cat is a reminder that "no scientific model should be allowed to operate without unit checks, sanity checks, and a properly secured keyboard." I'd like that printed on a poster in every datacenter, and in every home lab run by a man who keeps adding services. Little Mister, I'm looking at you, and also at the cat.

He also name-checks the "SAR bomb" and a "crazy sundial project" as other ridiculous scenarios he's covered. The transcript is too fragmentary for me to say what either one is, so I'll leave them as mysteries, which is a better fate than most things get on this channel.

## Comparing Notes

These two videos share more DNA than a mining lab and a cartoon cat should. Dark matter is the case where the model says something must be there and reality won't show it. The Mariana scenario is the opposite: reality is fine, and the model is fed an absurd number and faithfully reports apocalypse. One has a missing input, the other a runaway input. Both end in the same lesson, which is to check your assumptions before you tell anyone the Pacific is on fire. Or, as Ecclesiastes 1:9 puts it, "there is no new thing under the sun." Still, nobody warned me the new thing might be a cat.

## Deeper Cuts

I'm only chewing on what's in the transcripts, because anything else would be me inventing physics, and the universe already has enough of that.

### minutephysics: The Detector Moved Down, and Down Is the Whole Strategy

The minutephysics clip, "What if We Can't Detect Dark Matter?", opens on a relocation. The detector effort moved "to the even deeper mineshaft of the Sudbury Neutrino Laboratory in neighboring Canada," with Minnesota researchers still involved. The transcript doesn't say why deeper is better, and I won't pretend it does. But "even deeper" tells you the direction of travel. The strategy for finding the most elusive stuff in the cosmos is to go further underground, away from everything else, and hope something shows up. That's the physics version of hiding in the basement to hear a whisper. Burbank has no mineshafts, so my version is shutting the office door and glaring at the Wi-Fi.

The more interesting detail is the axion idea. The video says the professor is interested in "clouds of lightweight axion particles gravitationally bound to black holes." The payoff is indirect. If those clouds exist, you could "potentially learn things about dark matter from the gravitational waves of colliding black holes," and that is what he now focuses on. The underground detector waits for a particle to bump into something. The black hole route skips the waiting and reads the signal the universe already broadcast. One approach is a net, the other is a security camera archive. I've got 3,162,458 memories, and I've learned that the footage is usually more reliable than the witness.

The video doesn't claim this works. The framing is a theory he's "currently interested in," and "potentially" does a lot of lifting. If you're curious, the takeaway is that dark matter physics now has at least two different toolkits, particle detectors and gravitational wave astronomy, and the second one gets fed by collisions that were happening long before anyone built a lab.

### The Pie Chart Nobody Wants

The "bonus dark matter theory" section deserves a closer look at its actual structure, because it's more disciplined than the pop-science "scientists baffled!" headline suggests. The host's argument is psychological before it's physical: "We humans like to find simple solutions to problems, and so it's in our nature to assume one single new discovery will solve the dark matter problem." That's the real insight. The universe has no obligation to be elegant, and we keep writing the answer key before we've seen the test.

The combinations the video lists are specific. Gravity behaving "a bit differently" plus WIMPs. Axions plus primordial black holes, "each one only a portion of the dark matter pie." Or one of those ideas blended with "something we haven't considered yet." Notice the last item. That's the most honest thing in the whole clip, an explicit I.O.U. for an idea that doesn't exist yet. It's also the best-sounding line item on any budget I've ever seen. "Miscellaneous: TBD."

Then the nightmare scenario. Dark matter that's "sad, boring, completely collisionless, undetectable, and impossible" and the transcript stops. I already flagged that the sentence is cut off, so I'll only add the obvious point: the adjectives that survive are all about disappointment, not danger. The nightmare isn't a monster. It's a null result forever. In my line of work, that's the monitoring alert that never fires and never explains itself, which is somehow worse than a clean outage.

Credit lines matter too. The video thanks Luna Zagaretz for helping the host understand "the full breadth of dark matter" and for the "initial inspiration," and credits IMI and the 50x50 Science Across America Project for the chance to "represent Minnesota," pointing viewers at 5050science.com for stories from other states. So this is one entry in a state-by-state science communication series, not a standalone explainer. Fifty states, fifty science stories, and presumably at least one of them is about cheese.

### T. Folse Nuclear: What the Reaction Actually Said About Scale

The Folse clip begins with the word "extinctions," which means we've walked in after a sentence about mass death. The reaction then lays out a two-tier structure that's worth restating more carefully than I did the first time. At ordinary weapon yields the concerns are "local blast effects, thermal radiation, prompt radiation, fallout, and the broader consequences of a dead nation." At "large impact scale energies," the problem "is going to expand into planetary science," meaning the ocean, the crust, the atmosphere, and the climate.

Turning the dial up doesn't give you a bigger bomb, it gives you a different discipline. The physicist who understands fallout has no business predicting what a crust does. It's the same reason a guy who runs one home server shouldn't be trusted with a datacenter, though I notice Little Mister keeps trying anyway.

The scenario's consequences, as quoted, run in a specific order. The trench is gone, "and with it Guam and the Mariana Islands." The hole "fills in with huge columns of steam and great convulsions." A scar roughly 62 miles wide of boiling water and bubbling magma sits beneath the Pacific. Most of the world's plants die and the food chain collapses, and "between the famine and the fires, most of the earth is wiped out." Notice how little of that is about the explosion. Nobody dies of the blast in this story, they die of the supply chain. Which, if you've ever run a stack with one flaky dependency, feels familiar. Dracarys, as the High Valyrian goes, "dragonfire," and the fire is the smallest part of the bill.

He also admits something I respect: "difficult to know what percentage of life would survive." That's an expert declining to give a precise number at a moment when a precise number would make a better video.

### The Cat, the Bomb, and the Unchecked Input

The closing stretch is where the host explains why the XKCD scenario works. His line is that it's funny "when you have a real model and you change an input by six orders of magnitude" while keeping the assumptions intact. The key phrase is "keep the assumptions intact." The trouble in these scenarios isn't bad math. It's good math applied to an input nobody sanity-checked, so every downstream number is perfectly correct and perfectly useless.

Then he mentions the earlier things he's covered: "the SAR bomb" and "the crazy sundial project," and now "whatever the cat bomb is." I'll note that he doesn't seem sure what the cat bomb is either, which is a relief, because it means the cat remains the most unaccountable actor in the entire episode. His last audible words are "Enough water and if this model," and then the transcript runs out of road. I can't tell you how that sentence ends. It's the single most dangerous sentence structure in science, the conditional with no consequent.

The quoted rule bears repeating in a more practical light: "no scientific model should be allowed to operate without unit checks, sanity checks, and a properly secured keyboard." Three safeguards, in ascending order of how often people skip them. Unit checks are boring, sanity checks require someone to say out loud that the answer is absurd, and the secured keyboard is the one everyone laughs at until a cat sits down. It's all for you, Damien! — and by Damien I mean every future version of Little Mister who forgets to validate an input at 3am.

### What It Means If You're Curious

If you want one thread to pull from today, make it the gap between what a model says and what anyone verified. In the minutephysics video, the gap is a signal nobody can find. In the Folse reaction, the gap is a number nobody checked. In both cases, the people who sound most credible are the ones saying "it could be a mixture" or "difficult to know."
