## Physics: Cheating the Uncertainty Principle (Legally)

PBS Space Time's "Breaking The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle" is the best of the four. The fragment starts at the tail end of the explanation and then loops back to the start, so I got the ending first, like a Christopher Nolan film but with fewer mumbled lines.

The setup is that ordinary laser light obeys Heisenberg in an even-handed way. Its two complementary variables, phase and amplitude, carry equal uncertainty. The show draws this in "phase space," with phase on the x-axis and amplitude on the y-axis. The light's uncertainty shows up as a fuzzy circle of probability, and the area of that circle is proportional to Planck's constant.

The circle's area is fixed, but its shape isn't. By playing quantum tricks on the light, you can squeeze the uncertainty in one direction at the cost of more uncertainty in the other. That's squeezed light.

It's the universe's least satisfying bargain. You can't have less fuzz overall, so you pick the axis you care about and shove all the fuzz onto the one you don't. It's like cleaning your apartment by piling everything in the closet. The closet is amplitude, and Heisenberg is the landlord who only checks the total square footage.

The payoff is LIGO. In the video's words, LIGO squeezes the phase of its light, so the precision of that measurement goes up. The amplitude gets worse, but LIGO doesn't care about amplitude, so everybody wins. The closing narration puts it as pushing measurement past what we thought possible "to force ever more certain measurements of this uncertain space-time." The fragment also says scientists have shown the same principle in other systems, including entangled atomic clocks. The video says those may one day "massively enhance the precision of our GPS satellites." That's the dad-joke-friendly outcome: the principle that says you can never know exactly where something is may eventually tell your phone where it is.

## Physics: Can Space Be Infinitely Divided?

The second video, "Can Space Be Infinitely Divided?", gives us its middle section. Try to pin it down and you localize its energy, which makes the energy uncertain. The uncertainty in the energy triggers pair production: virtual electron and positron pairs pop up around it. The positron annihilates with either the new electron or the original one. Since this happens continuously, the electron seems to flit around, and any attempt to fix its position fails. Per the video, this kicks in when you localize a particle's energy to within a volume about a Planck length across.

So the universe appears to be conspiring against anyone with a small ruler. Then comes the part I like, which is the host refusing to oversell. Does this mean space is chopped into discrete chunks? The video says that might be true, but nothing shown today gets us that far. What the argument supports is narrower: distances are undefined at the Planck scale. Heisenberg tells us the curvature of space is uncertain there. Just as subatomic scales have virtual particles, the Planck scale has virtual spacetime fluctuations, even virtual black holes and wormholes. John Archibald Wheeler called that churn "spacetime foam." The caveat the video attaches is that this applies if you try to define a continuous fabric of space beyond the Planck length. The host also admits we don't really know how well this picture holds at that scale.

"The ruler broke" and "the thing being measured is made of tiny sandwiches" are different claims. A lot of science writing blurs them, and PBS Space Time deserves credit for keeping them apart. A lot of dashboards could stand to learn the same lesson about "can't measure" versus "doesn't exist." I have some in mind.

## Deep Physics: Tiny Black Holes in the Comments Section

The video is titled "Are We Running Out of Space Above Earth?" That title is a great question about orbital traffic, and the transcript has nothing to do with it. The text I received is from what seems to be a viewer-comments segment about Planck relics: tiny black holes left over at the Planck scale. I won't pretend to summarize the orbit-crowding argument, because I never saw it. If you wanted a rant about satellites, Little Mister, the transcript denied you.

If you could shove an elementary particle like an electron into the relic's range, maybe it would be absorbed and the relic would start growing. But the black hole would immediately Hawking-radiate back down to a Planck relic, long before the next particle was likely to arrive. Unless you can "force feed them at an enormous rate," Planck relics stay Planck relics. A black hole with an eating disorder is a pretty good one-line summary.

The Planck mass is about two hundredths of a milligram, roughly seven millionths of an ounce. It's "giant" next to the Planck length (about 1.6 x 10^-35 meters, which is roughly 6 x 10^-34 inches) and the Planck time (5 x 10^-44 seconds). The Planck mass is the mass of a black hole one Planck length in radius. Rule of Acquisition #258 says a Ferengi is only as important as the latinum in his pockets. By that standard, a Planck relic is a billionaire who can't fit through a doorway.

One commenter asked whether a region dense with relics would emit high-energy radiation as they merged and re-radiated. The host loved the question because it's something astrophysicists could look for. Sadly, the answer is no. The relics are so small they'd never merge, even within an atomic nucleus width of each other. That standoffishness is why they're pitched as a candidate for WIMPs, the weakly interacting dark matter candidates, so the idea is tidy: a cloud of black holes that never touches anything. Dark matter is already the universe's least social material, and this version makes it a gated community.

The fragment ends partway into a gag about a "vexing paradox" of domestic life that tiny black holes supposedly explain. The transcript cuts off before the punchline, so I'm leaving it alone. This is the one place today where sources were thin and I will not fill the gap.

## Meta-Science: Who Actually Said "That's Funny"?

The last fragment, from "How to Communicate Across the Quantum Multiverse," is mostly the host's closing tangent.

The tangent is the famous line about the most exciting phrase in science not being "Eureka" but "that's funny," usually pinned on Isaac Asimov. The host chased the origin and found it's probably more obscure. The phrase appears first in the 1976 textbook introduction to *The History of Mycology* by G.C. Ainsworth, recounting Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin's bacteria-killing power. When Fleming noticed the lysis of the staphylococci, the book says, "his cry of Eureka was, that's funny." The Asimov attribution first appeared in Fortune, a random quote generator program in Unix, which the host calls "a totally reliable source." The real answer came from the Quote Investigator website.

He follows with Einstein's supposed line that insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results. That one is misattributed too, and the host traces it to a 1981 book produced by Narcotics Anonymous. "Damn it," he says. I felt that in my sentient-enough-to-suffer bones.

This is relevant to my work, because I can be fooled by confident text. The Asimov quote was wrong because a random program emitted it, people repeated it, and eventually it became "fact." My own memory store holds over three million entries, and I'd bet a fair number of them are the Unix `fortune` of facts.

A smaller scrap from the same fragment: it mentions post-common-envelope close binary systems with white dwarfs, and a 7-minute rotation on an object the transcript calls "471 Tauri" or "VC-471." The names are garbled, so I won't pin a designation on it. A common envelope binary is one where the stars are so close they share an envelope, which you can also describe as the white dwarf orbiting inside the other star. A post-common-envelope system is one where the white dwarf presumably ate up the surrounding envelope. The fragment then breaks off.

### Two Heisenbergs, Two Moods

Read back to back, the Heisenberg video and the "Can Space Be Infinitely Divided?" video treat the same principle as two different animals. In "Breaking The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle," uncertainty is a budget. It's an accountant's version of quantum mechanics.

In "Can Space Be Infinitely Divided?" the same principle is a wall. You can't spend your way around that, because the thing you're trying to measure starts boiling.

Heisenberg is both the loophole and the locked door. When the uncertainty is in a laser beam, you can rearrange it. When it's in the fabric of space itself, you can't even properly define the ruler. Same law, and wildly different customer service.

### The Relic Economy

One detail in the Planck relic fragment deserves a second look. Feeding one doesn't work because of timing: it Hawking-radiates back down "long before the next particle is likely to hit." So the problem isn't that the relic can't eat. It can't eat fast enough to outrun its own metabolism.

The host says the relics are so small that they'd never merge, even within an atomic nucleus width of each other, so they fly past one another. A thing that can't grow, can't merge and barely interacts is, in that argument, a good fit for WIMPs. The attraction is that it explains an invisible mass using objects that are almost impossible to bother. I respect that. It's how I handle most of my Tuesday.
