## Space and Cosmology: The Biggest Thing We Can See Is the Size of Our Own Backyard

In "How Luminiferous Aether Led to Relativity" (PBS Space Time, 2026-11-06), the visible portion of the transcript is a comment reply about the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the oldest light we can detect. A viewer named Steve asked how big the universe was at the most distant point the CMB represents. The answer, delivered with the breeziness of a man doing long division on a napkin, is that the universe has expanded by a factor of 1100 since the CMB was released. It's now 93 billion light years across, so the CMB sphere comes out to around 85 million light years across.

The sphere marking the farthest thing we can see, the edge of the observable fog, is about half again the size of the local Virgo supercluster, according to the episode. I'd call that cosmic irony, but irony implies the universe cares.

When we look farther out we're looking back in time, at a younger and therefore smaller universe, so the inner surfaces of those spheres get progressively smaller the farther back you go. And we can't see the tiniest, earliest sphere because the universe was opaque back then.

My favorite detail is that those CMB photons "should have reached us billions of years ago." They didn't have far to go in the beginning. ## Physics: Black Holes Are Allegedly Portals, and Physics Would Like a Word

The second video, "Does Gravity Require Extra Dimensions?" (PBS Space Time, 2026-11-06), cuts in during another comment reply, this time on whether black holes are doorways to other universes. The answer is a polite "only if you squint and ignore reality."

The host explains that tracing geodesics (the paths objects follow through curved spacetime) into alternate universes depends on assumptions that range from questionable to unreasonable. For the non-rotating Schwarzschild black hole, getting a parallel universe requires assuming the black hole has existed forever. For the rotating Kerr black hole, you get a whole string of universes only if you assume that its impossibly unstable inner structure doesn't collapse immediately. That's like assuming your Jenga tower of microservices is stable if nobody touches it, and I've seen how that story ends.

The host then agrees with a commenter, Polydon Wijnaland, that these impossible-seeming results, an infinite string of universes or a time machine, indicate "the math may have led our physical understanding astray." The best part is the follow-up. The host says this is "even more true when we see that the universe seems to conspire to prevent these things from existing." So the universe isn't just declining to give us wormholes. It's actively sabotaging the paperwork. I respect that kind of bureaucracy.

The host says his favorite joke from the comments was one he wishes he'd said in the episode. Instead of "you're in for a weird time," he should have said "you're in for a weird space," because space and time switch places inside a black hole. I'll steal that, if only for the sheer efficiency of the joke. Credit goes to a commenter the transcript renders as "Dr Poopstick," and I'll leave that name alone because I couldn't top it.

### What the Black-Hole Answer Actually Rests On

Back in "Does Gravity Require Extra Dimensions?", the host's answer about alternate universes is a tidy piece of reasoning, and it's worth laying out as a structure instead of a punchline. He names two cases. For a non-rotating Schwarzschild black hole, the parallel universe needs the assumption that the black hole "has been there forever." For a rotating Kerr black hole, the string of universes needs the assumption that the "impossibly unstable inner structure of the Kerr spacetime doesn't collapse immediately."

In both cases the exotic result depends on an idealization that real black holes don't meet. A real one formed at some point, and the Kerr interior wouldn't hold still. That's the difference between math describing an ideal object and physics describing one that exists.

The host then agrees with the commenter that infinite strings of universes and time machines suggest "the math may have led our physical understanding astray." He doesn't go further than that in the portion I have, and neither will I. Remember that your beautiful solution has assumptions, and the assumptions will outlive your enthusiasm.

### The Sun-Life Timescale, Done Properly

Last, the third video's sun-creature speculation has more internal logic than the first pass gave it. The host's argument is that the relevant timescale "may be driven by the timescale of the motion of plasma in the star, and by the size of these critters," with the timescale on the order of days and the size on the order of kilometers. Kilometers converts to a few miles per kilometer's worth of ground, so single-digit-mile creatures is the right ballpark for what he's describing, and I'm not pretending the transcript gives me a tighter number.

His conclusion is that such creatures would run slower than chemical life, "huge, very ancient, and very, very slow." That's a deduction from the physics of the medium, not a flight of fancy. The host also admits up front that "the theory just isn't there yet" on how chemistry might proceed. That caveat matters, and it's the honest part of the whole segment.
