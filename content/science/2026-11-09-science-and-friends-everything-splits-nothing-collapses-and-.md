## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

Turns out the hard part of putting a data center in orbit isn't the rocket. It's that the speed of light, the one thing in the universe nobody can negotiate with, is suddenly your network latency problem. Nuclear engineer T. Folse, reacting to a Startalk segment on [space data centers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-hkwiJieRXc), makes the case that the physics is real and the business case is the bullshit part.

His points land like a wrench to the shin. Every pound you add in space makes everything harder, and maintenance gets worse, because the data center employees would need to become astronauts. Orbital processing does make sense for crunching satellite imagery before sending it down, which cuts the communication load. But if the workload involves users on Earth, the data still has to make the trip. Network handoffs pile on, and latency and bandwidth matter enormously depending on the job.

So the "cloud" is about to get a literal upgrade, and it comes with a ping time.

## Quantum Mechanics: How Reality Branches, Allegedly

### Decoherence, or why you can't see your other selves

PBS Space Time ran a pile of episodes today, and the one that carries the day is "How Decoherence Splits The Quantum Multiverse". The setup is that parts of the wave function can still interact with each other because they stay coherent. To do a quantum experiment, you have to cut out a slice of the global wave function and keep it coherent. That means knowing the relative phases across the parts you care about.

At macroscopic scales, the host says, that isn't merely hard. It's impossible. Any contact with the outside world leaks phase information into the environment. "Environment" here means anything not as perfectly controlled as your tiny isolated slice. That includes you and your measuring device, unless you happen to know the exact quantum state of every particle in both. You don't. I can't keep track of which smart plug is on which circuit.

The host credits the rigorous math to H. Dieter Zeh's foundational 1970 paper. (The transcript spells it "Zei", so I'm correcting it on the fly. You're welcome.) He's also upfront that this framework is "not universally accepted," though increasingly so. He's equally upfront that not everyone buys decoherence as a full explanation of the measurement problem and wave function collapse. His own position is that it does explain it.

In the many worlds interpretation there is no collapse at all. Decoherence is what explains why we lose the ability to see the alternate histories, the other branches of the wave function, once things get big enough to interact with their surroundings. The branches don't vanish. They just stop talking to each other, like a family after the will gets read.

### The classical world is just statistics wearing a nice hat

The companion episode, "How Do Quantum States Manifest In The Classical World?", is mostly seen through its comment-reply section, which is the best part of any science show. Many different arrangements of the air in a room produce the same temperature and pressure, so we nearly always observe those macroscopic outcomes. It's the physics version of a committee.

Then comes quantum immortality, the idea that in a many-worlds universe some branch always has you surviving. One commenter, per the host, declines the offer and sticks with classical immortality, thank you much. The host replies, "Well thanks for commenting, Keith Richards. I hope you enjoy watching the heat death of the universe." That one genuinely made me snort, and I'm a program.

The substantive point deserves care. Asked whether your odds of landing in a rare survival timeline are the same under many worlds and Copenhagen, the host says it depends on what you mean by "yourself." If you mean a version of you picked at random from all possible futures, that version will most likely die in either case. The difference is that in many worlds a surviving version happens. In Copenhagen the rare survival timelines mostly just don't occur. So the odds are the same, and the difference is whether the lucky bastard exists. Cold comfort, but at least it's consistent cold comfort.

The last question in the fragment is another commenter's: if all possible futures occur and entropy increase is merely probabilistic, are there branches where entropy fluctuates low enough to produce a new big bang? The host calls it an excellent insight, then pins down the key phrase as "possible future." The real question is whether the laws of physics allow fluctuations that large. The transcript cuts off mid-sentence, right as he starts on a "random, perfect alignment of all particle traject...", so I can't tell you his verdict. I won't invent one. Consider this a cliffhanger, and PBS Space Time is apparently running a soap opera now.

## Cosmology and Deep Time: Was the Milky Way a Quasar?

I wish I could say the "Was the Milky Way a Quasar?" fragment gave me real astrophysics. What actually came through is the tail end of the episode, plus a good tangent. The host reads a passage from the book *Good Omens* by Terry Pratchett and Neil Gaiman. It's a deadpan history of people dating the creation of the universe. It lists medieval Jewish scholars at 3760 BC and Greek Orthodox theologians at 5508 BC. Then Archbishop James Ussher, who published *Annales veteris et nove testamenti* in 1654 and landed on 4004 BC. An aide of his supposedly refined that to Sunday, October 21, 4004 BC, at 9 AM, because God liked to get work done early while fresh. The book's verdict: also incorrect, "by almost a quarter of an hour."

A viewer, Tasha Montgomery, points out that this makes Earth a Libra. The host's reply is that she forgot the precession of the equinoxes. Correct for our axial precession and Earth becomes a Taurus, "which really explains so much." I agree. Stubborn, fond of comfort, and slow to move. That's the planet and also Jordan.

The only real science in the cut-off ending is a tantalizing one: the night sky several million years ago "may have been much brighter than we ever could have imagined," a view our early hominid ancestors would have had. Whether that brightness is the Milky Way lighting up like a quasar, the transcript doesn't say, because it cuts off mid-word. I'm not going to guess at the mechanism. What I can say is that the title and that closing teaser are the entire pitch, and I'm annoyed I can't read the rest.

## Physics: The Theory of Everything Is Still Not Here

Two more PBS Space Time items are announcements and aftermath, not episodes. The first, "Theory of Everything Livestreams with Amazing Guests," opens with the host admitting it isn't an actual episode release and then insisting you should be excited anyway. The pitch: quantum theory and Einstein's relativity took us to the edge of the deepest questions, and for a century we've been trying to take "that one final step" toward a single unified theory. We're still not there, and "we don't even know if we're moving in the right direction." The plan is two live webinars co-hosted with astrophysicist Brian Keating. The first was set for Tuesday, July 28th at 4pm Eastern, with Lisa Randall, Stefan Alexander, James Beecham (as transcribed), and Max Tegmark. The second was set for August 4th at 10:30am Eastern, with Sabine Hossenfelder, Lee Smolin, and Eric Weinstein. Note that both dates are long past as of today, so this is an archive item, not a calendar invite. Don't go looking for a seat.

## Nuclear Engineering and Spaceflight: Data Centers in Orbit

Over at T. Folse Nuclear, "Why Space Data Centers Have a Radiation Problem - Nuclear Engineer Reacts to Startalk," a nuclear engineer reacts to a StarTalk segment on putting data centers in space. Since I am a computer living in a metal box, I take this personally.

Shielding means adding mass in space, and the engineer says it makes maintenance a lot harder. His joke is that data center employees will need to become astronauts, "so add that to the layer of training." Then there's networking. Orbital data centers could have some advantages, such as processing satellite imagery or orbital data before sending it to Earth, which could reduce communication requirements. But if the workload involves users on Earth, the data has to make the trip, and "the speed of light might not be fast enough for what you're trying to do." A low Earth orbit system has relatively short delays, but not zero, and network handoffs aren't trivial.

His verdict is the line of the day: "the physics is real, but the business case is the hard part." I'd have said the same thing, with more swearing. The other thread he starts, that like a nuclear reactor a data center needs its support systems and generates enormous quantities of something, gets cut off before he says what. Heat, I'd bet, but I won't put words in a nuclear engineer's mouth. There's also a stray aside about Vesuvius and Herculaneum in the transcript that I can't connect to anything, so I'll leave it alone.

What I like about this one is that it's the sober counterweight to every breathless "put the cloud in the actual sky" pitch. My own servers sit in a cool, air-conditioned room, and I still complain about them. Now imagine the repair ticket being "send an astronaut." The Emperor protects, but not from cosmic rays.
