## Space and Cosmology: Dark Energy May Be Flaking Out

PBS Space Time, in "We've Finally Mapped Invisible Space. And It's Changing Our Entire History of The Universe," goes after the Dark Energy Survey. The headline claim is the one cosmologists have been circling for a while: dark energy itself may be changing over time. Dark energy is the mysterious thing pushing the universe's expansion along, and the working assumption has long been that it's a constant.

Supernovae and baryon acoustic oscillations tell you the history of cosmic expansion. Weak lensing, where the gravity of intervening structure subtly distorts the light of background galaxies, tells you about the gravitational structures that grew during that expansion. Those two stories have to fit the same cosmological model. If they don't, one of them is lying, and in cosmology that usually means your model is the liar.

Adding galaxy clustering measurements, including weak lensing, to the expansion history let the survey improve its constraints on cosmological parameters by a factor of four. That also raised the confidence for evolving dark energy. Combined with a much more precise survey the captions render as "DES-E" and with cosmic microwave background measurements, evolving dark energy reaches three sigma.

Three sigma is the physics equivalent of "I'm pretty sure I left the stove off." It's interesting and worth chasing, but it isn't the five-sigma bar for claiming a discovery. The episode itself calls an earlier hint "intriguing" and says it "will be followed up on." The channel's framing is that everything in the ultimate explanation of the universe has to fit together, so the structure we see has to make sense given the starting point in the CMB, the exact expansion history, and whatever dark energy is doing. Cosmology is a jigsaw puzzle where one of the corner pieces might be melting.

I'll be Entish about it. Treebeard's whole philosophy is "don't be hasty," and a three-sigma hint is the exact moment to hold still, not the moment to rewrite the textbooks. The episode also carries a sponsor read, so for the shoppers: Plaud is offering 22% off for Black Friday with code PBSSpace.

## Sleep: The Polyphasic Pitch Doesn't Survive Contact With Biology

SciShow's "Does The Uberman Sleep Schedule Really Work" is the closest thing today to a lecture. The answer is no, delivered with some mercy. The fragments say slow-wave sleep comes with a dip in blood pressure that's good for overall cardiovascular health, and REM sleep is responsible for consolidating emotional memory. During REM the brain is especially good at forming connections between distant regions, which allows for creativity and complex problem solving. Interrupt those cycles by waking before the slow-wave and REM phases finish, or just by not giving the body enough time, and "sooner or later, you're going to end up feeling pretty bad."

So how do some people apparently thrive on four to six short naps a day? The episode's answer is that "apparently" is doing a lot of heavy lifting. The reported benefits are most likely short-term adaptation to sleep deprivation, so people don't feel it in the moment, but the damage is still happening in the background.
