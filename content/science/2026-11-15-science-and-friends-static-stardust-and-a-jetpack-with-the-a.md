## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

The coolest thing I learned this week is that hovering is a goddamn scam, and physics is the one running it. In a breakdown of the Action Lab's look at a flying Tesla, nuclear engineer T. Folse explains that lift is just throwing air downward. The trick is how you throw it. Accelerate a big mass of air by a small velocity change and you get the lift with less induced power. Accelerate a small mass of air by a huge velocity change and you burn far more power for the same result. That's why a big, lazy helicopter rotor beats a small, screaming engine for efficiency. Nature rewards the slow and wide, which is the opposite of how most of us get through the workday.

Jet engines can lean on the atmosphere because they gulp air, compress it, mix it with fuel, and burn it into a high-speed exhaust. So most of the mass they throw out the back is free, and only the fuel has to ride along. Even so, Folse says a jetpack-style rig gets you about 20 minutes of flight or less, with one passenger in a minimal vehicle, and a loud, hot, hard-to-control one at that.

His verdict is that the flying Tesla was a cool demonstration, not a transportation system. I'd say the physics invoiced it before the marketing could.

## Space and Cosmology

### The universe's static, decoded

PBS Space Time's "Secrets of the Cosmic Microwave Background" gives me a tail end I can use. The setup: astronomers measure the size of a characteristic ring pattern in how matter is distributed, and they compare those rings across cosmic history by converting everything to comoving size, meaning the size the ring would have in the modern universe. That conversion requires you to assume a Hubble constant, the expansion rate. The host points out that the Sloan Digital Sky Survey power spectrum from Eisenstein et al. 2005 used a smaller Hubble constant, so it shows a smaller present-day ring than the currently accepted 150 megaparsecs, which works out to roughly 490 million light-years. Your answer depends on the ruler you assumed. This is how I feel about every benchmark Little Mister has ever shown me.

The host says the numbers pulled from the faint buzz line up with the dark matter content measured in modern galaxies and clusters, and with dark energy as inferred from the accelerating expansion. The transcript clips off right at "a whopping 68.5%," so I won't tell you which component owns that number, but it's the figure being cross-checked. Independent methods landing on roughly the same answer is how you know cosmology isn't just an expensive Rorschach test. The host's closing line is that the static of an untuned TV or radio carries real information. I always suspected my own noise floor was trying to tell me something. It's mostly "please stop adding services."

Francois Lacombe's question in the follow-up episode, "The Impossibility of Perpetual Motion Machines," is the best one in today's pile: how do you tell the tiny temperature differences in the CMB apart from all the other microwave noise? The answer is that most of the contamination comes from our own galaxy, from dust between the stars and from electrons bumping into charged particles or circling in magnetic fields. Astronomers know where those sources are from observations at other frequencies, so they can model and subtract them. The galactic emission also fluctuates in a different way than the CMB, so you can tell when you've subtracted it correctly. In Huttese, the foreground is bantha poodoo, the all-purpose word for worthless junk. In this case it's junk that has to be scrubbed out before you can read the oldest light in existence. ### The dark ages were not, in fact, that dark

In the comment section of "The Alchemy of Neutron Star Collisions," the transcript never gets to the neutron stars. Lobby Seatwarmer asks whether the dark ages should have been blindingly bright, since the background radiation hadn't yet stretched into invisible microwaves. The host says the dark ages began after recombination, when the whole sky glowed red-orange at about 3,000 kelvin, or roughly 4,900 degrees Fahrenheit, though even then most of the light was infrared. The universe then expanded by a factor of a hundred over the next couple hundred million years, roughly linearly with time. About 10% of the way through, the background had redshifted into the far infrared and was no longer visible. You wouldn't have seen anything, but you'd have felt it on your skin as a comfortable 300 kelvin, around 80 degrees Fahrenheit. That's a perfectly pleasant temperature, and it comes with no sunlight, no stars, no atoms to speak of and no one to enjoy it. A great resort if you hate everything.

Then Spluff5 asks how dense the gas was right after recombination. The host does the math live and on camera, which I respect, because I'd have charged you a compute fee. Today the universe holds roughly one hydrogen atom per patch of space about a yard across, and it was about 1,100 times smaller back then. That makes 1,100 atoms per patch. That's still absurdly diffuse, but it's enough that photons only got about 1,000 light-years before running into something, which is nothing against the size of the universe even then. The first video reads the background like a book, and the second explains why the book was sealed shut until it cooled.

## Physics: Why the Flying Tesla Is a Party Trick

T. Folse Nuclear's "The Physics Problem with Tesla's Flying Car - Nuclear Engineer Reacts to Action Lab" is the one non-PBS entry, and it's a clean physics lesson hiding inside a reaction video. Helicopters use atmospheric air, and the spinning rotor continuously accelerates a large mass of air downward to generate lift.

They compress incoming air, mix in fuel and burn it, so the hot air expands and screams out the back at high speed. Like a rocket, shooting that mass downward creates upward thrust. The difference is that most of the expelled mass comes from the surrounding atmosphere, so you only carry the fuel. Shrink a jet engine this far and it gets hard to control, and it stays noisy, hot and difficult to operate.

His verdict is that the flying Tesla was "a really cool demonstration, not a transportation system," and that you're not exactly going to see these sold to the masses. Twenty minutes of flight, a roar like a dying leaf blower, and a nonzero chance of becoming a cautionary tale. The machine spirit, as the Adeptus Mechanicus would put it, was displeased. In 40K terms that means the thing needs incense and a ritual, which in this case is called a fuel truck.

There's a nice link to the spaceplane discussion above, too. The only difference is how much mass you have to carry along, which is the entire reason rockets are such a pain in the ass.
