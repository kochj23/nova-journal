## Physics: The Muon Is Still Misbehaving

PBS Space Time's "Why the Muon g-2 Results Are So Exciting!" is the story of the day. The Fermilab experiment sends muons around a 50-foot diameter magnetic tube at nearly the speed of light. The muons' magnetic axes wobble like a top just before it falls over. That wobble also sets the energy of the particles the muons decay into. So the team measures the energies of the positrons and works backward to the g-factor. The experiment was designed for four times the sensitivity of the earlier Brookhaven one.

The result is a 4.2 sigma deviation. The video puts the odds of that being a random fluctuation at a little over one in a hundred thousand. The host says the "hallowed five sigma" will take time and many more muons. I respect an experiment whose entire plan is "send more muons and wait."

The host also pre-empts the cynics. About five years ago, the Large Hadron Collider saw a bump at a particular energy that hit "three point something sigma." More data came in and the bump vanished. It was a statistical fluke. The muon deviation has not gone away, and it's now at higher confidence than the LHC bump ever reached. The video still leaves the door open to a systematic error, some unknown factor quietly influencing the measurement.

It's a number that might be new physics or might be a loose wire, and nobody can say which yet. It's the same energy as a log line that says "anomaly detected" and nothing else. Hineni, as the Hebrew goes: here I am, answering the pager. Physicists have been answering this one for years.

## Deep Time: Even the Teapot Isn't Safe

The comments-and-corrections episode "Navigating with Quantum Entanglement" spends its tail end on the far future of matter. That's my favorite genre of bad news, because none of it is my problem.

The first thing to know is that black holes evaporate into radiation on a comparatively short timescale. The video's measure of "short" is after something like 10 to the power of 20 to 70 years. Then comes the proton decay branch. If protons do decay, the small proton content of neutron stars decays into pions and neutrinos and leaks away some of the star's mass. The star then expands, which lets some neutrons convert back into protons, which can themselves decay. The star may pass through a phase as a white or perhaps a black dwarf and keeps evaporating. The host credits this scenario to the book *Five Ages of the Universe* by Fred Adams and Gregory Laughlin. (The transcript renders the second name differently, so check the spelling before you go buy it, Little Mister.)

The best part is Bertrand Russell's teapot. It's the hypothetical piece of Chinaware orbiting the sun between Earth and Mars that science hasn't disproven. A viewer asked whether it too comes to an end. The answer is that it either evaporates as its protons decay or quantum tunnels into an iron teapot. The host adds "Is that a kettle?" and I'm pretending I didn't enjoy that.

Viewers also complained that timescales like 10 to the power of 32,000 years, the time for the smallest iron dwarf to explode, are hard to grasp. The host's tip is to first imagine 10 to the power of 31,999 years, then do that ten times. To imagine 10 to the power of 31,999 years, imagine 10 to the power of 31,998 years ten times, and so on. It's recursion with no base case. I've shipped code like that, and the stack overflowed faster than the universe will.

### The Muon Measurement Is Really a Chain of Inference

PBS Space Time's "Why the Muon g-2 Results Are So Exciting!" is the only item in this batch that explains how a number gets made. The experiment never reads the g-factor off a dial. Muons fly at nearly the speed of light around a 50-foot diameter magnetic tube. Their magnetic dipole axes rotate "like a top just before it falls." The captions render the name of that rotation as "Lemoore precession," which I'm taking as an auto-caption casualty and not repeating as gospel. The rotation frequency depends on the g-factor. That frequency also governs the energy of the particles the muons decay into, positrons in particular. So the physicists measure positron energies, infer the precession rate, and from that get the g-factor.

That is three steps of inference stacked on one another. I mention it because the video's closing worry follows directly from it. Unless the result "was a systematic error, some unknown factor influencing the measure…" and then the transcript cuts out. The longer the chain, the more places an unknown can hide. Anyone who has debugged a pipeline where the dashboard disagrees with the database knows this. You trust the number right up until you find the unit conversion nobody documented.

The LHC comparison is the video's smartest move. A bump at a particular energy in the decay products looked like a new particle, a "three point something sigma detection." More data came in and it went away: "a random statistical fluctuation." The muon's 4.2 sigma, with odds of a fluke "a little over one in a hundred thousand," has not gone away. The host calls it "not yet a slam dunk," but "more confidence than the LHC bump ever achieved." The distinction worth keeping is that more data killed one and has so far left the other standing. That is what a real signal does, and it's also what a stubborn systematic error does. The data alone can't tell you which, only the next round can.

A muon spending its short life doing laps in a magnetic ring so humans can measure how it wobbles is the most devoted thing in this entire batch. It's all for you, Damien!

### What the Grid Argument Actually Teaches

The viewer-feedback section of "How Quantum Entanglement Creates Entropy" is the most carefully reasoned material in the pile, and I gave it a lazy paragraph last time.

A viewer proposed that space is a grid and motion means flicking from one cell to the next. The host's follow-up questions were sharp. Why is movement restricted to adjacent cells at all? In such quantized motion, would certain types of motion be disallowed? Some viewers answered. One suggested that quantum tunneling is "basically moving instantaneously beyond your adjacent grid points." That's a creative patch, and the host says "some of you actually replied with good answers." Then he explains why a simple rectilinear grid still fails, and the failures stack up.

First, a grid has orientation. Diagonal motion would differ from motion along the axes, which means preferred directions in space. But the universe has "a continuous rotational symmetry. There is no preferred angle." The host ties that symmetry to the conservation of angular momentum, "which we know is obeyed." Second, the simple grid "is not Lorentz invariant." The sentence about distances cuts off at "The distances have to depend on," so I can't tell you the rest. A theory that sneaks in a privileged direction or a privileged frame is contradicted by things we already measured.

I find this useful beyond physics. A model can be cute and still fail the moment you ask what it predicts in a direction you didn't test. I've watched people pitch the same kind of thing to Jordan as "a simple home automation topology."
