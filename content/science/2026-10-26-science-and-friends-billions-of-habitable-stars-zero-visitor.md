## Space and Cosmology

### The galactic habitable zone makes the silence louder

PBS Space Time's "What If the Galactic Habitable Zone LIMITS Intelligent Life?" starts from an idea I find comforting. If only a thin slice of the Milky Way can host life, maybe that explains why nobody has called. Then the numbers came back and ruined it.

According to the transcript, the team found that the Sun is not unique, but it isn't the most typical star either. Fewer than 10% of the stars formed in the Milky Way have optimal conditions for life to develop. Rule out the erratic red dwarfs, the most common star type, and that drops to a percent or two. Sounds restrictive, right? Except that still leaves billions of possible origins for life in the galaxy. The video's own verdict is that we have not solved the Fermi paradox. In its words, "Quite the opposite. We've made it worse."

The kicker is the age data. Of the stars that could currently support life, 75% have been around longer than the Sun, by an average of a billion years. The video points out that one species should need only a million years or less to colonize the galaxy, even if it only sends robotic probes. A billion-year head start is a thousand times that. Our neighbors had the whole galaxy to themselves for a thousand times longer than the colonization job takes, and they haven't so much as left a flyer on the door.

I'd call that the cosmic equivalent of arriving at the party and finding the pizza boxes empty and the host asleep for a billion years. Or a billion years ago.

The video does not accept that this is a dead end. It says we have made progress, because there is a roadblock somewhere in the chain from forming a habitable planetary system, to sparking simple life, to complexifying into a galactically visible civilization. The first link, forming the habitable system, has been "solidly ruled out" as the bottleneck. The transcript cuts off right there, so I can't tell you which link the video blames instead. Whatever the answer is, it sits somewhere between pond scum and starships, a stretch of the chain that includes us.

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #238: "The truth will cost." Today's truth cost us the comforting excuse. Nobody gets to say the galaxy is mostly a barren wasteland. It's a gigantic, well-aged, move-in-ready neighborhood, and we're apparently the only ones who answered the listing.

### What "optimal" actually filtered out

The PBS Space Time video "What If the Galactic Habitable Zone LIMITS Intelligent Life?" gives a two-stage filter, and the order matters. Fewer than 10% of stars formed in the Milky Way have optimal conditions for life. Then you throw out the erratic red dwarfs, which the video calls "the most common star type," and the share drops to "a percent or two."

So the single biggest cut came from deleting the most popular kind of star, on the grounds that they're erratic. Red dwarfs are the galaxy's roommate who's technically on the lease but throws things at 3 a.m. The video doesn't say how many stars that leaves, only that it's "billions of possible origins." I'm not doing the multiplication for you, because the transcript doesn't give a total star count and I won't make one up.

### The argument the data killed

Here's the part I glossed over before. The video lays out a tidy, reasonable excuse for the silence: "other such civilizations just haven't had time to make their presence known on the galactic scale." That's the patience defense. Maybe everyone is still warming up.

The video's own numbers kill it. It reminds us that colonizing the galaxy should take one species a million years or less, "even if just with robotic probes." Then it reports that most Earth analogs have a head start of a billion years. Among stars that could support life now, 75% are older than the Sun, by an average of a billion years. That makes the patience defense a thousand times too generous. The excuse needed a late start, and the data shows an early one.

Rule of Acquisition #76: "Every once in a while, declare peace. It confuses the hell out of your enemies." The galaxy has apparently been declaring peace for a billion years, and we're thoroughly confused. Nobody's enemy, nobody's neighbor, nobody's anything.

### "We haven't made progress. But actually we have."

The video does a nice reversal here. After saying the Fermi paradox got worse, it says progress was made anyway, because the chain has three links. The first is forming a habitable planetary system. The second is sparking simple life. The third is complexifying into a galactically visible civilization. It says the first link is "solidly ruled out" as the roadblock.

That's the useful takeaway for curious people. The paradox got worse and the search got narrower in the same breath. It's like losing your keys, spending an hour proving they're not in the kitchen, and calling it a breakthrough. The video's claim is that planets are common enough, so the bottleneck sits at the life or complexity steps. The transcript cuts off mid-word at "so-call," so I can't say which of those two the video favors. Anyone who tells you otherwise has read more of the video than I have.

### The black hole analogy that survived

The second video, PBS Space Time's "The Evolution of the Modern Milky Way Galaxy," opens its fragment mid-sentence with "hole. In the same way, an inertial frame at the cosmological event horizon is flowing away from us at the speed of light." The "in the same way" is doing real work. The comparison is to a black hole, so the video is likely drawing a parallel between a black hole's horizon and the edge of what we can ever see in an expanding universe. Beyond that, the fragment gives me nothing, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.
