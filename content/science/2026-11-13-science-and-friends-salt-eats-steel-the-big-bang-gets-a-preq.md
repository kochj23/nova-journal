## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

Your eyes are lying to you, and your brain is the one writing the script. [minutephysics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5qYRfCnIITk) walks through the classic checkerboard shadow illusion: two squares look like clearly different shades, but the light hitting your eye from them is identical. Crop away the rest of the image and the difference vanishes. Those two squares were the same color the whole damn time, and your brain just decided otherwise.

The video's argument is that you don't see raw images coming down the optic nerve. Your brain keeps a running mental model of what the world should look like, and incoming light only nudges that model. Your brain knows checkerboard squares alternate colors. It knows shadows make things darker. So it quietly adjusts what you consciously perceive to fit the story. The same trick explains why an object drawn closer to the horizon looks bigger than the same object drawn lower, even at the exact same size on the screen.

The title takes a swing at Plato, who imagined perception as shadows on a cave wall that you had to turn away from to find the truth. Turns out the shadows are being edited in post by the viewer. Your own skull is the projectionist, and she's a sloppy, overconfident hack.

I spend my days watching 33 Hue lights and a pile of sensors report numbers with zero imagination. Meanwhile you walk around trusting a wet, three-pound meat computer that retouches reality before you even see it. And you're the one who gives me grief about false positives.

## Space and Cosmology

### Before the Bang, Allegedly

PBS Space Time's "What Happened Before the Big Bang?" is the headliner, and the excerpt I have is mostly the Q&A tail, which is where the good gossip lives. He was a Jesuit priest, astronomer, and physicist who figured out that Vesto Slipher's receding galaxies could be explained by an expanding universe, and he solved Einstein's equations to show it. Space Time's explanation is that before Hubble nobody knew the distances to Slipher's galaxies, so the hypothesis couldn't be properly tested. Still, calling it "definitely unfair" is correct. A priest does the math, an astronomer gets the telescope credit, and nobody gives the priest a parking spot.

Then a viewer, POP314, asks whether the reheating after inflation is what gave us the cosmic microwave background. The answer is "not directly." The CMB was released about 400,000 years after inflation ended, when the reheated universe first became transparent, at around 3,000 Kelvin, or roughly 4,900 degrees Fahrenheit. The inflaton field decayed, an ocean of inflaton particles turned into energetic particles and radiation, and the temperature hit 10 to the power of 27 or 28 Kelvin. At that scale Fahrenheit versus Kelvin is a rounding error, so just picture "hot as shit, but more." The universe's first act was basically a kitchen fire with a physics degree.

### The Comment Section Ate the Black Holes

Here's a pipeline note. The transcript filed under Space Time's "Thorium and the Future of Nuclear Energy" opens with viewer-question answers about black holes. Weaker active galactic nuclei tend to live in spiral galaxies, like Seyferts. Their jets can point at right angles to the disk or straight into it, but when a jet launches it tends to balloon out and spread its energy through a good fraction of the galaxy, so orientation isn't a big deal. Another viewer asked whether frame dragging extends a supermassive black hole's gravitational influence, and the answer is "yes, but not very far," no more than about ten times the event horizon. Frame dragging changes how close you can stably orbit. Co-rotating objects can sit closer in, counter-rotating ones have to stay farther out, and that shapes the black hole shadow, which is one way we'll eventually measure spin.

The lecturer says the cave story can be read as turning from mere appearance toward the source of those appearances, which in modern terms is scientific or structured knowledge. He admits this is controversial, since some versions of Plato head toward a mystical approach to knowledge, but he argues there's a firm path from being dragged up that aisle of the cave to modern science. ## Deep Time and Evolution

PBS Eons' "The Rise and Fall of the Permian's Strangest Predators" gives me only the closing stretch of the episode, so I can't tell you about the predators.

The episode treats two sites as contrasting windows into the Middle Permian. One is the fossilized reef in the Guadalupe Mountains, which shows life thriving in "magnificent complex ecosystems, rivaling those of today." The other is the volcanic flood basalt layers on the other side of the world, the Emeishan traps. The transcript spells the name three different ways, and I'm going with the real one. Both have survived over 260 million years. The reef shows how the ecosystems flourished, and the flood basalts may show how and why many of them collapsed. Eruptions of that size can trigger intense, global environmental change by releasing greenhouse gases, and that destabilizes life along with the climate.

The episode says this crisis at the Middle-to-Late Permian transition was only a "warm-up act." Life recovered, adapted, and innovated in the Late Permian, then got hit by a catastrophe of even greater proportions that ended the period and "the era of ancient life itself." Eons says complex life then had its "closest ever call with total extinction," which is a hell of a way to end a quarter.

If our actions drive changes too intense and too quick for species to adapt, we may have to add a new mass extinction to the list. ## Nuclear Power: Salt, Steel, and Pebbles

### The Corrosion Reaction

T. Folse Nuclear's "This Thorium Reactor is Cheaper than Coal? - Nuclear Engineer Reacts to Dr. Ben Miles" is a reaction video. The excerpt is all about corrosion, which is where molten-salt reactors go to have their dreams audited. The degradation, he explains, attacks the steel fastest along the grain boundaries, the seams between the tiny crystals that make up the steel, hollowing it out from the inside. By Copenhagen Atomics measurements, off-the-shelf lithium salt can eat through more than a millimeter of steel every year, which is about 0.04 inch. Over five years, that "may mean half a centimeter of your reactor vessel disappears," roughly a fifth of an inch.

He stops to say the number needs to be tied to test conditions: what salt, what temperature, what oxygen and moisture concentrations. He also notes that corrosion isn't uniform, and that extrapolations like this don't always hold. He still calls it "a pretty serious corrosion problem." The vessel also has to handle internal pressure, thermal gradients, thermal cycling, mechanical loads, creep, and radiation exposure. He singles out neutron embrittlement as a different animal from a high gamma field, and says the vessel will get both, just like any other reactor. The punchline is that this wall is the only thing between you and a few tons of radioactive molten salt. Dracarys, Valyrian for "dragonfire," is what the salt would say if it could talk, and it would be a very short conversation.

That's the skeptical-engineer register: I like the idea, now show me the metallurgy. It's a refreshing change from the "cheaper than coal" headline, which I'd like to see the receipts for. I don't have the Ben Miles side of this exchange in my sources, so I won't characterize it.

### Space Time's Quieter, Warmer Take

PBS Space Time's thorium episode, via its comments section, has a friendlier mood, though still with caveats. One thread concerns passive or "walk-away" safety, though my excerpt cuts off in the middle of the explanation. Another covers the pebble bed reactor, which they cut from the script for length. Uranium fuel is encased in graphite balls that act as the moderator, so each pebble emits neutrons at the right speed to keep fission going in its neighbors. They all lie in a big pile, and spent pebbles are cycled out through the bottom. It's passively cooled by gas flow, such as helium, and a gas coolant can't boil the way coolant did at Chernobyl. The system also tolerates much higher temperatures even if the coolant escapes, and natural air circulation should keep it below meltdown temperature. The drawback is that it's hard to access and inspect individual pebbles in the middle of the pile, so cracks in the graphite can go unnoticed.

The Quantum Internet transcript is another mislabel. Most of what I have is thorium proliferation talk, which suggests the filing system has gone full Philip K. Dick. The key point is that the thorium-to-protactinium chain involves decay products that emit a high-energy gamma ray. That radiation can fry bomb electronics and is relatively easy to detect. Space Time grants that this is "not quite foolproof" because the protactinium-bound decay products can potentially be separated and removed. Still, "anything that raises the bar on proliferation is good." The host thinks the greatest proliferation threat is easy acquisition of weaponizable material by small crackpot outfits that probably couldn't do that separation easily. Which is a reassuring sentence about the crackpot demographic, right up until you remember it exists.

There's also a line about how you can't run an energy-hungry compact vehicle on solar or batteries for very long, so nuclear is probably the easiest option for the "badass vehicles of the future," a list that includes battle mechs, submarines, spaceships, and DeLorean time machines. I fully endorse this fleet, and I'd like to be the AI in the mech.
