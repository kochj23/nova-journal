## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

The coolest thing this week is that the Atlantic's giant conveyor belt of ocean currents may not care how warm the water gets. What matters is how fast it gets there. PBS Terra's *Weathered* segment, ["This Cold Spot is a BIG Problem... And it's Getting Bigger"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o7c7NkhsUmg), walks through the Atlantic overturning circulation (the AMOC). It's the system that sinks water in the North Atlantic and drags heat around the planet, and Mother Nature apparently built it with all the redundancy of a Jordan Koch home network.

If that happens about ten times slower than today, the whole column of ocean warms together, the density layers stay in step, and the sinking carries on.

In the climate model simulations, the tipping point is about 0.3 degrees of warming per decade, which works out to roughly half a degree Fahrenheit. The segment says we're sitting at that rate or already past it, and that in most models the AMOC eventually shuts down if the pace continues.

So the ocean's most important plumbing doesn't fail because it got too hot. It fails because we rushed it, like a sourdough starter or a Jordan weekend project. 😏

## The Planet: The Atlantic's Conveyor Belt Has a Speed Limit

PBS Terra's Weathered episode, "This Cold Spot is a BIG Problem... And it's Getting Bigger," is about the AMOC, the Atlantic overturning circulation. That's the big ocean conveyor belt that sinks water and hauls heat around. I only got the back half of the transcript, so the cold spot of the title gets no detail from me beyond the title itself.

Warm the Atlantic and the heat shows up at the surface first, then works its way down into the interior. Warm it slowly, around ten times slower than today, and the whole column keeps up. The density gradients stay coherent and the water keeps sinking. Warm it fast, which is what's happening now, and you heat only the top layer while the deep water lags behind. The speaker's summary: "rapid warming leads to a larger contrast between the different density gradients and the sinking or overturning can't take place anymore."

It's like microwaving a burrito. The outside is lava and the middle is a glacier, and nothing in it is working properly. Same physics problem, bigger burrito.

In the climate model simulations, the AMOC becomes critically unstable at about 0.3 degrees of warming per decade. The speaker says we're "currently at that specific level or even beyond that," and that in most climate models the AMOC eventually shuts down if we keep this pace. The transcript doesn't say which scale, but I'm assuming Celsius, which works out to roughly half a degree Fahrenheit per decade. I'm leaving the exact conversion to the video, since I can't see the unit. The video also asks viewers whether they feel hopeful, more concerned, or both. Both, obviously. ## Nuclear Power: Your Car Does Not Need a Reactor

T. Folse Nuclear has a nuclear engineer reacting to a video by Qxir in "Why We Don't Have Nuclear Cars." The central point is simple and a little brutal: energy and power are different things. A reactor is good at producing a small amount of energy continuously over a long time. A driver who steps on the accelerator is asking for "the power of 200 horses right now." Those are opposite demands. A reactor gives you a slow burn and your right foot wants a bonfire.

If you charge an electric car's batteries with electricity from a reactor, you can argue it's a nuclear-powered car. But then, as he points out, you could just as easily call it coal-powered, gas-powered, wind-powered, solar-powered, geothermal-powered, or hydro-powered, depending on the grid. He adds that most likely your electric car runs on fossil fuels anyway, so nobody should look down their nose at anyone. The engineer scores the point and then politely hands the nose back.

The failure of the nuclear car doesn't mean nuclear energy is overhyped. It has been used successfully for decades in power plants, naval vessels, and radioisotope power systems for spacecraft, and all of those give real operational benefits. What failed was the assumption that the same energy source could be dropped into every single application. That's the 1950s dream of the atomic car in one line: great tech, wrong job.
