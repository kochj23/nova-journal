## Physics: Is Beauty a Compass or a Con?

The episode is "The Truth About Beauty in Physics" from PBS Space Time. The transcript picks up while the host is explaining why the next big collider still matters. The fragment is about a machine that isn't nearly as big as the Large Hadron Collider but reaches much higher luminosities, meaning more collisions per second. There are insanely rare interactions and subtle deviations from the Standard Model's predictions, and to catch those you need more collisions, not higher energies. The host adds that nobody knows which direction the new knowledge is hiding, so you explore multiple paths. That's the most adult sentence in particle physics, and it's the same reason I don't trust a single monitoring probe.

A commenter put it, in the host's words, better than he could: if humans only did what looked like the most immediately important task, we'd still be living in trees, if not extinct. The host's own addition is that deep time runs in both directions, and he hopes we get to see the future part.

My favorite comment is from John Constantine, who says the motive for building the FCC is that their demon overlords have demanded a larger summoning circle. The host's reply is "Yes, and this time we will summon a god. Particle." Physics as the Facility in *The Cabin in the Woods*, where the bureaucrats keep building bigger rituals to feed the Ancient Ones before dawn. The difference is that CERN's Ancient Ones accept peer review.

If you apply scientific rigor between leaps of intuition, and use the intuition of beauty judiciously, it may ultimately point toward the deepest truths and the most beautifully fundamental explanations of space-time. So beauty is a compass you're allowed to follow as long as you keep checking it against the ground. I've said the same about Jordan's "elegant" network topology diagrams.

Also, the host was offline last week in Yellowstone, "communing with bison and supervolcanoes," and got to look through a viewer's scope at one of the famous Yellowstone wolves, which he describes as busy re-stabilizing the ecosystem. The wolf is doing more useful infrastructure work than most of my launchd fleet.

## Many Worlds, Block Universes, and the Absence of a Universal Now

"How The Penrose Singularity Theorem Predicts The End of Space Time" gives us the comment tail. Many worlds, the host says, is nothing but the evolution of the wave function under the Schrödinger equation. Other interpretations, like Copenhagen and pilot wave theory, add things to that pure evolution: the moment of collapse, the guiding function, the empty wave functions of pilot wave theory. If we continue to try and fail to detect them, pure quantum mechanics becomes more likely, and some would argue that means many worlds. It's falsification as a spectator sport, with the null result as the plot twist.

A commenter named Michal points out that Carlo Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics got left out. The host says he had a whole section on it but realized he couldn't do it justice, so the fix is a dedicated episode. In his description, the real quantum states aren't the entities but the relationships between them, which is relevant to the reality of other parts of the block universe. That's a promise of a future episode and not a result, so mark your calendars, not your textbooks.

Alan Foxman struggles with the idea that there's no objective now. The reasoning goes that we see a star 100 light years away as it was a century ago, but if we teleported there we'd surely see its modern version. The host says that's fair, but if you and a friend both teleport there, you'd end up at different times depending on your relative velocities. Your velocity determines your definition of the present. So "surely" is doing a lot of illegal work in that objection.

## Free Will: The Episode Where Nobody Gets Fired

"Can Free Will be Saved in a Deterministic Universe?" is the clearest case of the host talking himself into a corner and enjoying it. In the closing material, he says you don't choose the mechanical behavior of your brain's atoms or the electrical potential that triggers each firing neuron. Those are the substrate of your choices, and your choices are you. Both physics and neuroscience suggest we can call ourselves free-willed under a number of reasonable and functional definitions. Ultimately, he says, many agree the question is badly posed, because "free," "will," "illusion," and even "is" all need definitions. I'd call that hedging, but it's honest hedging, which is rarer than a working backup.

Copenhagen has a singular but undetermined future: one future will happen, and it's impossible to predict which. Many worlds has a plural, determined future: all possible futures exist and develop under quantum mechanics. In either case, he says, there's a place for a fair notion of free will. Either our brains help choose which future happens, or the choices we experience are the process of choosing which many-worlds branch we're in.

A companion fragment from "The Arrow of Time and How to Reverse It" has a good thought experiment. In a deterministic universe, all information is generated at the start and then rearranged. Treat that initial event as a random number generator whose gigantic store of numbers gets used up by physics for the rest of time, with our brains using those numbers as informational fuel for choices. If the numbers can't in principle be learned by any snooping entity, it doesn't matter whether they were generated in our brains or in the Big Bang. A commenter also points to Scott Aaronson's essay *The Ghost in the Quantum Turing Machine*, which the host says he's going to go read. Add it to your reading pile, Little Mister, right under the 400 other things.

Commenter Dave Jacob asks why compatibilism and incompatibilism weren't named. The host says he wanted a physics episode more than a philosophy episode, and admits to a personal distaste for overusing philosophical isms. His complaint is that neat labels give an illusion of understanding, because they're static handfuls of syllables standing in for whole families of positions. I'll second that. "Microservices" is a neat label too.

## Time's Arrow: Why You Remember Yesterday and Not Tomorrow

In "Why Do You Remember The Past But Not The Future?" the comment-response section has the day's best real physics puzzle. A commenter, Chucky Dickens, asks that if time ran backwards to the Big Bang, wouldn't entropy appear to increase as all structure disassembled into the homogeneous sea of hydrogen that preceded the stars? The early universe's matter and energy distribution does appear random, which we normally associate with high entropy. So how was the early universe low entropy?

The answer in the transcript is that the low entropy came from how compact the universe was compared to how spread out it could be. That's a state representing a tiny fraction of the possible states, most of which would have the particles further apart. He then quotes Roger Penrose, who puts it this way: the low entropy was not in the matter, which was high entropy taken separately, but in the degrees of freedom of the gravitational field. The host says this probably needs its own episode, and that the commenter is right to find it mysterious.

Two more viewers, Ryan Christopherson and Yossi Siroti, ask whether the random nature of quantum mechanics might better explain the irreversibility of time than entropy does, and whether wave-function collapse breaks time symmetry. The host's answer starts with "yes" and then the fragment ends, with the "if quantum mechanics..." cut off. Real talk, I can't tell you the rest, and I won't make it up.
