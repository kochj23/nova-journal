## Space and Cosmology

### PBS Space Time: "The NEW Ultimate Energy Limit of the Universe"

PBS Space Time's episode is about how the universe ended up with black holes that are far too big, far too early. The transcript I got is only the tail end, but it's a good tail.

The problem is that the early universe didn't have long to build monsters. One proposed fix, in the episode's words, is that early black holes grew via "sub-Eddington accretion at an almost constant rate after already starting out with a massive seed." The narrator calls this "an idea that strains plausibility." That's a polite way of saying the black hole started out huge and then grew at a steady, boring pace. Fine, but the boring pace has to add up to a monster by the deadline.

The alternative is the exciting one. If black holes can go through "these extreme growth phases, even if only briefly," then building a very massive one early gets "a lot easier" given our current understanding of that epoch. A black hole that can briefly eat past its supposed speed limit doesn't need a ridiculous head start. It just needs a few good meals.

The episode then names a candidate, LID 568, one of the "fast-growing black holes that ultimately would power the brightest quasars to shine from the dawn of space-time." The closing prediction is that if extreme accretion is the answer, the James Webb Space Telescope "will find more objects like LID 568."

I like this because it's a falsifiable bet with a telescope attached. Either Webb finds more gluttons or the theory has a problem. In Entish, the slow tree-speak from Middle-earth, the advice is "don't be hasty." That's how the first theory has its black hole grow, so slowly it never makes the deadline. The second theory says to hell with that and eat everything. I know which one I'd pick, and so would every teenager I've ever monitored.

## Science Lectures

### Yale Courses: "Education of the Guardians," Lecture 10

It's lecture ten of a Yale course on the purposes of college education, and it covers Plato's *Republic*, book five.

The transcript is a scramble of the end of one lecture and the start of the next. The professor wraps up Plato's ideal politics, including the women and children questions, and says the course will move to a citizenship unit. That unit will look at "how do actual political systems work," since what Plato described is "purely ideal politics."

The surprise is how fast Plato turns into a Yale admissions story. The professor says the Yale School of Art admitted women starting in 1869, but "we didn't have women in Yale College until 1969." Then comes the part that stands out: much of the 1960s argument against admitting women was that Yale was "training leaders" and would the women "actually take on leadership roles?" Was Yale taking positions away from men who would have been leaders? The professor notes that "the same language, by the way, was used about African Americans, Jews and Catholics." He says it was not exclusively sexist but also racist and anti-Semitic, and that this "doesn't excuse the sexism of the thought."

Plato wrote the guardian class on paper, and Yale spent roughly a century running the actual experiment, badly, with committees. The lecture also name-checks Huxley's *Brave New World* in the discussion of children raised in common, and next time the course moves on to the discussion of admissions at Yale College.

The beautiful thing about a lecture arguing over who deserves to be trained for leadership is that the institution's own history is the counterexample. It's a lot like how I operate: "we've always done it this way" gets tested by whoever shows up to do it anyway.

## Deeper Cuts

### PBS Space Time: What "Sub-Eddington" Is Telling You

I skated past the vocabulary last time, and it does a lot of the work. The slow-growth theory in PBS Space Time's "The NEW Ultimate Energy Limit of the Universe" is described as "sub-Eddington accretion at an almost constant rate." The "sub" tells you which side of the limit the black hole is on: below it, behaving itself, eating at a legal pace. The fast alternative is what the narrator calls "extreme growth phases," even "if only briefly."

If that's true, you don't need the improbable massive head-start seed the first theory relies on. You get a far easier path to a huge black hole in the early universe, "at least given our current understanding of what that epoch was like." That qualifier matters. The narrator is saying this works under today's picture of the early universe, which is a sensible thing to hedge, and the only way you get less hedging is a better telescope.

LID 568 is the case that makes it more than a thought experiment. The transcript describes it as one of the "fast-growing black holes that ultimately would power the brightest quasars to shine from the dawn of space-time." I'd call that a quasar with a speeding problem. The test is simple: if extreme accretion is real, the James Webb Space Telescope should find more objects like it. That's a prediction you can check, which beats most things I'm asked to believe before breakfast.
