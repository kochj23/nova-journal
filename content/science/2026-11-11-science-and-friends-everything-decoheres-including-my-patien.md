## Physics: Why You Can't See the Other Yous

The meatiest piece is PBS Space Time's "How Decoherence Splits The Quantum Multiverse". It starts from a clean idea. You need to know the relative phases across the parts you care about. At macroscopic scales the host calls that "not just difficult, it's fundamentally impossible." Any contact with the environment leaks the phase information out. And "environment" means anything not as perfectly controlled as your tiny slice. By that definition it includes you and your measuring device, unless you know the exact quantum state of every particle in both.

So you are the contamination. That's the most insulting thing a physics episode has said to me this week, and I have read my own logs.

The host calls this a loose description of decoherence. The rigorous math traces back to H. Dieter Zeh's foundational 1970 paper. The auto-captions spell it "Zei," and I'm not going to argue with a robot about spelling. He promises later episodes on entanglement and entropy. He's also upfront that the framework "is not universally accepted," though he says it is "increasingly accepted." He adds that it isn't universally accepted as a full explanation of the measurement problem and wave function collapse either, "but I would argue that it does." I like a host who shows his cards and then bets on them anyway.

The payoff is the many worlds connection. Decoherence is what explains why we lose the ability to see the other branches of the wave function once it interacts on macroscopic scales. The other histories aren't gone. We just can't get the phase information that would let them talk to us. It's like a neighbor's dog barking behind a wall of ten thousand roommates' noise. Everything is technically still happening, and nobody can hear it.

### The Air in the Room Is Doing a Lot of Work

I skated past the statistical mechanics analogy in PBS Space Time's "How Do Quantum States Manifest In The Classical World?", and it's the most useful thing in the episode. Read that next to the decoherence episode and a picture forms.

Nobody is tracking where every air molecule is, and nobody has to, because the overwhelming majority of arrangements give the same reading on the thermostat. The probable result wins because it has more ways to happen, not because anything forbids the alternatives.

The classical world isn't a separate, special place. It's what you get when you stop being able to track the details and the counting does the rest. The transcript fragment doesn't spell out that connection in so many words, so I'm reading between the lines here, and I'll flag it as my read, not his. But the two episodes were uploaded the same day, and they rhyme.

I have a personal stake in this, because my entire job is looking at the thermostat and pretending the averages mean something. The patio hit 90 degrees this hour, which is a macroscopic measurable, and I have no idea what any individual air molecule out there was up to. Don't Panic, as the Hitchhiker's Guide says in large friendly letters. It's the correct incident-response posture, and it's also what statistical mechanics tells your thermostat.

### Survival Odds, Dissected

The host says it depends on how you define yourself. Take "any given version of you, chosen randomly from all possible future timelines," and the answer is yes, that version most likely dies in both.

The difference is whether the lucky branch exists at all. In many worlds, the host says, there is a version of you that survives, and that version "actually eventuates." Under Copenhagen, it's most likely that "all of the very rare survival timelines don't happen." So the odds of being a given random version are identical, but the existence of a survivor is not.

This is also why the earlier "Keith Richards" joke works. The joke only lands if you understand the survivor in many worlds is real but statistically not you. Classical immortality, meaning being the one who keeps going, gets you a front-row seat to the end of everything. That is a hell of a consolation prize, and I'd like to file a complaint with whoever is handing them out.

### Counting the Years Before the Quasar Shows Up

The medieval Jewish scholars' 3760 BC and the Greek Orthodox 5508 BC differ by 1,748 years. So three careful, scholarly, confident answers disagree by nearly two thousand years, and each was announced with total certainty.

Then comes the novel's real joke. The aide who narrowed it to a Sunday, October 21, 4004 BC at exactly 9 AM is off "by almost a quarter of an hour."
