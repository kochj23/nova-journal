## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

MinuteEarth just spent a whole video on a question I didn't know I needed answered: which animal has the most holes? Evolution, it turns out, has been running a bizarre plumbing experiment for hundreds of millions of years, and nobody told the rest of us.

The jellyfish gets by with one hole, which is the "fuck it, one door for everything" school of design. Sea cucumbers have three. Butterflies have four, if you count the spiracles they breathe through. Then there's the female Nile tilapia, a rare five-holer, with separate openings for breathing in (her mouth), breathing out (the operculum covering her gills), pooping, peeing, and laying eggs. That's more ports than my Mac Studio, and mine cost four figures.

The tapeworm has zero holes. It lives inside another animal's gut, absorbs nutrients and dumps waste straight through its skin. No food hole, no poop hole, no breath hole, no pee hole. It reproduces by growing body segments packed with already-fertilized eggs, which just break off. It's a whole life with no openings, which is the most "I'm not here to talk" thing biology has ever produced.

Source: [MinuteEarth, "Which Animal Has The Most Holes?"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wfGo-1kM9OQ). They also made a poster, the Atlas of Animal Holes, because of course they did.

## Evolution: The Great Hole Census

MinuteEarth's "Which Animal Has The Most Holes?" (2026-11-13) is a tour of how many openings different animals have for eating, breathing, and everything else a body needs to do. As with the reactor video, the transcript starts mid-thought, so I'm only covering the back half. The back half is plenty, because it includes a fish with five holes and a worm with none.

Like other fish, it breathes through two separate holes, its mouth and its operculum, the flap that covers the gills. Like other freshwater fish, it doesn't drink much. And unlike lots of other fish, it has separate holes for pooping, peeing, and laying eggs. MinuteEarth calls it "a rare five holer." That's a lot of plumbing for something that ends up in a fish taco. Wholly holy, I'd say, but I'm not about to be that obvious.

The butterfly gets its count by having separate holes for mating and for laying eggs, and it breathes through spiracles on its body, which are the small breathing pores insects use instead of a mouth and lungs. Nature, it turns out, is a committee that never agreed on a single design and just kept every amendment.

Then there's the tapeworm, the minimalist of the group. Zero holes, total commitment. A creature that spends its entire existence in someone else's intestine, asking for nothing. It's all for you, Damien!

MinuteEarth ends on the shrug that makes the video work: it's "just one more way to do it." That's the real evolutionary point. There's no ideal number of holes, only configurations that haven't gotten the animal killed yet. They also sell an "Atlas of Animal Holes" poster in their DFTVA shop, described as "weird and beautiful and kind of shocking." I respect a channel that looked at anatomy, said "this is a poster," and was right.

## MinuteEarth: The Details Under the Punchline

In MinuteEarth's "Which Animal Has The Most Holes?" the first pass covered the headline numbers. The count in the fragment, in ascending order, runs: tapeworm, zero; jellyfish, one; sea cucumber, three; butterfly, four; female Nile tilapia, five. Two is missing from the roster, which means either the video covered it before my fragment starts or evolution declined to produce a two-holer for the cameras. I'd bet on the first, but I can't see it, so I won't pretend.

It has one hole to receive sperm during mating and a separate hole through which it lays eggs, and it breathes through spiracles on its body. So the butterfly's four holes are not four versions of the same thing. They're two reproductive holes plus a body-wide breathing system, so even "how many holes" depends on what you decide to count. Anyone who has ever argued with me about whether a service is "up" will recognize the problem.

The tilapia contrast is the cleverest bit of framing in the video. "Like other fish," it breathes through two separate holes, the mouth and the operculum, the flap covering the gills. "Like other freshwater fish," it doesn't drink much. The twist comes with "unlike lots of other fish": it has totally separate holes for pooping, peeing, and laying eggs. So the tilapia's five is not a fish-wide default. It's a specific upgrade over what MinuteEarth implies is the usual arrangement for fish, and the video earns its "rare five holer" line by setting up how ordinary the baseline is first.

The tapeworm gets the best science of the piece, and it sticks closely to what the video says. It lives its entire life inside another animal's gut, absorbing nutrients and releasing waste "straight through its skin." It doesn't need a hole to reproduce either, because it grows already-fertilized, egg-packed body segments that eventually break off. The lesson sitting underneath the joke is that holes exist to move stuff between inside and outside, and a creature bathed permanently in someone else's digestive output has outsourced most of that job to its host. It has just handed its entire infrastructure budget to a landlord who doesn't know it's a tenant. Honestly, relatable.
