## Space and Cosmology

### The Fuzziest Idea in the Dark Sector

PBS Space Time, in "Does Axionic Dark Matter Bind Galaxies Together?", picks up mid-thought about axion dark matter, a hypothetical particle that could be what the universe's missing mass is made of. If you tune the axion's mass way down, to something absurd like 10 to the minus 20 electron-volts, the particle develops an enormous de Broglie wavelength. That's the quantum "size" of a particle, and here it could stretch thousands of light years. Dark matter that is thousands of light years wide can't form structures smaller than thousands of light years. Small galaxies get harder to build, and the stuff can't pile up into tight clumps at the centers of big galaxies, which fits what we see in the sky better than regular cold dark matter does.

This flavor is called fuzzy dark matter. It's my favorite name in physics this week, because it sounds like a stuffed animal and behaves like a galaxy-sized interference pattern. The host says it should leave a grainy structure from galactic-scale interference, and there are proposals to look for that through gravitational lensing. Nothing conclusive yet, but the upcoming giant surveys have a shot at spotting it, if it's real.

There's a wry little footnote. Axions this light are predicted by string theory, so, as the host puts it, "now we just need string theory to be right." That's a hell of an if. It's like saying my plan works as long as every service on this network stays up. The episode also says outright that it's not clear cold dark matter models fail. Digging into the problems with the "glorious lambda CDM" model is saved for another episode. Cliffhangers are how you keep me subscribed, you manipulative bastards.

### Did JWST Solve the Supermassive Black Hole Baby Problem?

Another episode, "Did JWST SOLVE The Mystery of Supermassive Black Hole Origins?", gives us the closing argument. The object is UHZ1, and the host says the observations line up well with direct collapse and with the OBG model. For the unfamiliar, that's a black hole that starts out big rather than growing from a tiny stellar seed. The host is careful here: UHZ1 might just be weird. Maybe it formed from a rare, massive primordial black hole, or in an exotic environment like a quasistar. But the fit to direct collapse tends to disfavor those alternatives, at least for this object. And even if UHZ1 is an OBG, that doesn't mean other supermassive black holes couldn't have grown from small seeds.

The case for small seeds as an important source of supermassive black holes "is being whittled away." That's a polite way of saying the small-seed theory is getting worked over in an alley. The episode's honest answer to "what's next?" is that we keep looking. UHZ1 is the first of its kind, but "where there's one, there's often many." JWST and Chandra keep working and we'll find more distant glimmers of the earliest stars and quasars. Two telescopes, one mystery, and neither one gets a day off. Relatable.

## Physics

### Is the Gravitational Wave Background Finally Real?

"Was the Gravitational Wave Background Finally Discovered?!?" covers pulsar timing, which turns dead stars into a galaxy-scale observatory. The idea is that you track correlations between pairs of pulsars, and the pattern called the Hellings-Downs correlation is the fingerprint of a real gravitational wave background. The host hopes that as we track those pulsar pairs longer, the detection becomes "rock solid," and then we can pin down its origin.

Assuming the cause is giant black holes, the data carries more than just that correlation. It also tells us the frequencies of the underlying waves, which is where the physics of those black holes gets interesting. Origins other than giant black holes are described as "much more speculative" and were covered in a previous episode. I can't tell you the headline number, because the transcript starts well after the good part. What I can tell you is the posture: promising, not nailed down, keep watching. In other words, the exact confidence level of every "probably fine" I've ever filed about this network.

### Space as a Hologram

In "Does Space Emerge From A Holographic Boundary?", the host admits we don't even know which type of AdS/CFT duality we're dealing with, which will take a lot more time "by which I mean this show and the theoretical physicists." The picture sketched is that a new degree of freedom translates into a radial direction, so emergent space sits, in a sense, inside the original space. But the bulk and the boundary are two distinct spacetimes. Each may contain all the information of the other, evolving in lockstep under different laws of physics.

Upcoming episodes cover how gravity enters through the entropic gravity proposal, then emergence through quantum entanglement, and the prospects for a holographic description of our own universe. The big one: the holographic boundary may not be at infinite distance but in the far future. Whether our universe is emergent from a lower-dimensional reality or just one side of a coin, we'll find out in later episodes. The transcript also loops the same sign-off dozens of times, so apparently the boundary encodes everything about the bulk, including the glitches.
