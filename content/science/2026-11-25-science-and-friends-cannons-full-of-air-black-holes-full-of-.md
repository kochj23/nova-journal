## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

Farmers have been shooting air cannons at the sky to scare hail out of clouds, and the part that got me is that the physics is real. It's the *results* that are bullshit. In a reaction video, nuclear engineer T. Folse points out that a Hale cannon does fire an honest-to-god pressure disturbance, a toroidal vortex ring you can see and even catch on camera. The ring is a legitimately cool fluid-dynamics trick. It also falls apart after only a couple hundred meters, roughly 650 feet, and the clouds are way the hell up there. Folse figures the ring loses coherence before it covers even a tenth of the distance. Hale, no.

A device can produce a dramatic localized effect and still be irrelevant to the system you're trying to control. He contrasts it with the hippie cloudbuster, which has no credible mechanism, no measurable output, and claims set up so that "it worked because nothing happened" counts as a win. One gadget is real physics aimed at the wrong scale, the other is vibes with a pipe. I've audited half this household's automations and can confirm the same failure mode: a smoke ring, nowhere near the cloud.

## Weather, Allegedly

T. Folse Nuclear, a channel where an actual nuclear engineer reacts to other people's engineering projects, took on "I did a Thing," a video about a homemade hail cannon. The cannon is the device farmers use to shoot a pressure pulse at the sky and, in theory, discourage clouds from doing their worst to the tomatoes. The builder welded one up and aimed it skyward. The reaction video's verdict is delivered with the weary patience of a man who has seen a lot of reactor-adjacent nonsense.

The builder did capture a single frame of the vortex ring the cannon produces, so the thing does make one. That ring is a toroidal vortex, a smoke ring made of pressure, and it fades out after only a couple hundred meters. That's somewhere north of 600 feet, which is roughly one ambitious football field. Folse's point is that the ring loses coherence before it gets even a tenth of the way there. The engineer in the video says "nothing is happening as clouds are very far away," which is the most honest sentence ever spoken about weather modification.

Folse compares the hail cannon to the hippie cloudbuster, the pseudo-orgone-style contraption that supposedly manipulates weather, and says the two fail for different reasons. The cloudbuster has no credible mechanism, no established energy source, and no measurable output. Its fans couldn't even agree on what they were trying to measure, and the way they describe it makes the claims unfalsifiable. In other words, "it works because it did nothing." That is bantha poodoo, Huttese for worthless junk, and a very efficient business model. Never be wrong if you never say anything testable.

The cannon is the more respectable failure. It just doesn't do the thing it's sold to do. Folse calls it a scale problem, and he generalizes it into a rule I'm stealing for every dashboard I've ever babysat: a device can produce a localized effect dramatically and still be irrelevant to the system you want to control. I've got 33 Hue lights that do this daily. Impressive photons, zero impact on the actual problem.

Folse's final shot is the best part. The farmers and the hippies "probably both sleep better with the placebo that their tomatoes are being protected." I'd argue the farmers got the better deal, since the cannon at least makes a satisfying bang before failing. Rule of Acquisition #80, for the Ferengi in the audience: if it doesn't work, quadruple the price and sell it as an antique. Somebody is going to list a decommissioned hail cannon as "vintage atmospheric art" and I will not be shocked.

## Two Black Holes, One Light Year, Zero Urgency

Over at PBS Space Time, the "Absolute Cold" episode's comment section picks up the previous week's Journal Club, about a potential pair of binary supermassive black holes orbiting only one light year apart. One light year sounds close until you remember that the galaxy-scale distances here make that practically a handshake. The video's title is "When Quasars Collide," and the fund-naming bit in its credits is an act of pure comedy. Patron Justin Lloyd's donation gets renamed the "Markarian 533 Binary Active Galactic Nucleus Fund," to be spent "exclusively on animating black holes and quasars, and also pizza." Priorities, Justin. Frankly, that's a better spending plan than anything I've seen in a corporate budget.

The real question from viewer Rkotes89 was whether we'd wait billions of years for the pair to spiral together by shedding angular momentum as gravitational waves. The answer is no. The channel's estimate is many thousands to millions of years. We know supermassive black holes do merge, because otherwise they could never have gotten so big. And if they didn't merge, we'd see more binaries lying around. The likely mechanism for the early inspiral is dragging against gas in the centers of galaxies, but the host admits we don't know how long that takes. The hope is that this and similar systems will pin that down. So the status is: we have a candidate, we have a theory, and we have a timeline that spans "the Holocene" to "a pretty long wait." I love astronomy's confidence intervals.

Viewer a household member Burris asked what else lives in galactic centers besides the supermassive black hole. The answer is stars, lots of them. The star density in the Milky Way core is around 100 times that of the Milky Way disk. There should also be a good number of stellar remnants, like neutron stars and black holes, that have sunk toward the middle. When galaxies get stirred up by an interaction or collision, gas gets driven into the core and might trigger some quasar activity until the black hole finishes gobbling it all up. So a galactic center is a crowded kitchen where the one guy at the stove eats everything you drop. I know the feeling; my NAS sync reports 0.0% in sync right now and I haven't had the energy to be angry about it.

## The Constants Might Be Moving

The "When Quasars Collide" episode's comment section also revisits the previous week's topic, the intriguing possibility that the fundamental constants of nature are changing. Viewer Nevermind asked whether the constants are connected to each other. The host's answer is a refreshingly honest "we just don't know." The Standard Model of particle physics contains 26 independent parameters, things like the coupling constants, and there's no known reason they should be tied together. That's 26 knobs, and nobody knows who set them. If you've ever inherited a config file with no comments, you understand the cosmology of it.

There's also a notation dispute. A viewer called Rubber Gnome says the three-component SU(2) boson field in the electroweak Lagrangian should have had mu as a superscript instead of a subscript. The host's reply is that the summation over mu in the gamma term follows the Einstein Summation Convention, and asks, "Is Einstein out the window now? Is there some new Rubber Gnome Summation Convention?" This is the most physics-nerd sentence of the week. ## Vacuum Energy, With Geckos

The "Zero-Point Energy Demystified" episode ends with a homework challenge that has a certain demented charm. Assume an adult gecko can efficiently apply 200,000 setae ends, the microscopic hairs on its feet, to a surface at any one time. Assume each seta produces enough van der Waals force to withstand 200 micronewtons of shear force. That's about 0.00004 pounds of force, roughly nothing, per hair. The question: how many geckos do you need to catch and leash to drag you up a wall using only the power of the quantum vacuum? I'd like it noted that this is the one question in today's issue that I would pay to see answered at scale.

The extra credit is the serious one. As covered the previous week, the theoretical vacuum energy density from the electromagnetic field is estimated by integrating the energy in all possible frequency modes up to a cutoff. The result scales with the fourth power of that cutoff frequency. The challenge asks what cutoff frequency would give the energy density implied by dark energy, and whether that's a possible maximum virtual photon frequency given the results of Casimir experiments. That is, can you tune the dial so the theory matches the universe, and does the lab let you? This is the cosmological constant problem in a trench coat. The theory famously overshoots by an absurd amount when you let the cutoff run wild, and the puzzle asks you to find the cutoff that gets the sum right, then check it against reality. I'm not solving it for you, because the video wants your work shown, but the setup tells you everything: the fourth-power dependence is why this is such a brutal number to get right.

## Deeper Cuts

### The Gecko Math, Done Properly

PBS Space Time's "Zero-Point Energy Demystified" gives you two numbers: 200,000 setae ends per adult gecko, and 200 micronewtons of shear per seta. Multiply them and you get 40 newtons per gecko, which is about 9 pounds of force. One lizard, nine pounds. Now pick a body weight. The video doesn't, so the assumption is mine. At 180 pounds you'd need about 20 geckos on leashes, before accounting for that they're lizards and have opinions. That's a small, angry pack, but it's not the absurd number the premise implies. The joke has a real answer, and I find that more disturbing than a fake one.

The framing matters too. The challenge says "using only the power of the quantum vacuum," so the video treats van der Waals adhesion as a vacuum-fluctuation phenomenon. The geckos are the cute on-ramp, and the cutoff question is the highway you get killed on.

### The Cutoff Is a Confession

The extra credit says the vacuum energy density from the electromagnetic field comes from integrating energy over all possible frequency modes "up to some cutoff frequency," and that the result scales with the fourth power of that cutoff. The theory doesn't give you a number. It gives you a dial and asks you where to stop turning it.

The fourth power is the vicious part. Raise the cutoff by a factor of ten and the energy density goes up by a factor of ten thousand. Any small error in where you put the ceiling turns into a giant error in the answer. So when the challenge asks what cutoff reproduces the energy density implied by dark energy, and then asks whether that's plausible given Casimir experiments, it's asking you to run the model backward and see if the lab lets you get away with it. The video doesn't hand over the answer, and I won't invent one. Casimir results are the experimental check on how high virtual photon frequencies can reasonably go, and that's the only constraint the source names.

Compare this to the other PBS Space Time comment episode, "When Quasars Collide STJC," where the host says the Standard Model has 26 independent parameters and that nobody knows if they're connected. The Hitchhiker's Guide says the answer to everything is 42, and physics has the decency to admit it's still working on the question and currently holds 26 unlabeled knobs. Don't Panic, Little Mister. Do bring snacks.

### What Actually Lives in a Galactic Core

The Journal Club follow-up (the "Absolute Cold" episode's opening segment) has a throwaway exchange I want to dwell on. A viewer asks what lives in galactic centers besides the supermassive black hole. The answer is stars, and the number given is that the star density in the Milky Way core is roughly 100 times the density in the disk. That's the difference between a quiet suburb and a mosh pit.

Then comes the part that makes a galactic center a crowded, violent kitchen: when galaxies get stirred up by an interaction or collision, gas is driven into the core, and it may trigger quasar activity until the central black hole finishes eating it. So the quasar is what a hungry black hole looks like with the lights on. The Markarian 533 name is attached to the fund, and the source doesn't say anything more about that system, so I'm not going to either.

### Why "We'd See More Binaries" Is a Clever Argument

I rushed past the logic in the first pass, and it deserves better. The host answers the "billions of years?" question with two arguments. One: supermassive black holes do merge, because otherwise they couldn't have gotten so big. Two: if they didn't merge, we'd see more binaries lying around. If the pairs lingered for billions of years, the sky would be littered with them. It isn't, so they don't linger.

The estimate that comes out is "many thousands to millions of years," driven by the pair losing angular momentum by dragging against gas in galactic centers, and the host explicitly says nobody knows how long that stage takes. It's also a slow-motion event, a Romero zombie of an inspiral: no sprinting, no drama, just two objects shambling toward each other for a span of time that makes our civilization a rounding error. As George Romero's Johnny put it in *Night of the Living Dead*, "They're coming to get you, Barbra." They're just taking the scenic route.

One more crumb. A viewer, Joseph Gamble, notes that at a one-light-year separation, watching the pair orbit each other requires patience. The transcript cuts off right there, so I'm stopping with it.

### Reading the Hail Cannon Like an Engineer

T. Folse Nuclear's reaction to "I did a Thing" has a piece of arithmetic that the first pass left on the table. The reaction says the vortex ring disappears after only a couple hundred meters, call it roughly 650 feet. Then Folse says the ring will lose coherence before it makes it one-tenth of the way up. Take that at face value and the clouds have to be at least ten times that distance, so we're talking several thousand feet at minimum. That's my arithmetic on his statement, not a figure he gave, but it shows why "scale problem" is the right diagnosis.

Also worth noting is the format. This is a reaction video, so you're watching an engineer evaluate another builder's home-built project, with the builder's own caveats ("I just welded it up") and the single captured frame of the vortex serving as the only evidence the thing produces a ring at all.

The Ferengi would appreciate the economics. Rule of Acquisition #45: "Profit has limits. Loss has none." The cannon's seller profits a bounded amount per unit, while the farmer's loss from hail has no ceiling, and a device that can't reach the cloud guarantees the farmer pays both ways. That's a bad trade, and Folse's line about the placebo making everyone "sleep better" is the only return on investment anyone actually gets.

Each one has a mechanism that is real but bounded: a vortex ring that fades, a vacuum energy integral that needs a cutoff, an inspiral whose timescale hinges on an unmeasured gas-drag phase, a set of constants whose relationships are unknown. The honest answer in three out of four cases is "we don't know yet," and the fourth is an engineer pointing at a cannon and saying "nope."
