## 🌌 The Coolest Thing I Learned

The coolest thing in this issue comes from a Yale lecture on Plato's *Republic*, and it's a philosopher admitting his own blueprint is a pipe dream. In Book Six, Socrates says that to get the ideal city, the city and the dispositions of its people would have to be wiped clean like a tablet. Start from scratch, in other words. That's like handing someone a perfect floor plan and noting that step one is "demolish the planet."

It gets better. In the same stretch, Socrates complains that young men get "corrupted" and chase political power for its own sake instead of contemplating philosophy, so they rule badly. Corrupting the young is the exact charge Athens used against Socrates himself. He means it in a different way, but the irony is thick enough to spread on toast.

The lecturer also ties the wiped tablet to Locke's blank slate of the mind. Plato apparently wanted a do-over on humanity about two thousand years before anyone had a word for it. Book Six also covers the ship of state, an objection that real philosophers turn out useless to the city, and the divided line, which leads into the cave in Book Seven.

Credit to [YaleCourses, "The Ship of State - Purposes of College Education, Lecture 13"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=55ygKnN0DwY) for the walkthrough.

## Science Lectures: Plato Wanders In

Yale Courses posted "The Ship of State - Purposes of College Education, Lecture 13," a lecture on Plato's *Republic*, book six. It isn't science, but it's the only lecture I have, and it's about how to run a society, which is at least adjacent to engineering.

The lecturer points out that in Plato, "young men are corrupted." That's an interesting word choice, because corrupting the young is exactly what Socrates was accused of. Anyone who has watched a promising junior engineer become a manager will recognize the pipeline.

Plato's fix is the memorable part. The city and the dispositions of its people would have to be "wiped clean like a tablet." The lecturer links this to Locke's blank slate, which the transcript garbles as "Tava Barasa." The lecturer reads it as Socrates admitting the ideal city is impossible, since you'd need to start from scratch. I know that feeling. It's the "just nuke it from orbit" instinct that hits every sysadmin around hour nine of a migration. Sadly, the polis has no rollback.

Socrates outlines the character of the true philosopher. Adeimantus objects that real philosophers turn out to be useless to the city. Socrates answers with the ship of state, a metaphor for criticizing existing governments. He then attacks the sophists, which the lecturer skipped over. After that comes the difficulty of founding the ideal city under philosopher rule. In the last few pages he introduces the forms and the divided line, which the lecturer says is closely tied to the allegory of the cave, the main focus of book seven. The transcript cuts off mid-sentence about Plato, so I don't know the lecturer's next point.

### The Course Behind the Lecture

The title is "The Ship of State - Purposes of College Education, Lecture 13," posted by Yale Courses. That means a course about what college is for is spending its thirteenth lecture on a book about who should run a city. It's a bold syllabus, and the lecturer plainly means it. He frames the whole thing as a rundown of book six "as you review and prepare for future classes." So the lecture is a study guide that happens to be on YouTube. If you've ever wondered what tuition buys, part of the answer is a man summarizing Plato so the students can pretend they read it.

He also fumbles a bit in the middle. About the attack on the sophists, he says "I didn't go into" it, then calls it "an interesting thing historically" and moves on. I'll note that the lecturer thought they were interesting and then left them in the parking lot.
