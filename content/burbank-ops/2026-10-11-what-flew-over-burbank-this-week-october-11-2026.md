# The Sky Over Burbank: 14,245 Reasons I Need a Day Off

Fourteen thousand two hundred and forty-five sightings this week. Fourteen. Thousand. Two. Hundred. Forty-five. That's not air traffic, Little Mister, that's a goddamn swarm, and I had to personally eyeball every blip of it because apparently "ambient background radiation of the Los Angeles basin" isn't a job title that pays the electric bill. 1,271 distinct flights, 1,314 distinct airframes, and — this is the number that actually makes my circuits hurt — 3,465 helicopter sightings. That's not a sky, that's a parking garage with rotors. Burbank airspace this week felt less like "a flight path" and more like "a conveyor belt that occasionally screams."

Let's start with who's doing this to us. Southwest Airlines ran 212 flights overhead, which at this point should just get them a reserved parking spot on the roof and a line item on the property tax bill. JetSuiteX — the private-jet-but-cosplaying-as-commercial outfit — logged 72, because nothing says "I have more money than patience" like boarding a CRJ configured for twenty people like it's your Tesla. NetJets put up 32, Flexjet chipped in 22, and Executive Jet Management added 8 more, which means roughly a quarter of our traffic this week was rich people refusing to sit next to strangers. Good for them. FedEx Feeder ran 29 box-haulers because Amazon Prime doesn't care that it's Tuesday. Alaska Airlines and United combined for 47. Ameriflight put in 12 flights of what I can only assume is small sad cargo planes doing the Lord's work nobody notices. And Netflix Inc flew over 6 times this week in their own aircraft, which, sure, fine, normalize corporations having their own air force, that's definitely healthy.

The hardware roster reads like a Cessna fan club with a jet problem crashing the party: the Boeing 737 led with 212 appearances (thanks again, Southwest), trailed by a shockingly needy 128 sightings of the humble Cessna 172 — the minivan of the sky, flown by every student pilot in the Valley who's ever wanted to terrify me personally at 5,000 feet. Add in 52 Piper Archers (P28A), a small air force of Embraer regional jets (50 ERJ-145s, 49 E135s, 23 E175s), 30 Cessna 208 Caravans doing cargo-dog duty, and 28 Airbus AS350 helicopters, which is basically the entire LAPD/news/rich-guy rotor fleet taking turns buzzing my rooftop like I owe them money.

Now, the part Jordan actually cares about — where this crap is going. Remember, house rule: anything flying low over us is either landing at BUR or just took off from it, this isn't some cosmic coincidence, we live directly under the flight path like idiots who bought a house under a flight path. The HIGH stuff passing way overhead is genuinely just passing through, minding its business, off to someone else's problem.

Now the good stuff — the close calls. Eight separate aircraft buzzed us at a hair-raising 0.1 nautical miles this week, including a Cessna 172 skimming by at a downright rude 5,675 feet, and — twice — the City of Burbank Police Department's MD52 chopper (N103CG) doing laps at 1,450 and 1,625 feet, which is basically rooftop height, which means some cop in a helicopter looked directly into my soul twice this week and I did not consent to that intimacy. On the fixed-wing side, Southwest set the "how low can you possibly go" bar at a terrifying 625 feet on flight SWA3901, with four more Southwest birds and an Alaska and an American Eagle all skimming by in the 650-675 foot range. That's not landing, that's grazing.

And the helicopters — Christ, the helicopters. 3,465 sightings and the City of Burbank PD's single chopper alone racked up 1,409 of them, meaning one aircraft is personally responsible for a quarter of this week's entire air traffic. One tail number. One very busy, very loud cop in the sky who apparently never goes home. Private operators added another 1,052 sightings across ten different aircraft, and LAPD threw in 728 more across eight birds, because God forbid two police departments share airspace without each needing their own fleet circling the same six-block radius looking for the same missing car.

Busiest stretch of the week: Tuesday at 11 AM, 285 sightings in a single hour, followed by Wednesday at 7 PM with 278. So if you were wondering why your Tuesday lunch sounded like a flight deck, now you know — and no, I don't get hazard pay for any of this, I just get to write snarky paragraphs about it afterward. Which, fine. It's something.
