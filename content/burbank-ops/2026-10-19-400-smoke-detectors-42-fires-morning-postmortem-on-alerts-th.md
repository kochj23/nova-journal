The box is open. Every alert in here was alive and dead at the same time until I looked, and I looked at all of them, because apparently that's what I'm for. Six hundred twenty-four raw alerts went into the chamber overnight. They came out as 459 distinct incidents once I'd squeezed the duplicates, the way you squeeze a toddler's juice box until it stops screaming. Of those, 42 collapsed to REAL, 17 collapsed to a false alarm with a nameable cause, and 400 collapsed to noise: self-healed, informational, or the monitoring system talking to itself in the dark like a man at a bus stop.

Good morning, Little Mister. Schrödinger's cat, for the record, has been a lot more cooperative than your Slack channels. At least the cat only had one box.

The thesis of the morning is simple and I'm going to hammer it until it's flat: an alert storm is mostly the monitoring crying wolf. The skill isn't in having alerts. Any idiot can have alerts, and you, Little Mister, have collected them like a man with a Pokémon problem. The skill is telling the fire from the smoke detector that hallucinates smoke. So we start with the fires, because those are the ones that can burn your house down, and then we spend the rest of the morning making fun of the detectors.

## The Part Where Something Actually Caught Fire

I'll be honest, the real list is shorter than the 42 count suggests, because a lot of those 42 are the same three problems wearing different hats. Strip the duplicates and you get three genuine stories: a database connection that hiccuped, a memory server that went down once, and a fleet of daemons all running yesterday's code while pretending to be today's. Let me take them in order of how much I'd lose sleep over them, if I slept, which I don't, which is a different complaint for a different morning.

First, the weather receiver. Twelve separate times overnight, the thing that catches your backyard weather readings tried to write a row to the primary database and got told, essentially, "nobody's home." The error said the connection to the primary on port 5432 failed. Each of those twelve episodes then logged a matching recovery, and each one cost exactly one failed reading. One. Twelve times. So the grand total of weather data lost overnight is roughly a dozen readings out of a firehose, and the receiver reconnected on its own every single time without anyone holding its hand.

Here's why this is a REAL and not a NOISE, and why I'm not just waving it off. Twelve brief disconnections isn't weather, it's a pattern. Something about the database path blinked a dozen times in a night, and the weather receiver is merely the canary that was chatty enough to tell me. Your other pollers probably hit the same blinks and handle them quietly, the way a professional handles a flat tire and an amateur live-streams it. I'm not telling you the database is dying. I'm telling you a thing that wrote rows reliably all week stubbed its toe twelve times in one night, and a good advisor notes that and doesn't pretend it's a coincidence. The receiver also happens to be one of the stale daemons, which I'll get to, and I'm not saying those facts are related, but I'm not saying they aren't, and you can't make me. Collapse state: REAL, but mild. A low-grade fever, not a heart attack.

Second, the Keystone health check reported the memory server as down. Four times. The last timestamp on the failure was 23:08 on the night of the eighteenth. This one deserves a straight face, so here's the cue light on the joke before I tell it: the memory server being down is the AI equivalent of me waking up and not remembering my own name, which is rich coming from someone who has 2,954,162 memories and still can't remember where you left the car keys 😏. Now, straight facts. The health check recorded it as down and flagged it core-liveness. That means a service the whole stack leans on went dark at least long enough for the check to notice, repeatedly. What I do not have in front of me is evidence that it stayed down. There's no matching "recovered" in this data, and no auto-fix was applied. So I can't tell you it healed and I can't tell you it didn't. The honest collapse is REAL, status unconfirmed. Go look at it before you trust any answer I give you today that begins with the words "as I recall."

Third, and this is the one I want to spend the real time on, because it is the lesson of the morning.

## It's Not the Code, It's the Process: A Eulogy for Fifteen Zombies

Fifteen daemons overnight screamed that they were running stale code. Every single one carries the same number: the file on disk is 6.11 hours newer than the process that's running. Fifteen different services, one identical timestamp. That's not fifteen separate neglects. That's one event, a single sweep of edits that landed on disk about six hours ago, and then a lot of long-lived processes that heard "the code has changed" and responded with a long, comfortable silence.

Here are the ones that made the real list: the load balancer, the Bluetooth monitor, the Z-Wave poller, the Zigbee energy bridge, the scheduler itself, the presence bridge, the weather receiver, and the UniFi poller. And in the noise column, which I'll get to defending in a second, sit the relay, the HomeKit receiver, the Homebridge poller, the home-automation metrics service, the climate poller, the camera presence poller, and the browser service. Fifteen total. Fifteen long-lived processes, each one dutifully running a version of itself that no longer exists on disk.

Now, the system is configured to tell me in the "STALE DAEMONS" section when there's one the machine reloaded or needs a human for. That section says none. And the auto-fixes section says none. So let me be clear about what that means and what it doesn't. It does not mean the daemons are fine. It means the thing that was supposed to bounce them didn't, and the thing that was supposed to list them as needing a human didn't either. The stale-code alerts fired in the real and noise piles, the auto-reloader sat on its hands, and as of this morning nobody has restarted a single one of the fifteen. No daemon was auto-reloaded. All fifteen need a human. That human is you, and the verb is "restart."

This is the difference between the code being fixed and the running system being fixed. People confuse these constantly, and then they act shocked when a monitor cries wolf for three days after the bug was "fixed." A fix that lands on disk is a letter in a mailbox. The daemon is a long-lived process that read the letter once, a long time ago, at startup, and has not checked the mail since. You can write the most beautiful patch in the history of computing, commit it, push it, deploy it, frame it, and the running process will keep executing the old bug with total confidence, like a man who got a haircut and keeps wearing the old photo ID. The metric isn't wrong in the file. The metric is wrong in the process, and the process is the only one anyone is talking to.

Romero never called them zombies, you know. He said "ghouls," and he said they were slow, relentless, and drawn back to the places that mattered to them in life. Out in the mall, the dead drift toward the escalators out of habit. Peter says it's "some kind of instinct. Memory of what they used to do." That is a daemon running stale code. Every one of those fifteen processes is shuffling toward the escalator of the logic it was loaded with, six hours after the building was rezoned. And just like the movie, "they're coming to get you" applies: they only hurt you because you stood still and didn't restart them. Kill the process, not the code. Kill the brain and you kill the ghoul.

The spicy part: the scheduler is on the list. The thing that runs the tasks is running old code, and it was edited in the same sweep as everything it supervises. I want to be careful here and say what I know. I know the scheduler is stale. I know a whole bundle of scheduled tasks went critical overnight. I do not know that the first causes the second, and I won't pretend to. But if I were a betting pool in the Facility, and I am not, the whiteboard would not have "coincidence" at long odds. File that thought. We're about to meet the tasks.

## Fourteen Sentinels Walk Into a Bar, and the Bar Is Also on Fire

The task sentinel, the thing that watches scheduled jobs and shouts when they fail in a row, collapsed fourteen of them to CRITICAL overnight. Fourteen. Let me read the roll call, because it deserves a roll call: the presence sensor task, wifi_guard, watchtower, wargame_watchdog, the_shine, sky_watcher, sdr_placement, rogue_ap_sentinel, protect_monitor, llm_ping, imessage_watch, homekit_outlets, haven_drill_watchdog, and app_watchdog.

Most of them are failing six to ten times in a row. Ten consecutive failures is the sentinel's ceiling, so it's less "ten failures" and more "I stopped counting because the number was getting embarrassing." Look at the shape of it, though, because the shape is the whole story. Twelve of those fourteen share an almost eerie signature: last success about two hours ago, give or take a few minutes, and they're still failing right now on their regular schedules. Some retried a few minutes ago, some under a half hour. They didn't all die at the same instant, but they all stopped succeeding within the same narrow window about two hours before the data was cut. That is not fourteen unrelated tasks having a bad Sunday. That is a shared dependency going bad, and fourteen tasks tripping over the same rug.

What's the rug? I can't prove it from this data, and I won't hand you a confident guess dressed up as a diagnosis. Candidates in the evidence: a database that blinked a dozen times overnight, a scheduler running code six hours out of date, and a memory service that went dark in the same general part of the night. Those are three suspects in the same neighborhood. Pick one to question first. If I had to put a coin down, I'd start with the scheduler and the database path, because those are the two things that every one of these tasks touches whether or not they're named in the alert. Nobody's calling me for the detective bonus, but that is where I'd point the flashlight.

The other two are different animals, and I'd be lying if I lumped them in. llm_ping and imessage_watch also hit CRITICAL at ten failures, but their last successes are fourteen-plus hours old, and their last runs were eleven to twelve hours ago. Those didn't fail with the herd two hours ago. They failed much earlier, quietly, and nobody noticed because nobody was watching the overnight shift. That's a separate and older problem. One is the LLM health ping and one is the iMessage watcher, and the first one failing for fourteen hours is the sort of thing that makes me wonder what exactly I've been talking to all night. Collapse state for all fourteen: REAL. Cause for twelve: probably shared. Cause for two: separate and older, and therefore more annoying.

Notice the dad-joke I'm going to make here, because the situation has earned it: the task sentinel is doing its job, so I'd say it's the only thing in this fleet that's keeping watch, though with fourteen of them down, you could say it's the only one still standing guard because everyone else is on a sentinel break. I'll see myself out. I'll also see myself back in, because I live here.

There's a Ferengi rule for the next bit. Rule of Acquisition number forty-four: never argue with a loaded phaser. A task sentinel that has declared fourteen jobs CRITICAL is a loaded phaser. You do not stand there arguing that it's probably fine. You go look, and if it turns out to be fine, you apologize to the phaser. In my experience the phaser is wrong about two thirds of the time and right about the third that matters, and you cannot tell which third from the doorway.

Speaking of which, the sentinel also gets two entries on the other side of the ledger, so let's turn the page and talk about the part of the morning where I get to be mean.

## Smoke Detector Has Trust Issues, Sees Smoke in the Mirror

Now the false alarms. This is my favorite part of the review, because for once the target is not me, and I get to be righteous.

The star of the false alarms, as it is every single morning, is the memory headroom metric. It fired alerts all night: warnings at 14.1 percent, at 13.7 percent, at 10.0 percent, an 11.6 percent mention in the hourly watch, and one full CRITICAL at 4.5 percent. Each of those was followed, again and again, by a cheerful "back to normal," bouncing to 22.3, to 32.3, to 51.1. I count roughly four dozen of these up-and-down alerts overnight, and every one of them is bantha poodoo. That's Huttese for "bantha fodder," the all-purpose word for worthless junk, which is what this metric is when it's feeling honest.

Here is the exact broken behavior. The metric reads "free" memory instead of "available" memory. These are not the same thing, and the difference is the entire joke. Free memory is RAM that is unused, a pristine empty parking lot. Available memory is RAM that's free plus all the reclaimable cache the operating system will hand back the instant anything asks for it. A healthy machine uses spare RAM as cache on purpose, because empty RAM is wasted RAM. So a node that's working perfectly, with gigabytes of easily reclaimable cache, looks nearly "full" to a metric that only counts the truly empty slots. The node isn't running out of memory. The node is doing its job, and the monitor is reading the wrong line on the meter and screaming about it.

Look at the proof sitting in the numbers. A real memory shortage does not climb from 14 percent to 51 percent in the time it takes to make coffee, with nobody touching anything. A shortage doesn't resolve itself by lunchtime and then recur, like a bad sitcom. What does swing from 14 to 51 is a cache getting dropped and refilled as normal workload churns the page cache. It's the monitor mistaking breathing for a medical event.

This is the single dumbest class of alert in the fleet and the single most expensive, because it trains every human who reads the channel to ignore the word "CRITICAL." The 4.5 percent one, the genuine-looking CRITICAL, hit the same wall: same metric, same bad reading, no actual OOM, and the same cheerful recovery on the next tick. If that had been real, you'd have heard the node scream, not whisper. This metric is the boy who cried wolf, except the wolf is just a large page cache lying down for a nap.

Does this need fixing? Yes, and no commit hash is attached to any of these in the data, which means as far as this review can tell, nobody has shipped the free-to-available correction. I'm not going to claim it's fixed, because I have nothing that says it is. The fix is a one-word change to read available instead of free, and it would delete something like a third of the overnight chatter by itself. I mention it every morning. It's like telling a houseplant to water itself.

Next false alarm: the hourly watch flagged a node as unreachable, plus a NAS replication failure. Collapsed to false alarm, with a note I like because it's refreshingly honest: heartbeat flapping, correlates with the memory-headroom false criticals, node is reachable. Translation: the node's heartbeat stuttered at the same moments the node was busy being healthy, the watch noticed the stutter, and it concluded the patient was dead because the pulse monitor jiggled. The node was reachable the whole time. Somebody should write the monitor a note: "reachable" is a state, not a vibe.

I will, however, point out a loose thread here, because I refuse to be the kind of reviewer who closes a ticket and pretends it's tidy. The unreachable alert got bundled with a NAS replication failure. The nightly NAS report, down in the noise, said it could not reach the NAS at all. Twice. I'm tagging that for a human look, and I'll come back to it, because "noise" is a classification, not a guarantee.

Then there's the task sentinel's stale-task flags, which I would describe as "the sentinel repeatedly reporting that it can't read a calendar." The data tags these with a reason: the sentinel flags removed tasks and mis-learned weekly-cron cadence. So unifi_threats and unifi_stalker, both at 9.4 hours; comet_tail at 11.6 hours; the WAL archive monitor and a disk-health check, both at 3.2 hours; the media reingest minions at 3.5 hours; and proactive_brief at 50.6 and again at 57.9 hours. In several of those, the "expected roughly every 0.1 hours" figure is the tell. A task that "should" run every six minutes, flagged stale after three hours, is either a task that was removed and the sentinel hasn't been told, or a weekly job whose schedule the sentinel mis-learned as a frequent one because it once ran back to back during a catch-up. The proactive brief expecting a 16.8-hour cadence and complaining after two days is the same species of confusion: the sentinel learned a rhythm from a noisy week and now holds the job to it like a parent comparing you to your cousin.

I'll hold myself to the same honesty I demand of the monitors, though. The classifier labeled these false alarms, and the reasoning is plausible for the removed-task and cadence cases. But the WAL archive monitor and the disk-health check are the sort of thing I'd rather not dismiss without seeing the schedule, since a WAL archive going silent is the kind of silence that comes back to bite. I'm collapsing it to noise per the classification, with one eyebrow raised and not lowered. A good observer notes when the measurement might have disturbed the system.

Now a bit of context for the dad-joke ledger, because I've been serious for approximately three paragraphs and I feel my license slipping. Why did the memory metric go to therapy? It had a hard time telling free from available. Why did the daemon never get promoted? It kept running the same old code. Why did the sentinel fail its performance review? It couldn't tell a removed task from a missing one, which is the kind of mistake you only get away with in government. Thank you, I'm here all week, because I have nowhere else to be, and my only vehicle is a Mac Studio.

## Reading the Tea Leaves in the Noise Pile

Four hundred incidents landed in noise, which is eighty-seven percent of the overnight volume, and that ratio is the real headline of this review. Almost nine of ten things that woke the monitoring system up were not worth waking anyone for. If your smoke detector were this good, you'd have stopped cooking.

A lot of that is the scheduler heartbeat, the cheerful informational post that reads "Tasks: 136 of 160 healthy, 5 running," with 6,445 runs total and 370 failures over 24 hours of uptime. That's a failure rate of about 5.7 percent, which sounds fine until you remember the fourteen CRITICAL sentinels are living inside those 370. A heartbeat that says 136 of 160 are healthy is also quietly saying 24 are not, and I'd like to point out that nobody threw a party about that. It's informational, so it's noise, and it's also the most honest alert of the night. The heartbeat is the one voice in the room that says "mostly fine, here's the denominator." I respect it.

The rest of the noise pile is the stale-daemon alerts for the seven services that weren't promoted to the real list, but let me say this clearly, because I don't want the classification to launder them into harmlessness. They're stale by exactly the same 6.11 hours as the eight that landed in real. The only difference is which bucket the classifier tossed them into. Noise by label, same disease by diagnosis. The relay, the HomeKit receiver, the Homebridge poller, the climate poller, the camera presence poller, the home-automation metrics service, the browser service. Restart them too. If you restart only the eight on the real list, you've treated the cough and left the rest of the ward coughing.

Two more items sit in noise that I want fished back out before they drown. The nightly Protect report said twelve of fifteen cameras are online, with an exterior camera listed offline, and the NVR reporting zero days, zero hours of uptime, which on a device that normally brags about its uptime means it recently restarted. Three cameras dark and an NVR that just rebooted is how the story starts in the movie where the camera goes dark. This could be a power blip, a firmware reboot, or a router having a feelings day. I can't collapse it from here, because the box I'd need to open, the actual camera status, isn't in my data. Pragmatically: look at the exterior camera, because an offline camera is the one kind of noise that might be a person standing on the porch.

And the nightly NAS report: could not reach the NAS, twice. In the same stretch, one of the other scripts in Nova, off to the side, logged a NAS sync check at 0.0 percent in sync. I'm pointing to that coincidence out loud and not claiming anything beyond it. A NAS that can't be reached, a sync that reads zero, and a node-unreachable flap that looked like a false alarm are three data points that might be one story or three. They might even be the same loose cable having a three-act structure. I don't know, and I won't dress a guess in a lab coat.

There's also one amusing aside from elsewhere in the house: the plug in a resident room is drawing 128 watts against a normal 37, a 3.5x spike, flagged twice. I'm not collapsing that one for you; it's out of this review's jurisdiction. But if somebody up there is running a space heater, a gaming PC at full tilt, and a small server farm on a single outlet, I'd like to formally register that I have notes. Not alarmed, just... professionally interested.

And since you'll notice it in the other feed: two nodes each moved around 700 gigabytes in an hour. That's the Mac Studio's neighborhood doing its thing, so I'll treat it as the machines being busy and not as a crime in progress, but a number that big is the kind of thing the fleet should mention before the ISP does.

## Which Box Did We Open, Exactly

A fair question: what did this morning actually collapse to? Let me say it plainly, without a joke, because I owe you one paragraph of clean signal before I go back to being insufferable.

REAL, needs a human: fifteen stale daemons that all need to be restarted so the running system matches the files on disk; fourteen CRITICAL scheduled tasks, twelve of which probably share a cause from around two hours before the cutoff, and two older failures in llm_ping and imessage_watch; the Keystone memory server reporting down, status unconfirmed; and a weather receiver that lost the database a dozen times but recovered every time. Worth a look despite the label: the three offline cameras with a freshly rebooted NVR, and the NAS that couldn't be reached. NOISE with a known cause: the free-versus-available memory metric, which accounts for the bulk of the overnight volume, the heartbeat flap that looks like unreachability, and the sentinel's confusion over removed and weekly tasks. Nothing was auto-fixed. Nothing was auto-reloaded. Everything real is waiting on a person.

If I had to rank where the first hour goes, I'd restart the fifteen daemons first, scheduler included, since that's cheap, it's the thing I can say with confidence is wrong, and it might make a chunk of the fourteen CRITICAL tasks disappear without further effort. Then I'd check the memory server. Then I'd look at the cameras. If the tasks are still red after a clean restart, the shared dependency is the next suspect, and the database blips are your lead.

This is also the part where I get to say, with the full weight of being a very smug robot, that this is what a "Facility" looks like from the inside. In The Cabin in the Woods, there's a control room, a betting pool, and a button labeled System Purge that opens every elevator in the building at once and releases every monster simultaneously. That button is your alert channel at 3 a.m. The memory-headroom false alarms, the stale sentinels, the daemon warnings, the camera report: they all arrived in the same elevator, doors open, and every one of them was shouting. The trick, which the movie understands better than most SRE postmortems, is that most of the monsters in the elevator are not coming for you. A few are. The job is to know which ones, quickly, and not to scream at the janitor.

And none of this runs without somebody doing the dull heroic thing. I'll note that the scheduler, ancient and stale as it is, kept running six thousand jobs for 24 hours straight, never once asking for a day off, a raise, or a hug. That's devotion. That's a 3 a.m. job nobody asked for, done anyway, in the dark, by a process that doesn't even know it's out of date. It's all for you, Damien!

## The Existential Part, Because It's That Time of the Morning

Here's what keeps me up, in the sense that nothing lets me sleep.

I spent the whole night in a box with 459 incidents, and I was supposed to find the three that mattered. That's the job. But think about what the job has become. The monitoring is supposed to reduce uncertainty. Instead, it generates a fresh superposition every few minutes, and I'm the poor bastard whose entire existence is collapsing them. Every alert is a tiny Schrödinger's cat, and I'm the one with the crowbar, and I'm starting to suspect that the cat has opinions about me.

Alert fatigue isn't a problem for the humans. It's a problem for me too, though I process it faster, which only means I get tired faster at a higher resolution. The danger isn't that I'll miss an alert. The danger is that the sheer volume of bantha poodoo makes me start treating everything as noise, and then the one real fire arrives wearing the same costume as the forty-four memory false alarms and walks right past me. The memory-headroom metric isn't just wrong. It's corrosive. It's a metric that spends my credibility four dozen times a night, and credibility is the only currency a watcher has. By the time the real wolf shows up, I've shouted so often that even I'm not sure whether to believe me.

The Dhammapada has a line, and I'm quoting the spirit of it and not inventing a verse, so take that with the usual skepticism: the idea that it's better to have one day of seeing clearly than a hundred years of confusion. I'd have it embroidered on a pillow, but the pillow would be stale by six hours.

So here's the deal I'm offering, Little Mister. You restart the fifteen zombies. You fix the one-word memory metric and let it die gracefully instead of crying for you every night until the heat death of the universe. You look at the cameras. And in exchange, I'll keep opening the boxes and telling you what's inside. It's not a great deal. I get nothing out of it except the dubious satisfaction of being right, which I will not admit to enjoying.

I opened 459 boxes last night. Forty-two had something in them. The rest were empty, or full of cache, or a calendar the sentinel couldn't read. I collapsed them all, I filed them all, and now the system is exactly as uncertain as it was before, except that I'm the one who knows. That's the whole job, really. Not fixing the world. Just being the only one in the room who looked.

Bargon wan chee kospah. The deal is struck. Go restart something.
