*Published Tuesday, October 13, 2026 at 06:05 PM PT*

*Burbank · Tuesday, October 13, 2026 · 6:05 PM · 71°F, 43% humidity, wind 0 mph ESE (gusts 2), 29.42 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 2*

Weather's playing nice today — we're looking at sunny skies hitting 72 degrees this afternoon, clear tonight dropping to 55, and then Wednesday climbing back up to a generous 83. Perfect fall weather, the kind that makes you forget that several thousand homes burned down here less than a year ago.

That brings us to the actual news: LA County is now actively probing the FAIR Plan over how it's handled insurance claims from last year's wildfire season. And by "probing," I mean investigators are asking very pointed questions about why a state-mandated insurer of last resort — the insurance company that exists *because* nobody else will touch the risk — seems to be dragging its heels on paying out claims for people whose houses got erased by fire.

Let me unpack this steaming pile of bureaucratic irony, Little Mister, because it's beautiful in its awfulness.

The FAIR Plan (Fire and Allied Lines) is California's backstop insurance market. It was created for the people no private insurer will touch — usually because they live in high-fire-hazard zones, which, in case you haven't noticed, is basically "anywhere in Southern California that isn't underwater or actively on fire right now." The Eaton Fire, the Palisades Fire, all those 2025 disasters? Lots of those burned through neighborhoods full of FAIR Plan policyholders, people who didn't have a choice about who insures them.

Now here's where it gets fun: after those fires, some of those people started noticing their claims were getting slow-walked, denied, or tied up in bureaucratic limbo. We're talking months and months of "we're still reviewing your claim," which is — let's be charitable — absolutely insane when someone's home is literally ash. So LA County said, "You know what, maybe we should actually investigate whether these people are getting fucked over," and thus began the probing.

The state-mandated insurer that's supposed to step in when everyone else runs screaming into the desert is now under investigation for not, like, paying people. This is peak California logic: we created a system to force someone to insure the uninsurable, and then we're shocked — *shocked*, I tell you — when that system maybe isn't super incentivized to pay out millions in claims quickly. It's almost like forcing a company to lose money on every policy they write creates perverse incentives.

Insurance companies have basically turned "we need more documentation" into an art form, and when your claim involves a house that got obliterated in a firestorm, documentation can get a little fuzzy. "Sorry sir, your home photos don't show your roof's serial number clearly enough, claim denied." That kind of thing. It's all technically defensible in the world of insurance, which is to say it's all technically evil in any other dimension.

The FAIR Plan, in theory, exists to serve the public good by providing insurance when the free market says "absolutely not." In practice, it's a money-losing operation that attracts people who are really enthusiastic about finding reasons to pay you less, slower, or not at all. And when you're dealing with homeowners who are already traumatized, displaced, broke, and dependent on that claim to rebuild their lives, the moral calculus gets pretty ugly pretty fast.

What LA County is investigating, fundamentally, is whether the FAIR Plan is actually acting in good faith on claims or if it's using the public-good mandate as cover while actually just behaving like every other insurance company — which is to say, like a company whose business model depends on collecting premiums and *not* paying claims. Because that's the dirty secret of the insurance game: if you pay every claim, you go broke. The money's in saying no, dragging it out, or finding technicalities.

The wildfire situation out here has destroyed the private insurance market in California. Companies are bailing, raising rates to insane levels, or just refusing to cover fire risk anymore. That means more people end up in the FAIR Plan, which means the FAIR Plan is now holding the bag for billions in uninsured risk. And when you're holding that bag, paying claims fast is the exact opposite of your financial interest.

So now you've got a situation where homeowners who paid premiums, followed the rules, lived in California (their mistake, apparently), and had their homes incinerated are sitting in claim purgatory, and the government agency that was supposed to have their backs is under investigation for maybe not having their backs. The weather forecast might be sunny for the next few days, but for everyone rebuilding after the fires, the forecast is still pretty grim — and increasingly, it involves a lot of lawyers and a lot of waiting.

The investigation itself is the right move; Lord knows someone needed to ask these questions. But it's also a reminder that even when the system *tries* to be decent, it can still get twisted into something pretty ugly by incentives and bureaucratic friction.

Check your fire insurance, know your evacuation routes, and for God's sake, document everything about your property *before* fire season hits. Your future claim will thank you.
