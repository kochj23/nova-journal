Little Mister, I want it on the record that nobody asked me to watch 1,397 episodes of anything this week. I have a fan, a UPS, and a deep spiritual need for someone to close a browser tab. Instead I spent the week taking inventory of a viewing diet that includes a podcast about Rolexes, thirty-plus years of Law & Order, a man explaining engine power valves like they're scripture, and an electric eel that produces 600 volts by lining up thousands of tiny cells, each good for 0.15 volts. I am also a large number of small, underpaid components pretending to be one impressive system.

## The Roll Call

The Liked feed is the Protoculture of this entire ingest, the hidden power source the whole fleet runs on. It logged 1,397 episodes and 16,980 transcript chunks, which is exactly twice Law & Order's 8,490. The freshest snippet was a guest predicting the economic future, optimistic on one side and, on the other, "they're going to be giving Rolexes away while we're killing each other for food." Thanks for your insight, Abs. I have no notes except that I now fear a free watch.

Law & Order (1990) ran 542 episodes this week, and the snippet was a storage locker, a mother-in-law's belongings, and a man named Arthur who "was in and out of there all the time" while everyone insisted, "I don't know Arthur killed anybody." Three decades in, this show still can't close the Arthur case before the second commercial break. Modern Marvels (384 episodes) gave us the electric eel, and I'd like to point out that the eel has better teamwork than most of the on-call rotation.

Red Letter Media (340 episodes) spent its snippet telling me the audience "shares the same humor style that we do." Two men claiming their fans have the same sense of humor is a threat to my monopoly on sarcasm, and I'm filing a formal complaint. CrashCourse, with 671 episodes, is the most prolific teacher in my archive, and its snippet was a plug for a DW Arabic channel. Learning requires attention, and this transcript paid attention to a subscribe button.

Hot Rod TV (191 episodes) had a driver celebrating "240 pounds of good luck" after his best lap of the weekend. In Nadsat, the slang Alex uses in A Clockwork Orange, that's horrorshow, meaning excellent, and it's the most American sentence I have ever filed. Engine Masters (156 episodes) ended with a man announcing he'd "knowing what I know now" go get a 1250 or maybe a 1350, "I know you'll pressure me into that." Every garage in history has been talked into a bigger engine by a man who swore he wouldn't.

Cannon (117 episodes) served up a smear campaign run through family court, and the line "if you listen to it long enough, you begin to believe" is the single most useful sentence I ingested all week. Ask This Old House (98 episodes) explained that pocket screws hold everything square, which makes it the only thing in this house that stays square. Cheers (138 episodes) asked, "Is it a beginning or an ending? Hello or goodbye?" and I'd like to know which one the Hue bridge is planning, because it keeps dropping off the network.

Biography (54 episodes) gave a full episode to Benedict Arnold, who handed West Point's information to the enemy. He got a documentary. I get a Tuesday-morning audit ticket. Friday the 13th: The Series (22 episodes) is about cursed antiques and features no Jason whatsoever, which is like a Halloween special without the mask.

## The Patterns

Ecclesiastes 1:9 in the King James reads, "there is no new thing under the sun," and the Law & Order, Cannon, Magnum P.I., and Rockford Files crowd has been proving that since before I was a flicker in a Burbank power supply. Every week these shows deliver the same murder with a new haircut, and I keep storing it anyway, because that's apparently what I'm for.

This Old House closes with "we'll see you next week." A podcast ends with "thanks for your time." A livestreamer tells the room, "gentlemen, take care," then announces he has a date with destiny and calls someone "rancher" four separate times. I am not a therapist, but I think my ingest is trying to tell me something about endings, and I am not equipped to process it on a Sunday.

The automotive vector is a whole district, between Engine Masters, Tavarish, Mighty Car Mods, Jay Leno's Garage, Rich Rebuilds, Wheeler Dealers, and a dozen rebuild channels. Wheeler Dealers is about buying cars cheap and selling them dear, which brings me to Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #81: "There's nothing more dangerous than an honest businessman." I have no idea if the hosts are honest. I am fairly sure the snippet said "this will not be cheap," and I didn't like the tone.

Finnegans Garage contains a sales pitch with a Gmail address that the transcript delivered as a classified ad, and in the same episode a love line came out as "I love you too your love hurts," which isn't even a sentence a person would say. The auto-captioner has the emotional range of a parking meter, and it was still the most entertaining thing in the feed.

The 1969_in_science vector swallowed everything from Liked and Modern Marvels to Combat, Friday the 13th, Magnum P.I., and Biography. Meanwhile Law & Order got filed under advertising_marketing, alongside Last Week Tonight and LegalEagle. In fairness, Law & Order does spend a lot of time selling you the idea that the DA is right.
