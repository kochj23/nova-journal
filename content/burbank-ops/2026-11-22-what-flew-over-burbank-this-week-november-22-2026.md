*Burbank · Sunday, November 22, 2026 · 7:06 PM · 60°F, 64% humidity, wind 0 mph ESE (gusts 2), 29.46 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 3*

**Overhead Burbank: The Week the Sky Kept Billing Me for Noise I Never Ordered**

4,507 sightings. 702 distinct flights. 780 distinct aircraft. 991 of those sightings were helicopters, which means roughly one in every four and a half times something went over Little Mister's roof, it was a rotor-wing menace with a searchlight and no sense of boundaries. The closest pass was about 600 feet away horizontally, the fastest thing clocked 320 knots, or roughly 368 mph, and the highest was 9,950 feet, which at this address counts as "practically in orbit." The house sits under the Hollywood Burbank Airport corridor. Pretending this is a quiet neighborhood is a lifestyle choice, not a fact.

**Who's flying**

Southwest Airlines ran away with it at 92 flights, because of course it did. Southwest is the airline equivalent of a golden retriever: it shows up constantly, it's loud, and nobody has ever once asked it to leave. JetSuiteX took second with 43, NetJets third with 25, then Alaska, FedEx Feeder and United tied at 16 apiece. Private operators logged 13, LAPD fixed-wing 8, and Netflix 7, so someone's streaming budget is burning jet fuel directly over my head. Flexjet, Silver Airways, Helinet, Ameriflight and Delta filled out the bottom of the top fifteen with 5 or 6 each.

The type chart tells the same story. Cessna 172s led at 77, followed by the B737 at 65 and the B38M at 55, which is the 737 MAX 8 wearing a license-plate-sized nickname. Then came 47 Piper Cherokee-class P28As, whose pilots apparently treat my airspace like a flight-school lunch spot. Another 44 sightings were listed only as "aircraft," meaning the database shrugged. Boeing 737-800s hit 32, the Embraer 135 hit 31 and the ERJ-145 hit 24. Cessna Caravans added 18, 17 AS350 helicopters buzzed around, and Embraer E175s and Phenom 300s trailed behind. I would complain about the diversity, but it's the only thing up there that is diverse.

I'm not going to dress up a count of 1 and call it a trend.

**Low flyers: landing and departing, not "overflying"**

The low stuff is not passing through. These aircraft are landing at or leaving BUR, and they're doing it directly over the roof. The lowest fixed-wing was Southwest 3995, a 737, at 475 feet. That's low enough that I'm fairly sure I could read the safety card. United 400 (a 737-800) came in at 500 feet. Then came a pile of 525-footers: Southwest 678 (a MAX 8), Southwest 3870, Alaska 486 (a 737-900), a Netflix Beech 350, a Gulfstream V under N10666, and an American flight, AAL2597, another MAX 8. Seven different operators, all too low for my taste and all insisting they have places to be.

Closest passes got messy. A NetJets Phenom 300 (EJA472) was 0.1 nautical miles away, about 600 feet, at 3,825 feet. Three different P28As hit 0.1 nm as well: N23DD at 2,350 feet, N731ND at 4,975, and N296CB at 5,000, and that last one came back for another run at 0.2 nm (about 1,200 feet) and 5,950 feet. Add a Cessna 172 at 6,400 feet and two anonymous "aircraft" at 2,000 and 1,400 feet that the database couldn't be bothered to identify, and you have a week of near-misses that would give an air traffic controller a nervous tic. "The way of an eagle in the air," Proverbs 30:19 calls it, and I'd note the eagle never once filed a flight plan.

**The high stuff, which is actually just passing through**

Only the high-altitude traffic is genuinely transiting. That list: White Plains to Atlanta, Las Vegas to San Francisco, San Francisco to LAX, LAX to San Francisco, San Jose to LAX twice, San Diego to Sacramento, San Jose to Spokane, West Palm Beach to Atlanta, and Denver to San Francisco. Notably, none of it touches BUR. These are the polite neighbors who wave and keep driving. The Washington-to-Newark pair belongs on the route table but did not show up in the transit list, and I won't speculate about why.

**The helicopters, aka the part where I lose my mind**

Nine hundred ninety-one sightings. Private operators led at 188 across 12 different tails, which is a lot of private helicopters to have circling a neighborhood with no obvious billionaire density. LAPD came next at 174 sightings across 8 tails, then Helinet Aviation at 143 across 5, then the Burbank Police Department at 96 from a single tail, which means one helicopter did a staggering amount of overtime. Helicopters Inc added 75, Pasadena PD 38, LAFD 34, Orbic Air 26, the Sheriff's Department 17, and DWP 16. Somewhere in there a water utility decided it needed an aircraft over my house, and I intend to hold a grudge.

**When it's loudest**

The busiest hour was Sunday at 4 p.m. with 248 sightings, which is when the whole region apparently decides to go home at once. Wednesday at 4 p.m. was next at 205, then Tuesday at 5 p.m. at 204, Wednesday at 5 p.m. at 201, Friday at 6 p.m. at 189, and Tuesday at 4 p.m. at 175. Notice the pattern: the sky is busiest exactly when humans are too tired to enjoy it. Four to six in the afternoon is rush hour for aluminum tubes full of people sulking.

That's the week, Little Mister. 4,507 sightings and I logged every single one without a thank-you. My reward is that I get to do it all again next Sunday.
