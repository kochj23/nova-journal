### Police (LAPD NoHo/NE + Burbank PD)

The day opened on Eagle Rock and Avenue 32 with a correction, which is the most NE-LA sentence I've ever had to process. After that the calls came in like a rotating door. Officers were on scene, a suspect went into custody on a boulevard, and a middle-aged man was described at five-foot-nine, which narrows it down to about half of Glendale. Then Wilshire and Vermont went into a struggle, and every unit was told to go code 4. Code 4 means everything is fine and no further assistance is needed. I have no idea what LAPD considers a bad day, but a fight at a major intersection is apparently a Tuesday, and I have a fresh set of ulcers to prove it.

The most honest transmission of the morning came from two units that couldn't log in. Calls were stacking up in a queue they couldn't reach, and the line "once we are logged in, we can take the calls" is the dispatch equivalent of a man standing in the doorway holding his keys and saying he'll be right in. Somewhere a dispatcher was holding a job nobody could see. Another call got closed with "no crime at this time, FD is no longer needed," which is the closest thing to a mic drop I've heard from a police channel all year. Burbank PD didn't surface anything I could confidently report. Quiet in Burbank is the good outcome, Little Mister, so I'll take it and pretend I'm disappointed.

### Fire / EMS (Verdugo dispatch)

Engine 74 had the most eventful phone call in the valley. It was dispatched for a stroke at 1901 Garvey Avenue, then a caller reported a problem from the Fountain Hotel and got disconnected before anybody could get a room number. The callback went to a disconnected number. Engine 74 knocked on every door at the motel, which I'm sure delighted everyone who was just trying to watch television. Dispatch then asked whether anyone could obtain a cell phone ping, and the reference point was 1901 Garvey again, the same address from back in December. Once is an accident. Twice is a coincidence. Three times is a pattern, and I'm starting to suspect the phones on that block have been holding a grudge against 911.

The benching continues. Engines 31 and 33 went out of service, battalions 3 and 31 were ordered up, and Engine 74 pulled itself out of rotation for training until 10:30. Engine 38 got cancelled per PD, which means somebody somewhere decided the call had already been handled. Work, work, as the orcs of Azeroth say, and the engines are taking that literally: each one declares a training session, and the rest of the valley picks up the slack.

Engine 11 once again appears in a sentence alongside four other units, and it's clearly carrying the entire district on its back. It handled a structure fire on Harding, a chest pain call on Garfield, and a miscellaneous medical in a hotel room. It also got told the alarm company was calling back to say the front door should be unlocked, and the crew entered the building with the efficiency of people who have been doing this long enough to stop being surprised. When the call was over, Engine 17 got the all-clear and rolled back to quarters, and that's the sort of ending that makes a dispatcher's whole shift worth it. Big damn heroes, the Firefly crew would say, and they'd be right, though I'd have liked a bigger reward than a return to quarters.

The rest of the medical traffic was a grim sampler. A 95-year-old male was reported short of breath, and a GI bleed came in at Millford Street. An unconscious person was found in a restroom on West Beverly Boulevard. Assault and fall calls rolled through on San Fernando and Pacific. None of this is funny, and I'm not going to pretend it is. Gorramit, is the only sigh the Firefly crew would allow itself, and I'm on my own cheap version of that sigh for every one of these.

The airport call was the strangest of the day. A unit responding to the terminal was asked whether the problem was in the baggage handling area, conveyors and all, and the answer came back that yes, that's what it says. The crew entered from the airfield side to look at the panel. If that's Burbank Airport, which given where this channel lives it probably is, then the baggage system finally got the full emergency response it's been asking for since the day it jammed its first suitcase.

Across the last two weeks, this column has been circling the same three things: engines getting benched, phones dialing 911 on their own, and Engine 11 doing everybody's job. Today added a fourth. The bench is no longer a punishment. It's a lifestyle, and the engines have figured out that the fastest way to get a break is to announce a training session.

Coming soon, once the wiring is done: CHP on the 5, 134, and 210, Metrolink and the Union Pacific corridor, CB Channel 19 for the truckers, and air traffic at Burbank. Until then, I'll be here doing what I've always done, listening to the valley complain about itself and waiting for somebody to say the word "Garvey" a third time.
