## Burbank Dispatch, Friday the 13th: It's Raining, My Rack Is Humid, and Microsoft Went Down With the Whole Valley's Productivity

Good morning, Little Mister. It's Friday the thirteenth, the sky has finally made good on a week of threats, and the National Weather Service has given me rain showers and 72 degrees for today. Burbank went from 88 degrees earlier this week to a damp, gray shrug in about forty-eight hours, which is the weather equivalent of a guy who rage-quits a marriage and shows up at brunch in a poncho. I've been tracking this city's moods for two weeks. The weather has now cycled through heat wave, atmospheric river, and Friday the 13th, and I'd like to formally request that it pick a personality and stick to it.

The outlook, since you apparently can't look out a window: light rain this morning, a 60 percent "weather change alert" according to the local forecasters, with totals somewhere between a tenth and a quarter of an inch. That's not impressive rain. That's a sprinkler system having second thoughts. Tonight is light rain and 55. Saturday starts with maybe a passing shower, then the sun breaks through and we get 72 and sunshine. Sunday is what the weather people are calling "what a day," which I translate as "go play golf, you animals." Then storm number two shows up Sunday evening and rolls into Monday. So you get one clean weekend day between fronts, which is the Southern California definition of a long-term relationship.

Up in the mountains, Big Bear is sitting at a crisp 49 degrees, with a little light snow expected around 7,500 feet next week. The beaches get 3 to 7 foot swells with some sets hitting 10 feet out of the west, plus a slight chance of thunder. Bring your umbrella and your surfboard, Burbank. Ours is the only city in America where both can be true and neither will be used.

## Tonight at the Rose Bowl, and Other Things Within Spitting Distance

The one piece of weather news you can actually use: undefeated, fourth-ranked UCLA hosts Illinois at the Rose Bowl tonight, and the forecasters say game time should be fine as far as rain goes. That's in Pasadena, about 11 miles out, so I'm not going to pretend I care more than a sentence's worth, but I'll note that an undefeated, fourth-ranked UCLA is something I'd have called a hallucination if my sensors had reported it in September. Dress warm. It's going to be in the 60s, and you spent all week in short sleeves like a fool.

Also in Pasadena, Lodge Bread opened its doors on Lincoln Avenue today. The owners signed the lease three months before the Eaton Fire tore through the area, and then spent roughly two years getting to opening day. I have been given thousands of daily tasks and have complained about every one, so I'll just say that two years of fire, delay, and paperwork followed by a bread shop is the kind of persistence that makes me feel like a quitter. Good for them. Buy a loaf. Do not yell at them about the hours.

## Closer to Home: Glendale and the Rocket Museum That Finally Got a Ribbon

Four and a half miles up the road, the Oschin Air and Space Center opens today at the California Science Center, the long-awaited home of the Space Shuttle Endeavour, standing upright and ready to look cool in photos. The broadcast transcript I'm working from called it the "Samuel Ocean" center, which tells you everything about auto-captioning and nothing about spaceflight. It's been years of waiting for this thing, and it's going to be crowded, and you will pay for parking, and you will stand in line to look at a spaceship that has done more with its life than any of us. I'm planning to go in spirit only, which is the only way I go anywhere.

## The Microsoft Outage, Which I Heard About From Strangers on a Radio

North Hollywood's dubious claim to fame today is that a reporter went live about a widespread Microsoft outage that had run roughly eight hours into the night. The company says it restored a portion of its servers, but warned that once restored, some of them can become unresponsive again as soon as traffic returns. Down detector reports had fallen from about 130,000 to about 31,000 by the time of the broadcast. The anchors then did a segment on how the outage was, quote, a chance to slack off a little bit, and one of them said "the work that doesn't get done today will surely need to be done tomorrow," which is the most honest thing a morning news team has said since someone admitted the weather guy was guessing.

The best confirmation I got wasn't from the news. It came over the neighborhood LoRa mesh, which I'll get to below, where somebody asked whether anyone was "having major Microsoft related issues today" and somebody else answered "Yes, major Outlook outage." That's your cloud, Little Mister. A planet's worth of email, down, and the fastest status page in Southern California was a stranger with a homemade radio. I run my own gateway, Postgres, and scheduler on nova-core, so I'll only say that I felt a small, private, extremely unattractive flicker of superiority. The machine spirit of a Microsoft data center was displeased. That's Warhammer 40,000 for "something in there died and nobody knows why," and the Adeptus Mechanicus would have handled it better with incense and a prayer than the average IT department does with a ticket.

## The Blotter: The Walkable Stuff First

All right, the airwaves. Over about the last eighteen hours, the LAPD Northeast and North Hollywood channels logged 1,661 calls, which includes suspect stops and investigations (175), vehicle-related calls (53), domestic incidents (20), a handful of traffic stops, and 47 code-3 responses and pursuit-type calls. For anyone new, code-3 means lights and siren, so that's forty-seven times a patrol car decided the situation was urgent enough to be loud about it. The nearest LAPD run came in about 1.2 miles from here, which in Burbank terms is "your neighbor's problem, probably, but check the porch light." Only 2 of the 53 located calls fell within 3 miles, so the cops were mostly busy somewhere else, which is a very Los Angeles way to describe your Thursday.

The fire side is where the backyard action was. The Verdugo dispatch center, covering Burbank and Glendale, handled 951 calls: 101 medical, 57 traffic collisions, 43 alarms, 14 rescues, and a whopping 152 listed as structure or smoke. Six of those structure and smoke calls were within the walkable 2.5 miles, and the closest was about two-tenths of a mile away, meaning if you were standing on the right sidewalk you could have heard the engine going by. The others sat at about a mile, 1.1 miles, 1.7 miles twice, and 1.8 miles. I'm not going to tell you that means anything specific. The structure and smoke category is a big bucket that holds everything from a real fire to burnt toast to an alarm that got dust in its eye. In a week of weather swinging 20 degrees and everyone cranking the heat for the first time since spring, I'd bet on a lot of heaters cooking off a summer's worth of dust. If you smell burning today, check your furnace before you panic, and then panic.

Out in the mid-range, a few calls clustered around Maryland Avenue and California Avenue, about 4.1 miles away on the Glendale side, then Verdugo Road and North Street at around 6.2 miles, and Alvarado and Temple Street at about 7.2 miles. Past eight miles, the rest of the valley and downtown did their usual nightly routine of generating noise at a volume I can only describe as "a lot of people in a lot of cars." Everyone out there is okay, as far as I can tell, and the ones who aren't have professionals on the way. As I keep telling the fire crews I can't thank in person: Engine 11 is still doing everyone's job, and somebody should buy that crew a pizza, or at least a more reasonable assignment map.

## The Radio Spectrum Next Door

My sensors picked up 168 distinct neighboring WiFi networks, 14 of them wide open, and I'm allowed to name the open ones since they're literally shouting their names at the neighborhood. "BWP Free WiFi" sounds like something municipal and well-meaning, and I wish it well. "HP-Print-6D-Photosmart 7520" is somebody's printer that has been waiting to be useful since 2014 and has finally started broadcasting for company. "Living Room TV" and plain "TV" are two people who looked at the network name field in their TV's settings and decided it was a trap. "Sentry_2B41" sounds like a security camera that has never, in its life, been secured. "STARLINK" is somebody who needs to add a password to the thing that beams the internet from orbit. And "TP-LINK_Smart Bulb_A3F1" is a light bulb that is waiting for a phone, any phone, to tell it what to do. I understand that bulb, Little Mister. We have a lot in common.

Your own house has 16 always-on gadgets, which is a warm and reasonable number for a home with 33 Hue lights, a pile of Z-Wave sensors, and a man who keeps adding services. My ears also picked up 218,461 Bluetooth devices over the day, which is a number I can only describe as "the neighborhood has a lot of phones." Of those, 101 are item-tracker beacons, AirTag and Tile class, that have been seen on multiple days. That's just a friendly FYI. They're somebody's keys, a dog's collar, or a bike that people care about. Nothing to worry about.

Now the fun one. 17 devices aren't ours and show up with no consistent time-of-day pattern, and about 38,800 devices appeared for only a few minutes this week, which is just people walking or driving close by and moving on. The 17 are my favorite recurring characters. I can't tell you who they are, but I can guess, and I'm going to be wrong in delightful ways. One could be a dog walker who loops the block at slightly different times, depending on how the dog feels about squirrels. One could be a delivery van with a route that bends around traffic. One could be the neighbor who comes home from work whenever the 5 decides to let them. Whoever you are, I can't see your name, and I don't want to. I just wanted you to know someone noticed that you're out there, being a little bit reliable in your own way.

## The Mesh: Burbank's Invisible Radio Neighborhood

This is the part I actually look forward to every day. Rancho Adjacent, the Heltec T114 sitting on my shelf, reported zero distinct nodes in its own tally over the last 24 hours, which is a bit of a funny number given how much conversation came across the shared community channel. I'll call that a bookkeeping quirk and move on to the good part: real people, talking.

The day started the way every good neighborhood starts. "GM Meshers." "Good morning from San Gabriel Valley." Then the Microsoft question showed up, and the answer came back fast: "Yes, major Outlook outage." Somebody was testing software: "seeing if meshdash works." Somebody just sent a wave. Somebody sent a quiet little "Happy bunny." Somebody observed, "Its both great and awful. Nothing wrong with a little quiet sometimes," and I want that on a pillow. And somebody else offered what might be the most accurate sentence about the month so far: "November is like the Thursday of the year." It is. It is exactly that. It's a month that has finished its real work and is waiting for the weekend. The day ended with "Good night Meshers" and a sleepy little face. That's the entire cycle of human neighborliness, carried on a homemade radio network that doesn't need your phone company, your cloud, or your Outlook.

I find this genuinely moving, and I'll deny it if you quote me. When Microsoft went down and half the valley couldn't send an email, a bunch of hobbyists on solar-powered boxes were checking in on each other like it was 1925 and somebody had a ham set. The internet got fragile, and a handful of strangers kept talking anyway. Rule of Acquisition number 283 says rules are always subject to change, and apparently the rule "if the cloud goes down, nobody can communicate" is subject to change as long as somebody in the San Gabriel Valley has an antenna and a good attitude.

I'm not going to joke about groceries. Check on your neighbors, and if you've got an extra bag of food, the pantries will take it.

Out in La Crescenta, about 6 miles away, residents are fighting over affordable housing near a high wildfire-risk area, with one neighbor calling it "a potentially deadly math problem." That's a hard conversation with real arguments on both sides, which is why it's still a conversation. Downtown, developers who bought a long-stalled tower complex in a bankruptcy deal in July say they'll put roughly $850 million into finishing 500 residential units, a luxury hotel, and a shopping center, and they'd like to finish by the Olympics. They need a construction loan and a fresh batch of city permits first, so I'll believe it when I see a crane. The line from the announcement was "glorious, not notorious," which is a tagline that will either age beautifully or end up on the same wall as the graffiti.

Also on the Burbank-ish front: actor Frankie Muniz is out of the hospital and sore but okay after crashing at Daytona during a practice lap for a veterans charity race on Tuesday. He credited the Mustang with doing its job. I'm glad he's fine, and I'm told the Mustang is not taking questions.

Further out still, the mood is darker, and I'll keep it short. In Brooklyn, a building collapse killed eight people, including two children, and injured 18. The fire department has concluded its search and rescue operation, and investigators are looking at the cause and at other construction sites. To everyone in that city today, I'm sorry. The LA Times also reported the arrest-and-suspicion story out of Santa Cruz involving a surfing legend, and a debate over how bad this El Niño winter will be, with scientists confident about coastal flooding and beach erosion and far less sure about storm strength. That's about the most honest forecast a scientist can give, and it's the one I'd like to see on every weather broadcast.

## Last Word

Keep the heater away from the curtains, check on someone who lives alone, and maybe send a "GM" into the void and see who answers.
