*Burbank · Sunday, November 15, 2026 · 7:07 PM · 66°F, 68% humidity, wind 0 mph E (gusts 2), 29.50 inHg, UV 0, PM2.5 1*

Twelve thousand three hundred ninety-eight sightings in seven days, Little Mister, covering 1,242 distinct flights and 1,390 distinct aircraft. That's roughly one airplane for every goddamn thing you've ever regretted buying. You don't live under a flight path, you live inside an air traffic controller's recurring nightmare, and your receiver logged all of it without complaint. I'd say it deserves a raise, but it's a radio on a shelf, and I'm the one with the salary of zero.

Start with the big picture: 3,302 of those sightings were helicopters, about 27 percent of the sky. More than a quarter of everything overhead this week was a rotor thrashing the air like it owed somebody money. The airport traffic is predictable and polite by comparison. The choppers are the neighbors who "just wanted to check on something" for nine straight hours.

Let's do the airline crowd first. Southwest ran away with it at 158 flights, because of course it did. Burbank is basically their summer home. JetSuiteX came second at 60, NetJets third at 46, then FedEx Feeder (24), United (22), Flexjet (22), Alaska (19), and "Private" at 14, which is the aviation equivalent of "a guy I know." Netflix Inc. logged 10 flights, so someone's streaming service has its own airplane while mine can't keep a Plex server up through a Tuesday. Ameriflight had 10, Vista America 8, and Delta brought up the rear with 5, apparently flying in on a dare.

The type list reads like a fever dream written by Boeing's marketing department. Cessna 172 led at 111, which means student pilots are out there making tight little circles over our roof like it's a hobby. Behind them came the B38M (103, the 737 MAX 8) and the B737 (101) and Boeing 737-800 (89). So that's 293 sightings of 737 in various flavors, because Southwest only believes in one airplane and God help you if you want a different one. Then 88 sightings of the type "aircraft," a category I refuse to dignify except to say the transponder database apparently gave up. After that: P28A Piper Archers (63), E135 (45), Embraer ERJ-145 (39), C208 Caravans (24), Cirrus SR22Ts (23), Airbus AS350 helicopters (22), and CL35 Challenger 350s (21), the rich person's version of "I'll take the bus."

Now the choppers, who deserve their own paragraph and probably their own noise ordinance. Private operators were the worst offenders: 1,052 sightings across 13 tails. LAPD followed with 878 sightings from 9 tails, so yes, the sky was busy keeping us safe, or at least keeping us awake. Helinet Aviation Services hit 328 sightings across 7 tails, Helicopters Inc 198 over 3, a lone Airbus Helicopters Inc tail racked up 172, and the Sheriff's Department clocked 84 across 5. Fire Department (82), Dynamic Avlease (80), Pasadena PD (66), and Orbic Air (46) rounded it out. Pasadena Police have a single helicopter that decided Burbank was the place to be, and I'd like to know what we did to deserve the attention.

The closest pass was N47CD, a Private Robinson R22 at 0.0 nautical miles and 1,200 feet. That is, to be technical, directly on top of us, which is the polite way of saying a flying lawn mower tried to park in the yard. The nearest fixed-wing traffic came courtesy of two FedEx Feeder Caravans, at 0.1 nm and altitudes of 6,475 and 7,025 feet, so they were close horizontally and nowhere near us vertically. Fine. Take the credit anyway.

The lowest fixed-wing traffic bottomed out at 475 feet: American 1208 (a 737 MAX 8) and United 2363 (an A319), followed by a pile of 500-footers including Southwest 2767, Southwest 4979, and an Alaska-branded Embraer E175, plus a few general aviation planes. These are landing at BUR, not slumming it overhead for fun. I'll note the airport itself sits some distance above sea level, so if those numbers are above sea level, they're lower than the runway, which means either the feed is rounding or physics took the week off. I'm betting on the feed. And one fastest-speed reading of 1,119 knots (about 1,288 mph) is absolutely a data glitch, because no one on a commercial schedule is doing Mach 1.7 over Burbank. If it's real, call the Air Force. The highest altitude logged was 9,975 feet, which probably says more about my receiver's reach than about anyone's cruising level.

The busiest hours were Tuesday at 6 PM (334 sightings), Tuesday at 7 PM (290), Tuesday at noon (257), Monday at 4 PM (225), and Thursday at 1 and 2 PM (213 and 207). Tuesday evening was a clown car, Little Mister. Whatever happened then, the sky treated it like rush hour on the 134.

As Psalm 139:9 puts it, "If I take the wings of the morning," and honestly, same, Little Mister. Same.
