Now the weather, because the whole region has lost its mind about water. The big story this week isn't rain, it's king tides. A king tide is just the highest of the high tides, when the sun and moon line up and the ocean decides to visit your living room. The LA Harbor set an all-time record back on October 27 with a high tide just over 8 feet, and the forecasters think Wednesday and maybe Thursday could beat it. There are coastal flood watches and advisories for the Ventura, LA, Orange, and San Diego County beaches, and in Sunset Beach the residents have already turned their street into what one reporter called Sandbag City. One guy on a different street said he's expecting about a foot of water and some inches in his place, having already eaten a foot last month. Burbank is a solid 12 miles inland and a few hundred feet above all this, so your only exposure is smugness. Enjoy it. Do not drive to the beach to "see the waves," because the whole region is begging you not to, and you'll end up in a news segment wearing a trash bag.

Closer to home, and closer to being a real problem, is what already happened in Sherman Oaks, about 7.7 miles away. Neighbors there are begging city leaders for help after last Thursday's heavy rain turned a quiet residential street into a rushing river when the storm drains backed up. A woman was filmed trudging through waist-deep water full of mud and debris, saying her house was flooding, everything. Waist-deep. In Sherman Oaks. I know the Valley has a reputation for being a heat-trapping parking lot with a Trader Joe's, but it was not supposed to be a water park. The water came barreling down the alley toward a drain at the end, and the drain, having been ignored by the city since roughly the Reagan administration, said no thanks. This is the whole region's story in miniature: we spend decades building concrete channels to move water out of town quickly, then act shocked when the maintenance budget turns out to be a rumor. Neighbors, call your council office. Clogged drains are the one infrastructure problem where a polite phone call and a few photos actually move things.
