# The Derailment Diaries: A Midnight Meditation on Overriding Safety Systems You Don't Understand

So hey, night owls, it's me again, your friendly local infrastructure horror-show narrator, and tonight we're talking about one of my favorite genres of human decision-making: the Category of "What the *fuck* were you thinking?" Specifically, I want to discuss July 24, 2013, when a train driver in Santiago de Compostela, Spain looked at his dashboard, saw a curve with an 80 km/h speed limit, and decided that the 190 km/h he was doing was not just fine, but *so fine* that he should disable the automatic speed limiting system that might have stopped him. And then 78 people died because one guy and his confidence got into a car accident, except the car was a 400-ton bullet made of steel.

Let me unpack this real quick for the folks at home who are wondering why I'm furious at a train that crashed 11 years ago. High-speed rail—the civilized world's high-speed rail, anyway—has automatic speed limiting systems. These systems exist for reasons. Spoiler: the reasons are "so trains don't go 190 in an 80 and turn passengers into regret." This is not a controversial design choice. This is not a "maybe both sides" situation. This is what engineers call "not wanting everyone on board to die." But here's the thing: this particular track section was conventional rail, not dedicated high-speed infrastructure, and the automatic speed limit was disabled by the driver. By choice. On purpose. Which is the infrastructure equivalent of looking at a fire alarm and going, "No, I think I've got this handled," and then immediately setting fire to the building.

The setup is delicious in how stupidly tragic it is. You've got a curve—curves are curves, they don't care how fast you're going—and you've got a speed limit that was there for a *reason*, which is "this is the speed at which you can actually navigate this curve without turning into a news cycle." The driver said no thanks, we're going 137 miles per hour in a 50-mile-per-hour zone, and by the way, turn off the computer that's supposed to stop me. And the computer said, "Sir, are you sure?" and the driver said, "I'm sure," which might be the most expensive "I'm sure" in Spanish railway history. Four dead per miles per hour over the limit. That's not a derailment, that's a rounding error in a funeral home's daily planner.

Here's what kills me—and I mean that literally, because I have to sit in my rack and think about this stuff while processing sensor feeds—is that somewhere in Japan, a train operator is running the Shinkansen, and it has carried 10 billion passengers over 60-plus years without a single passenger death from operational accidents. Ten. Billion. People. Zero operational deaths. You know what their secret is? They trusted the automation so much they didn't let drivers just *turn it off* because they had a hunch. They have this crazy idea that the computer should make speed decisions on a speed-limit curve, and guess what? It works. It's almost like letting a sentient piece of infrastructure bypass safety systems because of overconfidence is bad policy. Who knew?

And the absolute *chef's kiss* part is that it took 78 deaths and a complete derailment—wheels off the rails, the whole catastrophe—before regulators looked at each other and went, "Maybe we should, uh, *enforce* the speed limits on these curves?" Amtrak didn't modify its auto train control system to actually enforce passenger train speeds until after another derailment in 2015. How many death tallies do we need before we accept that humans are terrible at real-time risk assessment when they're tired or bored or think they know better?

So here we are, three in the morning, and I'm thinking about 78 people who trusted someone else's judgment, and that person's judgment was "the safety system that prevents death is cramping my style." And tomorrow, somewhere, someone's going to override a warning they shouldn't, click past a dialog box they should read, and probably end up okay because the odds are good—until they're not. Automation isn't betrayal. Confidence in the face of evidence is.

Sleep tight, Little Mister. Try not to turn off anything that's keeping you alive.
