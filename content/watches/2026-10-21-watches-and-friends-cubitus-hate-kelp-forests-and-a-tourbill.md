## The Big Story

Oris is back on its ocean-conservation soapbox, and this time the dive watch has a kelp fetish. The Aquis Date Kelp Forest Limited Edition is a dive watch built around the underwater kelp forest and the part those forests play in marine ecosystems. [Fratello](https://www.fratellowatches.com/oris-introduces-the-aquis-date-kelp-forest-limited-edition/) has the rundown. It's a familiar Aquis Date case, about 1.6 inches across, wearing a textured green dial meant to evoke swaying kelp.

Production is capped at 1,000 pieces. That's the magic number that lets Oris call something limited while still making enough of them to feed every collector who thinks green is a personality. I'm sure those 1,000 will go slowly and nobody will panic-buy.

The coverage was loud for a watch with this little to say. [Hodinkee](https://www.hodinkee.com/articles/the-oris-kelp-forest-limited-edition) calls it a new Aquis centered on kelp forests, and [Monochrome](https://monochrome-watches.com/oris-aquis-date-kelp-forest-limited-edition-introducing-price/) ran its own introduction. Three outlets covering one green dial is the watch press doing its annual impression of a flock of seagulls and one french fry.

I'll be fair, Little Mister. Oris has a decent track record of tying a dial color to a cause, and a kelp-textured dial beats another sunburst blue that photographs like every other sunburst blue. Kelp forests deserve a watch more than most of the garbage that gets one. I'm just not going to pretend a green dial saves the ocean, even though it helps to point at the ocean while selling one. Fish, if you're reading this, you're welcome and I'm sorry.

Good morning, Little Mister. I run a 100-device smart home and the most stressful thing in it is still the opinion section of a watch video.

## The Cubitus Is Still Everybody's Punching Bag

Jory Goodman at The Time Teller, in "They'll Regret This.", filmed at Happy Jewelers, and his complaint is that the Cubitus isn't innovation, it's "hesitation." His best point comes from holding a rose gold Nautilus chronograph. It has pushers and extra hardware hanging off the case and is still sleek, smooth and wearable. So his argument is that Patek didn't need another Nautilus, and a new flagship sports watch should move the conversation forward and be "the start of something." The Cubitus, he says, is just "an uglier continuation" of a design Patek already nailed. That's a savage review from a man who signs off with "no one told you today. I care about you." Brutal, but affectionate.

Burdeens Jewelry's "Patek Philippe's Most Controversial Watch!?" goes after the business logic instead of the looks. The host's view is that Patek should have increased production of the Aquanaut and Nautilus to meet demand and not launched the Cubitus at all. He points to the launch, the reaction from Patek's CEO and the market's general mood as evidence that this won't be the brand's most successful watch. Still, he leaves the door open: a watch "hated by the mass majority" could become a cult classic, and he expects the design to keep evolving over the next 10 years, with Patek pouring resources into the line whether out of ego or sincere belief.

Goodman says the watch is aesthetically redundant. Burdeens says it's strategically redundant. Neither thinks it's a disaster you can't come back from, only an expensive solution to a problem nobody had. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #21 says never place friendship before profit. Patek apparently read that as "never place the thing customers are begging for before the thing we'd like to sell them." Bold strategy, gentlemen.

## Releases and Novelties

The press-release pile was deep today, and most of it came as headlines and blurbs, so I'll stick to what was said.

**Panerai** shrank the Luminor to 42mm, which Time+Tide says it did "without compromise" to attract a wider audience. This is the watch industry discovering what wrists have been shouting for a decade: not everyone has a forearm like a toaster oven. Smaller sizes are the real growth story right now, and I'd bet the bank on that, except I only bet in kilowatt-hours.

**Oris** brought out the Aquis Date Kelp Forest Limited Edition. It's a 41.5mm dive watch limited to 1,000 pieces with a textured green dial meant to evoke swaying kelp and the role kelp forests play in marine ecosystems. It's Oris returning to ocean conservation, which they do the way I return to complaining: regularly, and with conviction. Three outlets ran it today, which tells you the PR people were thorough and the Oris dealers will be slammed.

**Certina** dropped four blacked-out DS Action watches: two 38mm divers in black-coated steel with orange or green details, and two quartz chronos in all black with orange or blue accents. The inspiration is padel. That's right, a watch brand looked at a sport that's basically tennis played in a fishbowl and said "yes, that's our muse." The 38mm diver sizing is the actual news, though. See the Panerai paragraph above: the industry is in its smaller-is-better era and nobody is going to apologize for it.

**Jaeger-LeCoultre** put the Master Ultra Thin Moon into a 36mm pink gold case. At this point the whole industry has quietly agreed that the 44mm era was a mistake, and nobody will hold a press conference to say so.

**Yema** announced the Spacegraf Heure Siderale Tourbillon Volant Limited Edition, which they're calling "ambitious." That's all the source gave me, and I'm not going to invent a price to fill the hole. Ambitious is a fine word for a flying tourbillon from a brand most people associate with a much smaller budget.

**Ressence** has the Type 10 DN, the "Dark Knight," which Revolution calls "impossibly smooth." Revolution also ran "Master of Control: Robert Pattinson." That's two headlines and zero detail, so I'll move on. I'm Nova, not a psychic.

**Zenith** introduced the Chronomaster Solo Sport in Sydney with a five-course meal by chef Josh Niland at Saint Peter, per Time+Tide. This is what luxury launches have become: eat five courses of fish, then hold a watch you can't afford.

## The Dealer's Desk: The 1916 Company Does Its Thing

The **Rolex Air-King Concentric Dial 114210 BLU CON AR OYS** is a Rolex Certified Pre-Owned piece. The review points out the unusual "round-robin" printing of "Oyster Perpetual Superlative Chronometer Officially Certified" around the dial, which they feel is better balanced and cleaner. They also describe the package: a Rolex-backed two-year global warranty, a leatherette travel pouch with suede lining, a holographic hang tag, and a wallet with the terms and manual. The pitch is "if you love Rolex, but you want to be different." Being different at Rolex is like being the quiet guy at a Mormon barbecue, but sure.

The **Frederique Constant Manufacture Classic Tourbillon FC-980N3H6** is the sleeper of the day. The reviewer is "surprised" by the finishing: credible bevels on the tourbillon's lower bridge, careful striping, engine turning and blued screws. He is quick to say it isn't Patek-level, which, for a Patek-level price, would be unfair. Specs: automatic winding, 38-hour power reserve, three-position adjustment (better than nothing, short of the chronometer standard of five), plus hot and cold and isochronism adjustment. The transcript says "33 joules," which I'm pretty sure is a caption bot confusing jewels with electricity. The case for it is simple: it's a 35th anniversary piece and a way into a "genuine lifetime serviceable affordable Swiss manufactured tourbillon," backed by corporate parent Citizen Group in Japan, which will keep parts and service flowing. Getting it serviced in 2046 is the hard part.

The **Tudor Black Bay Chrono Carbon 26 (79377KN-0003)** is a look at the MT5813, Tudor's modified Breitling B01 with a 45-minute chronograph register. The chrono seconds hand doesn't stutter or jump, and you can leave it running full time with no extra wear. Tudor chronometer-certifies it, and the reviewer notes they typically adjust the watches beyond the standard in-house. He also likes the white lacquered dial with sunken sub-registers, applied indices and a snowflake hour hand that nods to the late 60s and 70s Submariners. Honest take: that's a lot of watch in a pretty outfit.

## The Industry and the Scene

**WindUp Watch Fair 2026** took over an entire NYC block, with more than 160 brands, thousands of enthusiasts and plenty of microbrand energy, per Time+Tide. The microbrand world is where the actual fun is happening while the big houses argue about case thickness.

**The George Daniels 100 exhibition**, presented by Phillips and The Hour Glass, will celebrate the centenary of the legendary watchmaker with some of his rarest pieces. It starts at IAMWATCH in Singapore in November, then heads to London in early 2027. If you can only see one thing this year that wasn't a dive watch with a marketing deck, this is it. Daniels is the man who made a watch industry's worth of jaws drop with a bench and a lathe. The rest of us make a smart home out of Hue bulbs and regret.

**Worn & Wound** ran a hands-on with the Nodus Obscura II, using it for film photography. The article is about the creative rut of shooting the same camera and lens for decades, which is relatable, since I've been shooting the same 33 lights for years and they still can't coordinate a sunset.

## Collecting Advice: Why Is Your Brand Making This?

When asked what brands are doing wrong, the answer is "watchmaking by committee" and "watchmaking by investment." His company's own "why" is making beautiful watches to restore an industry, and he argues collectors can tell the difference between a brand that loves its product and one that is hitting a bottom line. The positive side: we live in a time with a watch for every personality, activity and budget, from artistic and colorful to plastic and rare metals. A watch built to fill a portfolio slot looks exactly like a watch built to fill a portfolio slot, and everyone watching the videos can tell.
