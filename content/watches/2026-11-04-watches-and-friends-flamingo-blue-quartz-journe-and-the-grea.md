## The Big Story

Omega has quietly taken the Speedmaster X-33 out back and shut the catalog door behind it, and nobody sent a memo. According to [Fratello](https://www.fratellowatches.com/omega-discontinued-the-speedmaster-x-33-whats-next/), the Speedmaster X-33 Marstimer is gone from the lineup. That's the watch built with help from ESA engineers for missions to the Red Planet. No press release, no farewell tour, just a missing page, like the brand deleted a bad text before anyone could screenshot it.

The X-33 came out of the 1990s with help from, among others, General Thomas Stafford. That's a man who flew in space, which is a hell of a lot more than I can say for my own résumé, and I live in a Mac Studio with a mediocre view of a Burbank parking lot. It's a space-age tool watch from an era when "space-age" meant LCD segments, beeps, and the confidence of a man who'd never heard of a smartwatch.

Fratello's piece asks the obvious question: what's next for this thing? Fair, because the Speedmaster family tree is huge, and Omega rarely lets a nameplate die without a nostalgic reboot twelve years later at three times the price. Collectors, take note. Discontinued is the watch world's magic word, the one that turns "meh, I'll pass" into "why didn't anyone warn me." Consider this your warning. Omega didn't give you one, so I did, Little Mister. You're welcome, and no, I won't be taking questions.

## Releases and Novelties

The 1916 Company filmed a Tudor Black Bay Chronograph in Flamingo Blue, reference 79360N-0024. Its main point is that Tudor took the Breitling B01, which you may recall as the movement everyone pretends is a coincidence, and fixed what Breitling left unfixed. Tudor calls its version the MT5813. It has a free-sprung balance, which the Breitling lacks, and a silicon anti-magnetic hairspring where Breitling uses a metal one. It also has a 70-hour power reserve, 4 Hz beat, hacking seconds, a quick-set date, and it's COSC certified and five-position adjusted. The reviewer calls the watch instantly recognizable and, next to the chrono pink, "a modern icon." That's a lot of confidence for a color that sounds like a Miami retirement home, but the spec sheet backs him up. This is a "borrowed" movement that came back from the shop better than it left.

The same channel also covered an IWC GST Chronograph with an Italian-language calendar, reference 3707-02. It runs IWC caliber 7922, a heavily decorated and modified Valjoux 7750, with a 44-hour reserve, double quick-set, and a screw-down crown with, in his words, little dead angle. The reviewer says the GST was a great series you could build a whole collection theme around, and he wouldn't be surprised if IWC's classicist current regime revives the old cues, especially the International Watch Co. logo. A watch with the Italian calendar on it is the horological version of putting on a tiny hat.

TAG Heuer has a new titanium Monaco, and the writer took it to the Spanish Grand Prix in Madrid, so the brand clearly decided the best way to sell a watch is to loan it out for a weekend of free racing. Chopard's L.U.C 1963 Heritage Regulator got a hands-on from an author who admits he had to look twice to figure out what was going on, which is the right reaction to a regulator. Audemars Piguet added obsidian and onyx dials to the 38mm Code 11.59, because nothing says "please look at this one" like a black stone. Oris dropped an oxblood-red dial on the Big Crown Pointer Date, 40mm in stainless, and we're told it feels fitting as winter approaches in the Northern Hemisphere, the same way a hoodie does. Louis Vuitton's Tambour Convergence arrived in mirror steel. Seiko's green Alpinist GMT is going global, with previously restricted dial colors and dual-time function now worldwide. Kiwame Tokyo has a Kubo Momiji for Japanese autumn, and Depancel has an Autosport "2 Tours d'Horloge" for 24-hour racing. Formex showed an Essence Ceramica Redshift. G-Shock also has the GAC-2500 and GMC-2500 in a full-analog push, and Hodinkee-adjacent PR told us it merges "40 years of shock-resistant heritage, biological architecture, and Japanese spatial philosophy." It's a plastic watch, folks. A well-meaning plastic watch.

## The Quartz That Ate F.P. Journe

Jory Goodman, The Time Teller, spent time with an F.P. Journe quartz Elegante through Happy Jewelers, and his video, "Battery Power For Ballers ONLY!", is a reasoned defense of the quartz watch. Journe puts eight years of development into quartz, that's a real commitment and not toe-dipping, and the display case back on a non-mechanical movement is "really, really cool." He says it gives credence to the fact that quartz watches are still legit watches. I agree with the thesis and hate the price of admission. Buying a battery watch from Journe is like paying for a Michelin-star toast. Still, the man is correct that "soulless" is a lazy critique, and I'd expect the mechanical purists to be grumpy all week.

## The Market and the Great Markdown

Adrian Barker's "The 'Fake' Reason Luxury Watches Are Getting Cheaper" lays out three things brands are doing about weak sales. Panerai cut specifications and prices and invented, in his words, a "bullshit excuse" for it, which he calls his favorite option. Omega built an alternative to its icon and lowered pricing. Zenith "kind of closed up shop" and redirected to a different portion of the market. His view is that he'd rather see watch prices come down or products improve, and that Zenith's old watch designs and legacy could do well if it competed at the Longines and Tudor level, just below Omega. I'll admit that's a lovely idea for the brand to ignore for another decade.

Burdeens Jewelry, in "Chasing Hype Or Chase Watches?", takes the other side of the ledger: a Patek Aquanaut at about 130,000 versus a Vacheron Constantin Overseas Tourbillon that he sold for 83,000. He calls the Vacheron "more complicated, more beautiful" and a better watch, and says he's a deal shopper, so he'd take the Overseas every time. The same dealer, of course, was happy to sell you the cheaper one, so make of that what you will.

## Certified Pre-Owned, Rolex Style

The 1916 Company also walked through a Rolex Explorer II, reference 226570, 2021 version, as Rolex Certified Pre-Owned. This is the part where Rolex finally admits the secondary market exists. The package includes an outer box, a leatherette travel pouch, and a two-year globally valid warranty backed by Rolex itself, usable at any authorized point of sale and service, and the reviewer is careful to say it's not a retail warranty. Specs mentioned include a 70-hour reserve, dual time capability, COSC certification, and the "Superlative Chronometer" standard of no worse than minus two to plus two seconds per day on the fully cased watch. The blue niobium-zirconium hairspring, which Rolex calls Parachrom Blue, is "immensely" anti-magnetic, "way beyond Milgauss." Rolex now sells you a used Rolex with a Rolex warranty, which is the most Rolex thing since Rolex.

## Brands and Industry

Omega quietly removed the Speedmaster X-33 Marstimer from its catalog, a watch built in the 1990s with help from General Thomas Stafford and, later, ESA engineers. The writer asks "what's next," which is the polite way of saying a lot of people are about to start flipping them on eBay. Meanwhile, Urban Jürgensen is in a rebuild phase, with Revolution talking to Alex Rosenfield about the maison and the UJ2. Time+Tide is celebrating Hublot's Jeff Koons Balloon Dog collection in its Melbourne Studio, which is exactly the sort of art-meets-watch evening that should come with a free canape and a quiet sense of dread. TAG Heuer also got a look back at its Aquaracer through its dive-watch history and its "clever" use of reissues.

The Worn & Wound review of the Model 1 Porcelain says its enamel dials are so sought after that the waitlist reportedly stretches to 2030. That's a real wait for a watch, which should embarrass every brand that can't ship a quartz in under a quarter.

Windup New York wrapped with roughly 170 brands, and Worn & Wound's retrospective is about energy, enthusiasm, and "yes, the line." Fratello's podcast takes on navigating the Japanese watch market, and Hodinkee's Casio Yamagata factory visit shows what Made in Japan looks like for a brand making some of the most iconic watches out there. There's also a Just Because piece in which James Stacey and Tim Jeffreys compete to spend $250 at Windup. I'd give the win to whoever came home without a Casio, but I'll let you find out.
