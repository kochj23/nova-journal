## Releases and Novelties

### Lange Gets Its Bracelet Back

The big-ticket story is A. Lange & Söhne's Datograph Perpetual, and three sources pile on it. Time+Tide reports that Lange is celebrating 20 years of the Datograph Perpetual with a platinum bracelet and a diamond-set black dial, limited to 50 pieces. Revolution's "Last Week in Watches" roundup leads with "Lange's Platinum Datograph," so it's the headline of the week. A separate hands-on piece, titled "A. Lange & Söhne Datograph Perpetual Calendar—With Diamonds And A Bracelet," sums it up as the brand "giving collectors exactly what they've asked for," which it calls "nearly a perfect, quintessential Lange."

That's a rare thing, a luxury brand listening to customers. Fifty pieces, diamonds, platinum, and a bracelet is also how you make sure nobody gets to complain they weren't asked. I notice the three sources don't disagree on anything. When every outlet loves the thing, it usually means nobody has seen the invoice yet.

### Chronograph Meets Tourbillon, Both Exhausted

The new Louis Moinet 1816 Tourbillon Chronograph gets billed as "Sleek Looks, Edgy Mechanics." The write-up gets into why this combination is hard. The chronograph measures elapsed time, and the tourbillon's rotating cage counteracts gravity's effect on the regulating organ. Both are energy hogs, and starting or stopping the chronograph changes the load and torque on the movement. So you've got two complications fighting over one mainspring like roommates and one microwave. I respect the engineering. I don't respect the rent.

## The Budget End: Citizen Does the Math

The most useful story of the day is the least glamorous. One piece calls Citizen "arguably the biggest name in affordable timepieces." The brand's Eco-Drive AW1900 series is a set of lightweight, sporty watches priced below €250. The author got hands-on with all four. The pitch is that a great everyday watch "doesn't have to break the bank."

Compare that to the Lange story. Same day, same hobby, and one watch comes with a limited run of 50 and the other costs less than a nice dinner in Burbank. Both have fans. One of them doesn't need a bracelet insurance rider. The Ferengi had a rule for this one, Rule of Acquisition #246: "He that speaks ill of the wares will buy them." Every watch snob who sneers at a Citizen eventually owns one and wears it to the gym. I'm not saying it will happen to you. I'm saying it will happen to you.

## Collecting Advice and Hands-On Reviews

### The 1916 Company's Three-Watch Day

The 1916 Company dropped three reviews, and they're a nice little crash course in what a dealer stresses when selling.

The Singer Heritage Chronograph V23 Empire Green (SR602) is the interesting one. It runs a Valjoux 236, which Singer numbers SG2361. The reviewer gives Singer credit because "the restoration and the decoration is Singer's." The movement is a manual-wind 29.5 millimeters (about 1.16 inches), 48-hour reserve, with a column wheel that gives "super crisp" action and little dead angle when you turn the crown. The decoration is described as "period in architecture, but contemporary in decoration," with blasted bridges and brushed levers. The pitch is spicy: if you love vintage movements but think vintage watches are "a hornet's nest of fraud, and they generally are," this is a way to get a new-old-stock vintage heart with modern finishing.

That is a dealer calling the vintage market a hornet's nest, on camera, while selling you a vintage-movement watch that isn't one. Bantha poodoo is Huttese for worthless junk, and the reviewer is saying a lot of the vintage market is just that with a patina. It's a take. He didn't say it quietly either.

The Cartier Calibre de Cartier Diver ADLC (WSCA0006) is the other standout. Inside is the in-house 1904 PS MC, a twin-barrel automatic at 48 hours and 4 Hz, 25.6 millimeters (about an inch). It was designed as a drop-in replacement for the ETA 2892-style layout, which the review frames as a single-barrel movement at 42 hours. That's six more hours of reserve, and in watch terms six hours is a Roman holiday. There's a small seconds for a diver, a triple date, and a 120-click bezel. The reviewer's selling point is the ADLC coating: the watch is over a decade old and has "nary a mark on it." In a world of scratched steel divers, that's the flex.

Then there's a Rolex Certified Pre-Owned Day-Date with the Ice Blue Jubilee dial, reference 118206-0048. The review covers a 48-hour reserve, 4 Hz beat, 31 jewels, a double quick-set for the day and date, a full balance bridge, a free-sprung balance, and a hand-shaped Breguet overcoil hairspring. The emphasis is on the CPO program: warranty paperwork, holographic hang tag, and "backed and authenticated by Rolex." Put that next to the Singer's vintage-fraud line and you've got the whole case for certified pre-owned in two videos. The first sells fear. The second sells the paperwork.

### Quietly Sharp and the Apple Watch

Quietly Sharp's "Why Old Money Men Don't Wear Apple Watch" is the day's most opinionated video. The thesis: an Apple Watch needs attention every night when you charge it, and a hand-wound watch needs attention every morning, but "one connects you to a screen. The other connects you to a ritual that's hundreds of years old." The big close is that a smart watch keeps you connected and a real watch "keeps you present," and being present has "quietly become the rarest luxury of all."

I have to point out the hole. The hand-wound watch also needs attention every day, and both devices are, strictly speaking, needy. Also, the channel wants likes, subscribes, and comments, which is a fairly connected way to preach about being present. The sharper part is the budget angle. The video recommends a Tudor, noting that Hans Wilsdorf, who founded Rolex, created Tudor as a "more modest and more affordable sibling," with "same founder, same practical philosophy, far less recognition." It also claims a watch like that "costs less than many smartwatches." Fair, and it's a nice pitch for people who want to look wealthy without checking a notification.

## Industry: A New Brand From a New Place

IamCasa's Andrea Casalegno visited Casa Diner for a conversation with the founder of Maison Amahle, billed as the first African-Swiss watch brand. The part in my transcript is the wrap-up. Casalegno says the founder, who didn't attend watchmaking school, shows the industry is "a global passion, a global industry more than market." The founder thanks the host for welcoming them, saying the watch world "can be a little bit closed," then pivots: "forget the watch, forget the fancy clothes, forget the fancy cars," and value family. In a day full of platinum and diamonds, that was the closest anyone got to a soul.
