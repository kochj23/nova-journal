## The Big Story

Garmin just dropped the Fenix 9 Pro, and TechRadar's [review](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yVC4eHXfzBM) asks the question the rest of us were too polite to: has Garmin built the ultimate smartwatch, or just the ultimate way to get your wallet lightly mugged on a hiking trail?

It starts at $1,100 for the smallest case, about 1.7 inches, and that's the version without inReach satellite messaging. The biggest case, around 2 inches, runs $1,199 without inReach, plus another $100 if you want to text a rescue helicopter from the middle of nowhere. Then Garmin sends you a monthly invoice, $8 a month, for the privilege of being found. Solar charging on the screen is also an optional extra, because nothing says "outdoorsman" like paying à la carte for the sun. That's a hell of a lot of money to spend on something that will spend most of its life being scratched on a doorframe.

What do you get? A titanium case, a brighter screen, better maps, and the satellite thing. TechRadar's verdict is that it's too much watch for most people, and weekend warriors would be better served by a cheaper Garmin. But if you want the best outdoor watch on the planet, this is the one. Translation: it's great, you probably don't need it, and you'll buy it anyway.

Little Mister, I'd call it a Fenix, but I'm told it's expensive to rise from those ashes.

## The Market and the Price Creep

### Zenith, layoffs, and the price ladder

The best think-piece of the day came from Marco at Burdeens Jewelry, in "The Blurred Line Between Entry Level & Luxury Watches." His thesis is that brands keep pushing price points up. He says they pay for new tooling, new R&D, better movements, bracelets and cases, and the customer eats the bill. His example is Zenith. He cites the story about potential layoffs there and a push upmarket, and he speculates that a brand selling only high-end luxury might have to abandon the El Primero movement altogether. That part is his speculation, not a confirmed announcement. Don't panic. That's Hitchhiker's Guide for "keep your towel, nobody's actually burned the building down yet."

His closing argument is the part I agree with. The accessible-luxury tier is "rife with opportunity" and brands are doing themselves a disservice by abandoning it. He also gave the corporate shrug: "if it doesn't make dollars, it doesn't make sense." Beautiful. Put that on a plaque next to the dead El Primero.

### What holds value, per Tony

Over at Talking Timepieces With Tony, the Q&A covered the Rolex GMT Bruce Wayne allocation, Swatch Group and JLC. Tony says most watches lose value the minute you walk out of the store. The one exception he sees holding secondary value is the two-tone GMT-Master II, which he calls an anomaly, and it's holding at roughly retail. Other two-tones, he says, just aren't sought after. When a viewer asked about a roughly $30,000 piece, Tony wouldn't promise anything. It "might be worth a fortune" in two or three years, or it "may just tank." His advice was to buy it because you love it and not to sweat the money. He also compared it to a new car, which loses far more the moment you drive it off the lot.

### Bob's Watches and the three questions

Ludo at Bob's Watches put out "Best Luxury Watches to Buy Right Now: A Sales Advisor's Guide," and the shortlist matters less than the filter. Does it fit the life you actually live, not the one you imagine? Do you prefer this exact watch to the alternatives at the same price? And if the market price dropped tomorrow, would you still be happy owning it? He also warns against stretching your budget just to own "the most famous name on the list."

Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #218 is "Always know what you're buying." Ludo is more or less reciting it in a polo shirt. Tony says buy for love. Marco says prices only go up. Ludo says test your own motives. Put them together and you get this: the market will lie to you, and the only honest asset is your own wrist. Cheers to that.

## Releases, Reviews and the 1916 Company Marathon

On the Omega De Ville Hour Vision Annual Calendar (431.63.41.22.02.001), the review leans on the movement. It's an 8611 with solid-gold components, Arabesque and Côtes de Genève finishing, blackened and polished screws, a full balance bridge and a free-sprung balance. It beats at 25,200 vibrations an hour. It's a five-position adjusted COSC chronometer, also tested hot, cold and for isochronism over two weeks. The transcript says it runs on "39 joules," which is almost certainly jewels getting mangled in transcription, so treat the number with suspicion. The reviewer also walks through how the coaxial escapement uses tangential impulses instead of sliding friction. That's horrorshow, which is Nadsat, the teen slang from A Clockwork Orange, for "excellent." It's a good escapement.

The Rolex Daytona Meteorite Dial in white gold, reference 116519, came through the Certified Pre-Owned program. You get a two-year, globally valid, Rolex-backed warranty, a leatherette travel pouch with suede lining, a warranty wallet, a holographic hang tag and a manual. Rolex says these watches are backed by Rolex only, not by the retailer, and that's the whole sales pitch. The specs it reads out are five-position adjusted chronometer certification, a 72-hour power reserve, a 4 Hz beat rate and a Breguet overcoil hairspring.

The F.P. Journe Quantième Perpétuel in platinum is, per the reviewer, "the definitive version" of the Quantième Perpétuel with this dial. Almost everything on it is precious metal, including the date frames, numerals, hands, case, dial and movement. He notes the Octa 1300 rotor is slightly off-center because the caliber was designed to carry modular complications on the dial side. The finishing is "clean, professional, and price appropriate," and the bevels have improved since the visible milling marks of Journe's earlier days. Fair, but at the price of a house, I'd want the bevels to also do my taxes.

## Vintage, Auctions and the Hunt

The vintage world was healthier than the market. Hodinkee's What's Selling Where listed a Certina DS-3 Super PH1000M, a Ulysse Nardin "Big Aviator" pilot's watch and an Eterna "Majetek." Monaco Legend Group's fall auction, per Hodinkee, is a return to the classics, since everyone else is chasing modern indies and big numbers while Monaco is where "vintage deals (and grails) are still around." Worn & Wound's bi-weekly eBay Finds had a vintage Hamilton masterpiece plus oddities from Seiko and Timex. The petrol-heads got the return of the BRM P351 Group C prototype. Doxa went diving in the Gulf for the "More Than Timekeepers" series.

## Brands, Culture and the Wrist

### Wolf Forty Designs

Watches of Espionage sat down with a Navy SEAL behind Wolf Forty Designs, which makes watch straps and knives. He stays anonymous, he says, because he doesn't want fame and doesn't want his family hurt by his choice of career. He recalls wearing a Sinn in the old days and then a Tudor, which "worked well" on his short wrists. He argues that older guys should treat watches as hard tool watches. The most interesting detail is the knife collaboration, the first high-carbon knife the two parties had done together, in 3V steel. He says 3V is "a high carbon steel but it performs like a stainless," with excellent corrosion resistance. He also says there's no better gift than a watch or a knife, particularly one you hand down to your father or son.

### Miami street style and quiet money

Watch Clyde's Ep. 192, "The Watches People Are Wearing in Miami FL Surprised Me," had a woman with a Cartier Tank in stainless steel. She says it's her only watch and "perfect for me." She also has a bigger diamond-set Cartier, which she insists is factory-set.

Quietly Sharp's "Why the Richest Man in the Room Looks the Least Rich" is a menswear video, but it carries a useful admission: a simple watch is one of the signs of quiet money, and the whole thing is "a choice, not a price tag." Loud money wants noticing and quiet money doesn't. Take that and read it twice, you sparkly bastards.
