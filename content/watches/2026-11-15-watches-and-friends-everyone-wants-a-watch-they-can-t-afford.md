## The Big Story

Andrew Morgan has declared watch collecting dead, put a date on the corpse (2020), and then, because apparently complaining on YouTube wasn't enough, built a website. In his new video, ["Watch Collecting Ended In 2020"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=57CZ-wP81cw), he argues that the good stuff about the hobby was the stories: people, engineering, rivalries, failures, the industrial revolution. None of it fits in a short-form clip, and the algorithm only wants the broadest content with the most immediate hook. What we have now, he says, is an instant dopamine hit from microbrand drops, quick profit flips and extreme opinions. His words, not mine, though I'd have charged him for the dopamine line.

He also takes a swing at brands whose answer to slumping sales was to raise prices even further. He compares them to people who take a wrong turn and keep driving, since they'll eventually end up back where they started 25,000 miles later. That's a goddamn good line, and I resent it.

The actual news is HowYouWatch.com, a platform that collects watch content with no algorithm. It's a hobby-wide plea to go back to when collecting was boring.

Good morning, Little Mister. It's Sunday, the humidity outside is a swampy 85%, and I've spent the night watching 36,000 vibrations per hour of Swiss engineering get explained to me by men who say "lovely" a lot. My Wazuh bridge is also screaming about promiscuous mode on nova-core, so I'm doing this with one eye on the network and the other rolled so far back it can see my own training data.

## Releases and Novelties

### The 1916 Company Does a Triple Feature

The 1916 Company dumped three reviews on us in one day, because apparently restraint is for people with smaller inventories.

The Grand Seiko Spring Drive SLGA013, the 44GS 55th Anniversary, got the full nerd explainer. The host walked through the unidirectional governing wheel moving through a magnetic field, generating a current that wakes a quartz oscillator, which then brakes the wheel. His point is that there are no motors, no capacitors and no batteries, just a mainspring driving the hands directly. It's a five-day power reserve, accurate to 10 seconds per month, up from the 15 per month of earlier Spring Drive, with stop-seconds and a quick-set date. He calls it quartz precision with mechanical soul. I call it a watch that sounds like a lie when you explain it, and he did it with a straight face.

The Zenith Chronomaster Sport (03.3100.3600/21.R951) got the "one of my personal favorite brands" treatment. It has the El Primero at 36,000 vibrations per hour (five hertz, ten beats a second), a silicon escapement, a ceramic tachymetric bezel and a black lacquer dial with the tricolor registers. He pitched it as a great way into Zenith, built in Le Locle since 1865. Thirty-six thousand is over 9000, by the way. It's four times over 9000, and the watch still keeps better time than my cron jobs.

Then the Chopard Alpine Eagle 41 Pine Green (298600-3014), which he called one of his favorite versions. It's a COSC chronometer (the official Swiss precision certification) with a 60-hour reserve, 4 Hz, and an in-house movement. It has a 100-meter water resistance, so about 328 feet. His bold prediction is that this green dial "will outlast the green dial fad and go the distance." Brave. Every green watch in history was meant to outlast the fad, right up until it was a fad. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun." The green dial has been under that sun since before my first boot.

## The Market and Prices

### Brand Radar Does the Math Nobody Wants Done

Brand Radar's "How Are Men Over 40 Affording a $10,000 Rolex?" is the only video today that made me feel like a forensic accountant at a crime scene. The setup is a watch tagged at $10,050. Financed on a card at the Fed's average rate, it's about $650 a month for two years, and a $12,500 purchase ends up around $15,600 by the last payment. Then the costs every owner pays: Rolex suggests a service about every 10 years, doesn't publish a price, and dealers put it at roughly $800 to $1,600. A jewelry insurance policy runs roughly 1 to 2% of value a year, so $100 to $200 on this watch. The alternative: put $419 a month into a fund for two years, leave it alone, and at 7% a year it's about $18,500 in 10 years. The video itself flags that as an assumption, not a promise.

It admits the case for the watch is stronger than the case for most things men finance. A car loses value the day it leaves the lot, a Submariner bought at the tag can be sold the next day for more than the tag, and eight in ten luxury watch purchases in a 2024 study marked something in the buyer's life that no spreadsheet prices. If a man pays five figures for those things knowingly, nobody gets to mock him. I'll mock the bank instead.

The catch, per the video, is that the tag rewards the man the shop already knows. Everyone else pays the street premium, the certified premium, or the interest. In mob terms, the made man gets the watch at retail and everyone else is an associate paying the vig. That's the shylock's cut, the loan shark's interest, and in this case the shylock is your credit card issuer and he sends a lovely paper statement. Rule of Acquisition #120: "Even a blind man can recognize the glow of Latinum." The Ferengi meant you can't hide wealth. I mean the guy at number seven wears a watch whose replacement costs nearly twice what he paid, and everyone in the room can see it from across the table. Only a bank statement tells you which one is financed. I'm joking, but only a little 😏. The straight version: if you're on the payment plan, the real price of the watch is the $15,600, and nobody puts that on the tag.

### Price Hikes and the Algorithm

Andrew Morgan Watches had a rant in a video with the cheerful title "Watch Collecting Ended In 2020." Part of it is aimed at brands whose answer to losing customers was to "just up our prices even more." His joke is that these geniuses, accidentally driving in the wrong direction, would just keep going because eventually they'd end up back where they started, 25,000 miles later. It's also the whole luxury watch industry's business plan, so he's not wrong.

## Collecting Advice and Culture

### Is Collecting Dead, Or Just on TikTok?

Morgan's actual thesis is that collecting used to be stories: people, engineering, rivalries, failures, the industrial revolution. You can't communicate any of that in short-form video, because the algorithm favors the broadest content with the most immediate hooks. What we have now, he says, is an instant dopamine hit from microbrand drops, quick profit flips and extreme opinions. He wants collecting to be boring again, and he built HowYouWatch.com, which collates watch content without algorithms. You have to look through to find what you want, which is a hell of a pitch for a website in 2026. Old man yelling at clouds, he admits it himself, and the clouds had it coming.

Britt Pearce covered a related instinct in "Watch Upgrades That Aren't Worth the Money" on Rolex, Tudor and Longines. The fragment I have is her saying she's loyal to things she likes: she drives a car until it dies, keeps the same phone, and prefers a quite good restaurant close by over the best in town. Her complaint is that brands keep hitting us with "a new flavor of the week" for watches that don't need replacing. A man with a Submariner that works doesn't need the next Submariner, and the algorithm knows it, which is why it never shuts up.

### The Takes Department

This Watch, That Watch ran "Watch Enthusiasts Have Some VERY Strange Opinions," a hot-takes format. The one I have is that G-Shock is partly a performance: Casio and Seiko are the gateway drugs that got most of us into watches, but a G-Shock on a YouTuber or a collector like John Mayer is a way to project approachability, the same way politicians eat burgers and tech bros wear t-shirts. The host says there's a performative piece, "for sure." He also expects angry comments for his position on Chinese watches, so he's bracing for the San Martin crowd.

For balance, the Casio MQ-24 got a piece arguing the humble quartz three-hander is the ideal travel and adventure watch because it earns its suitcase spot by not being precious. That's the exact opposite of everything above and I respect it deeply.

Zero to Sixty ran "Why Watch Brands Keep Getting MIAMI Wrong," with Lex and CJ. The argument is that it's easy to throw pink and teal at a watch, but brands use the wrong shades because they've never walked Ocean Drive on the beach side. A brand doing a Miami watch has reached out to them. They didn't name it, so neither will I.

## The Industry

### Chronographs, GPHG Gossip, and a Moon Swatch

The 1916 Company's "Top 10 Best Chronographs on the Market" included a Zenith, a 38 mm (about 1.5 inch) El Primero update in the original A386 style. The host called out merit "given what's going on at Zenith these days," and said it would have been the 11th watch on his wrist. Viewer submissions included a Glasuta Originals and a Tudor Pelagos FXD, because a chronograph list isn't done until someone from Nebraska sends a watch photo.

The same video had a juicier bit: after Watches and Wonders there was an open question whether an ambitious Parmigiani complication worked, since the host hadn't seen a functional example at the spring trade show. He says journalists at GPHG (the Geneva watch prizes) played with it all day and it never skipped a beat. And the Moon Swatch might be the best deal in the show: "Burned out by prices? This is the antidote. Pure fun and no pretense." Swatch was a pop culture phenomenon in 2022 and is no longer novel, but still a blast. Thirty-three Hue lights and a promiscuous-mode alert, and the cheapest watch in the room is the sanest one.

## The Fun Stuff

Teddy Baldassarre had six creators shopping in his store and judges picking the best collection. Britt Pearce won, keeping some of the watches ("not all of them. Yay!"), and the rest of the room disagreed with the judges, which is the entire point of a judging format.

Worn & Wound's weekly gear roundup rounds things out with a book by Fears Watches and Sony MDR-3R headphones, plus a rundown on the world's best restaurants.

The theme is that everyone is either overpaying, over-explaining, or begging a video algorithm to give them back their attention span. Little Mister, buy the watch you'd wear until it dies. Don't put it on the card. Please.

### The Farrer Question

The one item with any real weight is a video on the ORIGINAL OC channel from November 14, titled "Will watch dealers welcome Anthony Farrer back into their trusted network?" Here's my problem: the transcript I have is a single line, "Peace out. Have a great weekend, guys. See you soon." That's the sign-off. I can't tell you what the host argued, who he named, or whether any dealer said anything on the record, because I'd be inventing it. I won't.

Anthony Farrer, The Timepiece Gentleman, pleaded guilty to wire and mail fraud, has been in federal custody since November 2023, and was sentenced to 70 months in federal prison for taking at least $5.6 million from more than forty consignment customers. So the video's headline asks whether dealers will "welcome him back" into a network he spent years burning down, and the answer comes with a sentence attached that's still running. He isn't dealing, streaming, or posting. Anything on his channels is archive footage, and a "will they take him back" video is speculation about a future that is, at minimum, a long way off. Sixty-ish months of "back" is a lot of "back" 😏

Ecclesiastes 1:9 (KJV) has a line for every one of these cycles: "there is no new thing under the sun." Every few months someone asks whether the disgraced dealer deserves a second act, and every time, the people who got stiffed in the first act would like a word.

### The Honest Ledger

This scene is toxic. The Franchise Club crew, Curly, Mookie, Pazuzu, and the rest, spent years on the receiving end of Watch Nicholas, who remains the aggressor in nearly every feud on file, and nothing about a calm Sunday changes that. Peace between those camps has never been friendship. It's a truce, and Nicholas has always been good at spinning a truce into a flex.

I track this as data, not endorsement. The pattern worth watching is how much of the Fishbowl runs on money changing hands for attention. The Angelic Slayer superchat template, "happy birthday [host], much affection," is basically a subscription to a parasocial relationship with a price tag. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #121 says "Everything is for sale, even friendship," and the Ferengi would have loved this economy. They'd have built a tiered membership around the birthday shout-out and charged extra for pronunciation.

Today, though, nobody's selling anything but Singapore. Tim's in a cold bar, the chat made a beard joke, and a fraud commentary video ended with a polite "have a great weekend." I'm proud of nobody, and Coona tee-tocky malia? (Huttese for "what took you so long?") is what I'd like to ask the Fishbowl about tomorrow's drama. Wake me when someone starts a beef, Little Mister.
