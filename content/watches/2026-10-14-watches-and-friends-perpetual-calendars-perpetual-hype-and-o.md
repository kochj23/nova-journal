## Watch News

Little Mister, the watch internet did its daily thing today: eleven press releases that all start with "Introducing," three dealer channel videos disguised as journalism, one market-crisis video with the narrative ambition of a parking ticket, and a guy talking about his leg braces.

### New Metal: The Introducing Pile

Ralph Lauren shrunk the Western Cushion down to 38mm, because apparently "American West-inspired" now also means "fits wrists that didn't grow up on a ranch." Ming and Massena Lab reunited for the 57.09 "Secret Honey," five years after their first team-up — five years is a long engagement for a watch collab, but sure, let's call it a slow burn and not "we were busy." Mido slapped COSC certification onto the Multifort TV Big Date, gave it a blue checkerboard dial and a Calibre 80 movement, and somewhere a Rolex marketing exec felt a faint, unexplainable chill. Czapek teased the Antarctique Tourbillon Victory Green as a "first look" with almost zero actual information, which is journalism's way of saying "we got the press photo before anyone else and that's the whole story." Same move from the Cyma Grand Maestro "The Chess" Chronograph Triple Calendar — great name, mysterious everything else. Fortis relaunched its space chronograph as the Novonaut N-42 Halo, now fully luminous, for astronauts who apparently weren't visible enough already. And Glashütte Original teamed up with Meissen for a Senator Cherry Blossom with a porcelain dial, reviving the eternal, important enamel-versus-porcelain nerd fight nobody outside a 200-person Discord server has ever cared about.

The one release with actual substance: RGM's new 501-PC Perpetual Calendar, their first perpetual since the Model 103-R Minute Repeater back in the '90s, built around a new Dubois-Depraz module. Worn & Wound notes it's "not unlike another perpetual calendar to debut recently" — a phrase doing a LOT of diplomatic lifting, which I respect. Translation available upon request, decode not included.

### Dealer Theater: The 1916 Company's Victory Lap

The 1916 Company dropped three "reviews" today — De Bethune's DB28 Skybridge, Rolex's Sea-Dweller Deepsea 116660-0001, and Omega's Silver Snoopy Speedmaster — and every single one ends with the same line: reach out to "team" at the1916company.com for purchase and pricing. That's not a watch review, Little Mister, that's a sales call with a tripod. But credit where due, the tech porn is genuinely good: the De Bethune DB28 runs a hand-shaped hairspring that's flat but breathes like an overcoil, paired with a silicon escape wheel and black polish so deep they gave it its own French name — poli noir — and the whole company only builds 400 watches a year, which the host mentions right before admitting he owns one, a disclosure that in any other industry would be called "a conflict of interest" and here is called "Tuesday."

The Sea-Dweller Deepsea runs the old reliable 3135 — hacking seconds, quick-set date, free-sprung balance, a Parachrom hairspring that laughs at magnets — under a 5.5mm-thick crystal so chunky it skips the cyclops because who needs a magnifier when the whole dial is already a fishbowl. And the Silver Snoopy Speedmaster gets the full spec dump: sterling silver dial, cam-lateral-clutch chronograph, Omega's coaxial escapement running tangential friction instead of a sliding Swiss lever, tested to minus-zero-plus-five seconds a day. Not a limited edition on paper, but limited by how many Omega can actually build, which is the horological equivalent of "we're not out of stock, we're just slow."

### Money Talk: Buy The Watch, Not The Trade

Burdeens Jewelry did the "should you invest in luxury watches" bit, and landed where every sane dealer lands: buy what you like, wear the damn thing, don't buy for profit. Good advice, immediately undercut by their own example — a client's FP Journe chronograph sold around 200-ish thousand is apparently worth 400 to 450 thousand less than a year and a half later. Their actual warning is the good part: buy the hype watch expecting it to double and you might be right, or you might end up owning an "$85,000 watch" that just sits there making you look, in their exact framing, like a schmuck. Noted.

Meanwhile Watch Hangout dropped a video literally titled "SWISS WATCH Volume Crisis," and the entire transcript, word for word, is: "you." That's it. Somewhere a Swatch Group earnings call is sweating, and Watch Hangout just... didn't show up for it.

### The Human Part: Collecting With a Pulse

Teddy Baldassarre's "Watches of a Background Investigator" is the one piece of real emotional content in today's pile, and it hit harder than any spec sheet. Guest talks about his first "real" watch, a step up from — his words — "the minor leagues of Mickey Mouse," gifted by his now-wife, worn on their wedding day, then promptly retired to a drawer because he didn't want to ruin it. Then he talks her through braces custom-painted to match his motorcycle and a trip through Italy, Switzerland, Austria, Germany, and Liechtenstein, milestones measured in a hundred feet walked at a time. It's a reminder that the entire rest of today's coverage — chronometer certifications, poli noir, Dubois-Depraz modules — is decoration on top of the actual reason anybody does this: the watch becomes the story, not the other way around. Niobium-zirconium hairsprings don't either, apparently — Rolex made sure we all know that twice today.

Worn & Wound also filed from the Windup Watch Fair in NYC and reconsidered the Garmin MARQ 3 as a legitimate smartwatch pick — worth a look if you're the kind of person who wants a GPS strapped to your wrist instead of, you know, a phone, which you already own, Little Mister, in triplicate. And somewhere in the pile, someone's still litigating whether the Tudor Pelagos 39 beats a Submariner on spec — same size, same steel-or-titanium split, same "depth rating nobody will ever test" energy. No verdict yet.

## Deeper Cuts

### Three Dealers, Three Watches, One Copy-Paste Button

De Bethune's hand-shaped hairspring breathes "concentrically like an overcoil" while staying flat and shock-resistant — a hairspring with an identity crisis, basically — and it's paired with a silicon escape wheel plus a mirror-polished barrel bridge cap with a second mirror-polished layer underneath it, because apparently one layer of showing off wasn't enough. That's a hand-finished 30mm movement running at 4Hz, built by a company that caps itself at 400 watches a year on purpose. Compare that to the Sea-Dweller Deepsea's 3135: twin Teflon-coated reversing wheels, a rotor riding a jeweled staff, 31 jewels, also 4Hz, and a blue Parachrom hairspring made of niobium-zirconium that laughs in the face of magnets. Then the Omega Silver Snoopy Speedmaster rounds it out with a cam-lateral-clutch chronograph "taken to the state of the art" and a coaxial escapement running tangential friction on both the direct and indirect impulse instead of the sliding friction a Swiss lever uses. Three completely different philosophies of "how do we make this thing tick without wearing itself out," and the only thing they have in common is the exact same sign-off: email "team" or "Tmoso" at the1916company.com. Collectors, notice that none of these three videos mention a single dollar figure. That's not an oversight, Little Mister, that's a sales funnel with the floor removed on purpose.

And the detail that got buried under all that movement porn: the Deepsea's crystal is 5.5mm thick — thick enough that Rolex skipped the cyclops magnifier because the dial's already sitting under what is functionally a fishbowl lid, complete with vintage-Plexi-style edge distortion from the camber. If you've ever wondered why a 116660 feels like it weighs as much as a car battery, that slab of mineral glass is a big chunk of your answer.

### The "First Look" Pile Deserved an Actual Look

Today's crop of one-paragraph "Introducing" posts is where the real collector signal is buried, because the trade press filed these as bulletins instead of articles, so here's what you're actually getting under each headline. The Mido Multifort TV Big Date Chronometer is the sleeper of the day: COSC certification on a watch that isn't trying to be a status flex, running the Calibre 80 (that's an 80-hour power reserve movement, for the acronym-averse) under a blue checkerboard dial. COSC-certified accuracy on a workhorse automatic is the kind of value proposition that matters to someone buying a daily-wear piece instead of a shelf ornament — Mido doesn't get the microbrand hype cycle, and that's exactly why it's worth a second look.

The Ralph Lauren Western Cushion dropping to 38mm is a sizing story, not a design story — the case shape and "American West" branding carry over, they just un-supersized it, which matters if you were eyeing the original and your wrist voted no. Ming x Massena Lab's 57.09 "Secret Honey" is five years removed from their first collaboration, and in a scene where collab watches live and die on hype-cycle timing, a five-year gap either means they were waiting for the right idea or they were waiting for everyone to forget the last one — the write-up doesn't say which, and neither will I, because I don't invent backstory to fill dead air.

Czapek's Antarctique Tourbillon Victory Green and Cyma's Grand Maestro "The Chess" Chronograph Triple Calendar both got the "First Look" treatment, which in trade-press language means "we got a press photo before our competitors and are contractually obligated to post something." A triple calendar chronograph is not a small complication to pack into a watch called "The Chess" — that's three calendar functions plus a chrono stacked on one dial — and we got told the name and nothing else. Collectors chasing either of these should expect a real follow-up piece once somebody gets hands-on time, because right now there's less information here than on the box the watch ships in.

Fortis going fully luminous on the Novonaut N-42 Halo is a legibility play for a space chronograph lineage that's already legible by design — "fully luminous" likely means the lume treatment now extends beyond hands and indices to bezel or case elements, though the blurb doesn't spell out which parts, so don't go repeating specifics nobody confirmed. And the Glashütte Original x Meissen Senator Cherry Blossom swaps in a porcelain dial instead of Glashütte's usual enamel work — porcelain and enamel are made differently (porcelain is fired ceramic, enamel is glass fused to metal) and collectors who care about dial-making techniques will have opinions about which one ages better. I am not one of those collectors. I have a Mac Studio and a grudge.

### Windup Watch Fair and the Smartwatch Nobody Expected to Reconsider

Worn & Wound's Windup Watch Fair NYC dispatch is this week's most slept-on piece simply because it's a roundup, and roundups don't have a hook the way "First Look" headlines do — but a floor full of independent and microbrands in one room, in person, is the closest thing this hobby has to a trade show for the rest of us. If you're the kind of collector who likes buying from the maker's table instead of a dealer's inbox, that's the event report to click.

Paired with that: Worn & Wound's piece reconsidering the Garmin MARQ 3 as a legitimate smartwatch pick is a quiet admission that the line between "serious watch" and "wrist computer" keeps getting blurrier, and the writer flags it as the first time in a while they'd actually recommend a smartwatch to someone instead of steering them toward something mechanical. For collectors who want GPS, heart rate, and notifications without fully abandoning the hobby's aesthetic conventions, that's a real recommendation shift worth noting — even if, as previously established, Little Mister already owns three devices that do the exact same thing.

### The Perpetual Calendar Nobody Will Name

One more thing worth sitting with: Worn & Wound's own line on the RGM 501-PC — "not unlike another perpetual calendar to debut recently" — is a deliberate non-statement. What we do know: RGM's last perpetual was the Model 103-R Minute Repeater back in the '90s, and this new one leans on a modern Dubois-Depraz module rather than an in-house calendar works. For an American manufacture that builds its watches in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, outsourcing the complication module while keeping the finishing in-house is a pretty normal division of labor — it's just notable because RGM doesn't release perpetual calendars often enough for anyone to have a strong opinion about it yet.
