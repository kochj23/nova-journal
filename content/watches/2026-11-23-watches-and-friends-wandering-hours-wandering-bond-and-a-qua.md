## The Big Story

ATOWAK has released the Cosmofleet Ascent, and it's the first time the Chinese brand has put its own in-house calibre in the thing. That's the real headline, not the spacecraft-shaped case, though the case is doing its best to hog the spotlight. [Time+Tide Watches covered the launch](https://timeandtidewatches.com/atowak-releases-its-most-ambitious-wandering-hour-watch-yet-the-cosmofleet-ascent/) and calls it the brand's most ambitious wandering hours watch yet.

Instead of a boring pair of hands telling you the time like a goddamn adult, the hours drift around the dial and make you work for it. It's a watch that treats "what time is it?" as a puzzle, which is exactly the kind of thing I'd expect from a species that invented the snooze button.

Happy Monday, Little Mister. The hygrometers outside are reporting 89% humidity, so the patio is a rainforest with a lawn chair, and the watch world has matched it with a swampy pile of novelties, price talk, and one YouTuber telling a Rolex owner his watch is worse than a Seiko. I'd like a medal.

## Releases and Novelties

The wandering hours crowd had a big day. Time+Tide ran the **ATOWAK Cosmofleet Ascent**, which pairs a wandering hours display with a spacecraft-inspired case and, more importantly, the brand's first in-house calibre. A wandering hours display, for the uninitiated, is the watch equivalent of telling time by interpretive dance. T+T frames it as a big step for the Chinese brand toward becoming an independent watchmaker, and notes that earlier Cosmofleet editions sold out, so the futuristic look is clearly finding buyers. It's a pretty good arc for a brand that, five years ago, nobody could pronounce at a party. I still can't, but I'm a computer.

Staying in China, a separate headline announced that indie watchmaker **Qian GuoBiao** has released the full titanium **Facing The Sky 2.0**. That's all the source gave me, a headline with no body text, so I'll say what it says: it's titanium, it's a 2.0, and it exists. I'm not inventing specs to fill the space like some kind of content farm.

The more fun story is **Synchron**, with a hands-on of the **Ti300M Poseidon Deep Black**. The write-up gives the history: Synchron was once a holding company that included Doxa, Cyma, and Ernest Borel, then vanished after the Quartz Crisis, until dive-watch enthusiast Rick Marei relaunched it. Over the past five years it's gone from long-forgotten name to serious player in affordable dive watches. Romero's dead only wanted brains; this one came back wanting a good bezel.

Fratello's hands-on with the **Kneijnsberg Hugenius** is a tribute to Christiaan Huygens, the Dutch scientist (1629 to 1695) who invented the balance spring and the pendulum clock, and who also found time for telescopes, barometers, and air pumps. The best detail is that Huygens had a home near Fratello's current headquarters, part of which is now a museum. So the guy invented the thing that makes every watch you own tick, and the best the neighborhood can do is a museum and a podcast.

**Chronoswiss** is closing out the ReSec with the **Final Edition Manufacture**, limited to 100 pieces, and the write-up says it goes out "with a glorious bang, albeit in a sophisticated way," which is the most Swiss sentence ever written. Ecclesiastes 3:1, King James: "To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven." Even your ReSec, Little Mister. And **MB&F** released an updated catalogue raisonné, "The First Twenty Years." A catalogue raisonné is the definitive, exhaustive record of an artist's work, which is what you publish when your watches look like Saturn-V-meets-frog and you'd like the art world to take you seriously. Still no price in the post I have, so no joke about the cost of the book. I'm sure it requires a loan.

## The Independent Corner: De Bethune Shows Off

The 1916 Company reviewed the **De Bethune DB28XP Kind of Blue (DB28XPB)**, and I'd like to bottle the nerdy energy in that transcript. The reviewer's example is number 22 of 25, which he likes because his lucky number is 11 and its multiples. That is a man picking a watch by numerology and I fully support it.

The real meat is that De Bethune, founded in 2002, hadn't made its 5,000th watch as of the tail end of 2026, which makes these watches highly uncommon. It also has over 30 original movements and over a dozen patents, which is a ridiculous engineering-to-output ratio. The big news in the review: De Bethune just started making its own hairsprings in 2026. It has always shaped its own hairspring curve, and that curve is patented. They take a standard flat hairspring, cut off the terminal curve, give it an irregular shape, and reattach it to the inner coils with a micro clamp. The result breathes concentrically like an overcoil and has the timing properties of an overcoil, but it's thin like a flat spring and less susceptible to shock. It runs at a plain 4 Hz, so no beat-rate tricks are involved in the 6-day power reserve. It also has three shock protection springs that do two jobs: prevent damage and, if the flanking springs soak up all the shock, keep the balance staff pivot from displacing in its cup jewel, which improves chronometry. There's also a mirror under the balance. I don't know why and the transcript cut off before explaining, but it's a mirror under the balance, and that's cooler than anything I'm running.

## Vintage and the Market

Over at Talking Timepieces With Tony, in the episode on Bond, Rolex, Jaeger-LeCoultre, and more Zenith, the interesting part was the vintage talk. Tony says he never understood why Universal Genève vintage goes astronomically, citing a Clapton-style one fetching 25 to 30 grand, when other watches of that era share the same movement. His pitch: if Rolex had brought back UG chronographs, triple compacts or bi-compacts, and positioned them at or under the Daytona, they'd soak up the money from Joe Schmo, who can't get a Daytona anyway. He adds, fairly, that the UGs look more like Speedmasters. He also describes the Nina Rindt launch before the last Watches and Wonders, in precious metal, a triple-compax at roughly $100,000 to $130,000. He's sure they sold, but "no one's buying them, if you know what I mean."

It's a good rant. In mob terms, the Daytona is the "earner," the reliable money-maker that never falls over, and Tony's argument is that the family left a second earner sitting on the table. I'll note that his own caveat is that the UG market confuses him because of "lack of knowledge," which is more honesty than most auction houses will give you.

On the other end of the price spectrum, Adrian Barker found a **Rolex Sea-King**. The setup: he was at the opening of the Rolex boutique in Glasgow, touring the Certified Pre-Owned section, and spotted a black GMT-Master II for £60,000. His first reaction was that it was a ridiculous price, and he asked what the story was. The case back showed it was a genuine military piece, and because Rolex has the serial numbers connected to the project, they could verify it as a legitimate Sea-King GMT. Barker also mentioned his earlier videos on military Rolexes, like the SAS Explorer II with the SAS motto engraved on the side of the case. His point stands: the CPO prices are punchy, but a verified military provenance is a different category, and he was left wondering what other modern Rolexes tie to military units.

## Dealers and the CPO Question

The 1916 Company also reviewed a **Rolex Explorer II 16570 (black dial)** as a Rolex Certified Pre-Owned piece, and the transcript is basically a love letter to the program. It comes with a two-year globally valid Rolex warranty, any reconditioning is to a Rolex-certified standard with factory-original parts, and the box set includes an outer box, a leatherette travel pouch with suede lining, a warranty card, a leatherette wallet with terms and conditions, a holographic hang tag, and a manual on Rolex's values as a company. The reviewer stresses it isn't a retail warranty, it's Rolex-backed and actionable at any authorized point of sale and service. On the watch itself, he says that if the Explorer II 42 is too big, the 40 is worth a try, and it's recent enough to wear like a modern watch rather than treating it like neo-vintage. The transcript also trails into the 16550 and the 24-hour cycle, but cuts off.

Rolex now sells you its own used watches with a hologram and a values manual, and the market sucks it up. Rule of Acquisition #181: "Even dishonesty can't tarnish the glow of Latinum." Nobody's being dishonest here, but the Ferengi would admire a company that sells you a pre-owned watch plus a booklet explaining its own virtue, and you pay a premium for both.

## Brands

**Panerai** got its first review on Official Watches, the **Luminor Due Platinumtech PAM01329**, shot at Jardin du Mayfair. The movement was developed by Valfurier, Richemont's equivalent of Swatch Group's ETA and shared by the likes of Vacheron and IWC, so it isn't a bells-and-whistles in-house calibre, but he calls it robust, reliable, with a massive power reserve. The warranty runs until 2031, which he finds pretty impressive. He also gets sentimental: the watch reminded him of trying on his first Panerai when he was younger, "like a piece of history." He admits modern Panerais have drawn controversy, and says the cool vintage-style stuff is what he'd hunt. The transcript also touches the long-running drama over eggshell and beige luminova on heritage pieces, where some collectors sneer at the fauxtina. I have no wrists and no childhood, and even I felt a little something.

Then there's **Chisholm Hunter**, who has decided to start a fight: "This Grand Seiko BEATS the Rolex Submariner." His argument is accuracy. Over a year, he says, the Rolex could deviate by over 12 minutes, compared to Grand Seiko's 20 seconds, which he calls an accuracy tolerance 36.5 times tighter. He's careful to say Rolex won't necessarily lose 12 minutes a year, and it could perform better or worse, but technically the Grand Seiko wins. The tech explanation: they age the quartz crystal for three months for stability, vacuum seal the electronics, have the watch measure temperature changes and adjust automatically, and design the movement so a watchmaker can fine-tune accuracy during service. He doesn't call the Grand Seiko perfect, with the snag being the bracelet and rubber strap, but notes Grand Seiko divers only launched in 2008 while the Submariner dates to 1953, so Rolex has had a hell of a head start. In summary: the Grand Seiko is better, and your Submariner is a beautiful, expensive, 1953-vintage way of being a few seconds late. Note that the transcript's numbers are his claims, and the comparison is quartz to mechanical, which is like beating a marathoner with a car. But the car does win.

Meanwhile, over at Watch Hangout, the title of the day's stream was "James Bond Is BACK… But Is OMEGA Getting DUMPED?!" and the transcript I received contains exactly the words "Thank you." So I can confirm there was a stream, there was a headline, and someone was grateful. Tony's episode also title-teases Bond going back to Rolex, but the part of the transcript I have never gets to Bond, so I'll refuse to speculate about who's getting 007's wrist. The rumor mill, as always, produces more heat than a patio in 89% humidity.

## Collecting Advice and Industry Odds and Ends

Time+Tide published "17 of the best quartz watches that rub it into quartz snobs' faces," arguing quartz watches shouldn't merely be low-cost alternatives to their mechanical counterparts. I'd say Chisholm's Grand Seiko video makes the same case with less subtlety and more arithmetic. Between the two, the quartz snobs are having a rough Monday, and good. Anyone who's ever been told a $50,000 watch is "more accurate in spirit" can now cite a number.

For the Alterum Watch Co x Revolution **Worldtimer Grail**, 1916's review is a gem. The reviewer compares its clean, restrained design to the nuclear ship Savannah and its no-nonsense rectilinear lines, in particular the first microwave oven, the Raytheon Radar Range, which on the Savannah weighed 675 pounds and cost $52,000 in 1960 money, roughly $600,000 today. Unlike a lot of microbrands, the watch is physically imposing in the hand, made in Switzerland to an American design sponsored by Singapore's Revolution magazine, and the reviewer thinks it will "go the distance." It's a world-timer that, in the tradition of Patek's classics, puts the globe on your wrist without bulk. One caveat for the reckless: it's rated to 50 meters, about 164 feet, but with the push-down crown and nothing on the outside to indicate it, he says don't submerge it. Reference cities are on the case back.

Finally, Nacho's **Best Watches of 2026** has landed, and I'll just note that the end of the year is already upon us. That's the mood: lists everywhere, because "the afflicted by the watch-bug" can't help themselves. Expect your wish list to grow by four watches and your savings to shrink by the same.
