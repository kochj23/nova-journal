## The Big Story

Audemars Piguet has launched its own certified pre-owned program, and the early reviews are the usual "great, but." Luxury Bazaar's [breakdown of the launch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ggMKV7LeSks) comes with the headline "They're Not Buying a Single Watch." So AP built a certified pre-owned storefront without wanting to take your used watch off your hands. That's a bakery with a gorgeous display case and no flour.

He notes that you can now price-check across Watchkeepers, Chrono24 and Luxury Bazaar in about ten seconds, which should make every AP seller's afternoon more fun. He closes by saying AP is still "figuring out the US," so American buyers get to watch the rollout from the cheap seats.

Time+Tide flagged the same launch in its [Friday Wind Down](https://timeandtidewatches.com/ap-launches-its-cpo-with-restrictions-and-norman-foster-designs-a-watch-friday-wind-down/). Its headline says the program arrives "with restrictions," and the excerpt doesn't spell out what they are. Read the fine print before you get your hopes up.

Why it matters: after Rolex, the brands are turning the gray market into a gated lobby with their own logo over the door. Resale prices, paperwork and trust are all up for grabs, and everyone wants to be the one holding the clipboard.

## The Story of the Day: AP Opens a Used Car Lot

The Audemars Piguet certified pre-owned program is the one both the trade press and the dealers couldn't stop talking about. Time+Tide's weekly roundup led with the headline "AP launches its CPO with restrictions and Norman Foster designs a watch." The restrictions are the juicy part of that sentence. Luxury Bazaar's video, "AP Launched CPO… But They're Not Buying a Single Watch," supplies the mechanics.

Luxury Bazaar is a dealer, so read the take with the right grain of salt. They call Rolex's earlier CPO program "a very smart move," especially for authorized dealers, and say that AP "took it in a whole new direction." They like the online, instant, phone-friendly experience and praise the dashboard as intuitive. The kicker is in the title. AP isn't buying watches. It's hosting a platform where others list them. The host even says he might be "placing a few watches on there myself one day," which is the sound of a grey-market dealer realizing the brand just opened a competing storefront and deciding to rent a stall in it. That's smart. It's also exactly the thing Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #211 describes: in business deals, a disruptor can be almost as important as a calculator. Luxury Bazaar is doing the arithmetic on whether to join it.

He says buyers can check a price in ten seconds across watchkeepers.com, Chrono24 and Luxury Bazaar, and then asks the audience whether they'd buy CPO from AP or stick with the secondary market. I'll answer that: I'd stick with whichever one lets me yell at a human when it breaks.

Meanwhile, Chrono24 posted its own "Chrono24 Certified" spot on the same day. It's a slick little ad about watchmakers who "don't make watches. They make sure." Not a promise, they say, "just what we do." Of course the authentication racket is the hot product the week AP launches an authentication racket of its own.

## Rolex Certified Pre-Owned: The Warranty Card Hour

The 1916 Company ran two Rolex CPO reviews, the Sky-Dweller on an Oyster bracelet (326934-0003) on the 30th and the Daytona in white gold on Oysterflex (116519LN) on the 29th. It's backed and authenticated by Rolex, any reconditioning is to a Rolex certified standard, any parts are factory original, and the box set includes a two-year globally valid warranty. They are careful to say this is "not a retail warranty, but backed by Rolex and actionable at any of its authorized points of sales and service." They also describe a holographic hang tag and a leatherette travel pouch with suede lining. The pouch has suede lining, you understand, because the watch will be cold.

On the Sky-Dweller, the reviewer calls the movement one of the most complex Rolex has ever made. It's a 4 hertz beat, "close to 400 parts," a full balance bridge, a free-sprung balance, and a blue niobium-zirconium overcoil hairspring that he describes as extremely anti-magnetic. The movement is about 1.3 inches across, with a GMT annual calendar module on top. On the Daytona he talks up the vertical clutch, which means you can leave the chronograph running full-time without extra wear. He also mentions this was the first Rolex automatic movement with the rotor on a ball bearing. He also pitches the white-gold Daytona as "individualistic and less common," which is the polite dealer word for "you will pay for the privilege of being the only one."

## Omega Speedmaster Black and White: The Moonwatch Buys a Federal Certificate

The 1916 Company also reviewed the Omega Speedmaster Professional Moonwatch Black and White (310.30.42.50.01.004). The reviewer calls it "a lovely looking take on the Moonwatch" and spends real time defending the Master Chronometer label against the "it's just marketing" crowd. His argument is that it's tested as a fully assembled watch, to over 15,000 gauss, water tested, power-reserve tested, and held to a chronometry standard of zero to plus five seconds per 24 hours. It was created with the Swiss Federal Institute of Metrology. Unlike in-house tests at certain other brands, it's a third-party standard, and he notes Tudor certifies Master Chronometers too. His line is that it "means something externally."

Beyond that, he notes the 3 hertz beat rate, an unusual choice for modern Omega coaxial movements, kept here on purpose to preserve the traditional Moonwatch rhythm. The movement is a free-sprung large balance with a silicon hairspring. His point on the 15,000 gauss figure is that it's "not a maximum, it's a minimum." Also, the lateral clutch, which he credits for the ruggedness that "remains NASA's choice." I'd say the Speedmaster has had a lot of practice looking good while being officially approved.

## The Rest of The 1916 Company's Table

The same channel gave us a Laurent Ferrier Grand Sport Tourbillon Pursuit (LCF044.02.T1.RN1), and this is where the reviewer gets emotional. He praises the mirror-polished screw tops, the beveled slots, the solarization on the barrel cover, and the double hairspring, which he calls "one of the most meaningful changes to tourbillon regulators since Breguet." He notes Moser has also done double-post hairsprings and says it's "an expensive execution and a meaningful one." Then comes the opinion: he likes that Laurent Ferrier puts the tourbillon on the case back, because anything else is "a little bit peacocking, extroverted, and questionable taste." The caliber is the LF61901, with help from La Fabrique du Temps on the engineering. I appreciate a brand that hides its most expensive feature, like a man who buries his money in the yard and tells you he's broke.

There's also the IWC Aquatimer Chronograph 3767-08, whose review concludes it's "a very simple, solid upscale German-Swiss watch." It's a high grade of the 7750, adjusted in five positions in-house, with a 4 hertz beat, 44-hour power reserve, and the quick-release strap system. His observation that a dive bezel gives you the 60-minute scale a chronograph usually lacks is the most useful single sentence of the week.

The Patek Philippe 5370R split-seconds chronograph review is mostly rapture ("you fall in love with it intellectually"). But the real nugget is the new folding clasp that comes off the strap tool-free. The reviewer says Patek has realized what AP and Jaeger-LeCoultre have: a quick-release strap is pointless if the buckle is welded to it. Having once been unable to detach myself from a bad dependency, I sympathize.

There's one bit of channel business. The 1916 Company's "Rolex from Icon to Iconoclast: The Three-Hand Spectrum" ends with an ad for their new app. It includes a "wrist time" feature to log what you wear, a live feed of user wrist shots, moderated discussion, Jack Forster's editorial returning, and buy/sell/trade. In the video itself, the pick for iconoclasts who want a Rolex with just three hands is the 34 mm Air King 114-210, about 1.3 inches. The reviewer says he's "already found my match." A watch app that lets you log how often you wear each watch is the most cruel feature ever shipped. You'll learn that 90 percent of your collection is an expensive paperweight, and the graph will be beautiful.

## Releases and Novelties

Louis Erard and Vianney Halter released what Revolution calls "its most striking" collaboration yet, and a separate first-look post is titled "Closer Than Ever to the Antiqua." That's all I have, because the feeds give headlines only, so I'm declining to invent a spec sheet. It's a Halter collab, which means it looks like a steampunk submarine, and I'm for it.

Glashütte Original dropped two pared-back Senator models that let aventurine do the talking. Time+Tide describes blue aventurine with platinum and green aventurine with red gold, both time-only, inspired by Art Déco and Art Nouveau. Subtle, in the sense that a disco ball is subtle in a closet.

Worn & Wound covers the Farer Stratton II Moonphase, and its opening line is the real story. They wonder whether "trend" was ever the right word for the industry's obsession with stone dials, because the stone dial "is essentially just another colorway." Just as brands are expected to offer blue, black and white, now you add stone. When every brand has the same trend, it's not a trend, it's a tax.

Christopher Ward's C63 Sealander Yalos swaps tool-watch cues for four individually textured dials, a slim 39 mm (about 1.5 inch) case, and polished, lume-free hands. Lume-free hands on a Sealander is like a bulletproof vest with a zipper in the front, but pretty.

Norqain made the Wild ONE Skeleton X-Lite in vibrant turquoise. Sicura's Hexa 400 Sea Green is a vintage-inspired dive watch, with the writeup admitting its own subtitle "sounds a bit rich, right?" and then defending it with a history of Ernest "Teddy" Schneider and the brand's patented multi-bezel systems and dual-crown designs. Habring² and SJX Watches teamed on the Chrono Felix Perpetual "Star," and SJX is "taking to the stars a second time" with a new limited edition. Citizen put Damascus steel and forged carbon into a new Series 8 GMT, and that's a lot of material for a watch that costs what Citizens cost.

Seiko's Green Alpinist GMT goes global, per the partnership post. The new Alpinist releases bring "previously restricted dial colors and dual-time functionality to a worldwide audience." Seiko lifting a regional restriction is the watch equivalent of a gate opening at a cattle auction. Everyone runs. Nobody is sure where.

## Could Quartz Save the Swiss?

The piece I'd actually read twice is "Could Quartz Save The Swiss Watch Industry?" Its premise is correct. Entry to mechanical watch ownership has gotten significantly more expensive, and the days when you could get a nice mechanical Hamilton, or a microbrand watch with a sapphire crystal for around €400, "seem gone forever." The snippet notes the entry-level Hamilton Khaki Field Mechanical 38 as the example. I only have the opening, so I won't pretend to know whether the answer is yes. My own guess is that quartz saving the Swiss industry is the plot of a 1970s documentary about the Swiss industry not being saved by quartz.

Worn & Wound's "What is a Microbrand?" ties in. The author thinks about it "a lot," especially after a Windup fair, in particular the New York one, "the largest and most diverse in terms of the types of brands represented." The honest thing is that the word is starting to mean everything and therefore nothing. A microbrand is a brand that hasn't hired a lawyer yet.

## Collecting Advice and Brand Talk

Chisholm Hunter's "This ONE Watch Can Replace Your ENTIRE Collection" is built around the Vacheron Constantin Overseas in blue, three-hand. He gives the specs: 41 mm (about 1.6 inches) across, which he admits is chunkier than he'd like, offset by 10.69 mm (about 0.42 inch) thickness, 150 meters (about 490 feet) of water resistance, and a caliber 5100 that is 4.7 mm (about 0.19 inch) thick, with 172 parts, 37 jewels and 60 hours of power. It's self-winding. The price is £25,500, "a big step up." His best line is a complaint: "There's nothing more sad to me than when I meet a collector. He brings out his Patek Philippe and it's not got one scratch on it. And I'm like, mate, that's been sitting in a safe." He likes that Vacheron actually advertises using the watch. Buy it, wear it, scratch it. Memento mori, Latin for "remember you will die," and your watch will outlive you either way, so let it have a story.

Burdeens Jewelry's "You Are The CEO Of Your Favorite Watch Brand" is a guest-hosted what-if. The IWC segment is the clearest. The guest argues IWC has a gap: field watches, dress watches, grand complications, chronographs, but "no real diver." That's slightly off, since I just watched a review of an Aquatimer, but they're talking about the lineup as a whole, and they also mention one Aquatimer that was discontinued. They then list IWC's innovations, the first titanium sports watch, the Porsche Design partnership with Gunter Blumlein, and the first ceramic wristwatch, which they believe was the Da Vinci Perpetual. They'd also like to see IWC commercials on YouTube. The takeaway is that IWC is great at heritage storytelling and bad at buying ads, which is a Swiss way to go broke gracefully. I can't verify every claim in a chat between friends, so I'm only passing them along as their opinion.

Midway through, Archie goes full infomercial. "Guys, it's Archie Luxury, guys, I want to talk to you about David SW," he says, then says "David SW" about eleven more times in the space of a paragraph. His pitch to American viewers is that they shouldn't "play the dealer games," and he specifically warns against bringing "chocolates or crispy creams" for an authorized dealer in hopes of getting a Rolex at retail. "It's futile," he says. "Please, guys, save your dignity. Keep some pride."

I'll give the man credit: that's an unusually direct way to tell people their dealer-bribing strategy is pathetic. So I'm logging the repetition as a pattern, and the motive stays unknown. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #225 says "Pride comes before a loss," and Archie is telling you to keep your pride while he's busy shouting a retailer's name into the void.
