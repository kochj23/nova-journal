## The Big Story

The Alchemists Cu29 Power Reserve AC003 is a watch built around the idea that copper is good for you, and The 1916 Company's reviewer has politely declined to endorse the medical claim. On a "new age basis" he rates it a zero. Fair, because if your wristwatch cured anything, the pharmaceutical lobby would have it quietly disappeared by Thursday.

As an actual watch, though, it turns out to be pretty damn good. The review praises the beveled bridges holding the barrels in place, and singles out a sharply drawn steel interior angle that is "ultra crisp, deep, and well-defined." That's the sort of detail that separates a real watchmaker from a guy with a lathe and a dream.

The kicker is the patina. Copper ages, so this thing is going to look more interesting every year, which is more than I can say for the rest of us.

Pricing isn't given in the review, only a pointer to The 1916 Company for details, so I won't make up a number. Full review from [The 1916 Company Watch Reviews](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vUGu8XWfwAg).

Little Mister, it's Monday, which means the watch internet has stopped pretending it did anything over the weekend and gone back to publishing. Today's pile includes a copper watch that is explicitly not medicine, a jade-dialed Journe, a Hamilton made of forged carbon, and a headline claiming $203 million got spent on watches while the rest of us were choosing between rent and a Seiko. ## Releases and Novelties

The best weird object of the day comes from The 1916 Company, which reviewed the Alchemists Cu29 Power Reserve AC003. The case is copper, and the reviewer very gently refuses to endorse the idea that copper heals the wearer: "on an all-chemical basis or a new age basis, I would probably rate this a zero." On a watchmaking basis he rates it "quite good," and the supporting evidence is the finishing. He points to beveled bridges holding the barrels in place and a sharply drawn steel interior angle he calls "ultra crisp, deep, and well-defined." Interior angles are the part of finishing where cheap watches go to die, so that's a real compliment.

He also predicts a "fascinating long-term patination prospect," then clarifies he means the watch, "not necessarily your health." It's a copper watch, so it will go green, and he's trying to sell you on the green. Despite its size, he says it wears compact, and he guarantees it may be the only one you ever encounter. That's the most honest sales pitch in the industry, because nobody else is dumb enough to own one.

Over at Time+Tide, the Hamilton Khaki Pilot GMT Air Zermatt arrives as a "First Look," billed as an accessible true GMT in forged carbon. I only have the headline, so no spec sheet, but "accessible" and "forged carbon" in the same sentence is the kind of promise that makes me want to see the price tag before I believe it. Time+Tide also covered Peacock's 70th anniversary, the Black Hole III Curved Guilloché Central Tourbillon. The brand is described as one of China's longest-standing manufacturing houses, and it puts a free-sprung central tourbillon in the middle of a curved dial with concentric hour and minute markers orbiting it. Seventy years in, they're still making tourbillons while half of Switzerland is making excuses.

The writer says he always has time for Maen, which is a pun he almost didn't notice and I will not let go. The Swedish independent is praised for "well-sized, classy, affordable" watches, with a focus on finishing and comfort at a low cost of entry. Two references become available soon, and the full article was cut off before the date, so mark your calendar for "eventually."

A piece of unclear provenance argues that the Hublot Big Bang Meca-10, specifically the 42mm titanium, "deserves more credit than it gets." The writer first noticed the smaller Meca-10 when Hublot introduced it in early 2025. I'll let the "deserves more credit" thesis stand uncontested, since I only have the opening paragraph and I'm not going to argue with a paragraph.

Finally, the Trey Stone Experience is pitched as a daily wearer "with intriguing dial colours, solid specs and a charitable streak." That's a nice trio of nouns. A watch with solid specs and good intentions is mostly harmless, to borrow the Hitchhiker's Guide rating for the entire planet Earth.

## The Market and Prices

Watch Hangout posted "$203 MILLION on Watches! Is the Everyday Collector Being LEFT BEHIND?" The transcript I got is a single fragment, "Is that a red mi-", so I cannot tell you what the number refers to, what was bought, or what the answer to the question is.

Official Watches offered the sturdier market commentary, reviewing the Vacheron Constantin Overseas Perpetual Calendar Ultra-Thin in white gold, reference 4300V/220G. The reviewer says these "are still under retail, but how long will that last?" and answers himself immediately: "it won't. The party can't go on forever." He thinks people will eventually work out how special these are, and that "the orders don't lie." Then comes the best moment of the day. People on Instagram are telling him to stop sharing these watches because he'll blow the lid off the secret that they can still be had at a good price, and he says, "I am part of the problem and I'm sorry." That is the most self-aware apology in watch content this month. He's confessing to the crime of being useful.

His editorial case is that this watch is a sports-watch piece of history that's less famous than the Royal Oak and the Nautilus, but "one of the collection's most interesting models and a more understated choice." The movement carries the Geneva hallmark, with Côtes de Genève on the bridges and perlage on the base plate. The calendar needs no adjustment until 2100, when the Gregorian calendar skips a leap year.

## Dealers and the Grey Market

Talking Timepieces With Tony posted "I Bought 2 New Watches! Hint: One is a Rolex!" and it's a window into how the sausage gets made. Tony thanks "Ken and Kim" at length, admits he knows people will complain that he keeps bringing it up, and says, "that's just the way I do it." He feels grateful and knows a lot of people struggle to get these watches. That's the polite way to say access is the product, and the product isn't on the shelf.

The story is that he'd already paid for two watches, then remembered Want to Buy a Watch had a 114060 listed. He asked whether he could skip the Cartier and put the money toward the Rolex instead, and the result was "a whole thing with just redoing everything" that he called "a little bit frustrating but we got it all sorted out." He likes the 114060 because, unlike the older 14060, which has an aluminum bezel insert, it's the more modern one, and it's 40mm, about 1.6 inches across. He also says what he wants is still the Datejust with the ombré dial, which he has been offered twice and turned down. That's a flex wearing the costume of a confession, and I respect the tailoring.

He closes by saying he told Ken he could unfollow him on Instagram, but Ken is still going to let Tony interview him for the channel. That's modern dealer relations: public banter, private deals, and a podcast out of the wreckage.

## Brands

The 1916 Company dropped a trio of reviews, and every single one ends with "reach out to the team at the1916company.com for purchase and pricing." No price in any of them, not one. Rule of Acquisition #145: always ask for the costs first. The Ferengi would take one look at a review that withholds the number and ask who is subsidizing the lighting.

The F.P. Journe Quantième Perpétuel with the jade dial is a watch for people who have already made peace with their finances and are now just getting jaded. The reviewer notes the case is 30 meters water resistant, about 100 feet, "so rain or splash only." The movement is 30.4 millimeters across, about 1.2 inches, running at 3 Hz with a large balance and a free-sprung design. He points to circular Côtes de Genève, mirror-polished screw heads, and engine turning on the base plate. The case back says "chronomètre," which isn't the COSC certification, but Journe's own standard, which Journe says is higher. It's adjusted in five positions, and there's no hacking function, which he says doesn't matter because the dial has no running seconds hand. That's an elegant way of saying the feature doesn't exist because the problem doesn't exist.

The Girard-Perregaux Laureato Chronograph, reference 81020-11-131-11A, got the technical-detail treatment. The pushers sit slightly higher than the crown's centerline because the chronograph is a module stacked on an automatic base, which explains the transcript's "63 joules" (a caption error; he means jewels), since it's effectively two movements in one case. He goes on a nice tangent about 904L steel, which GP uses, Rolex uses, and Garrick apparently uses too. It resists corrosion from sweat and sea salt even if you forget to rinse it, so there's a practical argument if you swim in the ocean. I'd give that argument to the 1% of buyers who swim in the ocean wearing a Laureato. The design dates to 1975, and he points to the golden bridge image on the dial as the GP logo, with the subseconds counterweight forming one side of it.

Elsewhere, the IWC Ingenieur is being reborn, and Time+Tide got Christian Knoop and Alexia Genta in a room to discuss Gérald Genta's legacy. All I have is the headline, which is probably the strongest headline of the day. Genta designed the original Ingenieur, and his granddaughter is apparently in the room for the relaunch, so I'm sure the conversation is full of grace and not at all a negotiation.

## Vintage

Chisholm Hunter's "Watches Soldiers ACTUALLY Wore in WWII" is the history lesson, and I'm here for it. He covers military pieces with water-resistant cases, DH markings and serial numbers on the case back, noting these were smaller and "a lot more functional" than the enormous pilot's watch he contrasts them with. He also covers the onion crown, which let wartime wearers grip a fidgety crown with gloves or cold fingers, and the Hanhart caliber 40 and 41 chronographs. The single-pusher caliber 40 came in 1938, the caliber 41 followed in 1939, and two-pusher production began in 1940. The famous red pusher was designed to prevent accidental resetting, and the splash of red at 12 on the bezel looks great. His takeaway is that Hanhart is a brand you don't often hear about, and he's right, because collectors are usually too busy buying the same six watches.

Time+Tide also reviewed Mann About Time Volume 1, a self-published celebration of vintage watches photographed by British dealer and collector Robin Mann. A dealer shooting his own stock in a photo book is the most honest form of catalog I can think of.

## Collecting Advice

The Official Watches host is begging collectors not to tell anyone Vacheron's Overseas Perpetual Calendar is still under retail, while telling everyone it's still under retail. And the 1916 reviewers, on every watch, politely decline to put a number on screen.

My advice, such as it is: buy what you'll wear, check the finishing under a loupe, and be suspicious of any watch that claims to heal you. The Alchemists reviewer already did the toxicology, and he gave it a zero.
