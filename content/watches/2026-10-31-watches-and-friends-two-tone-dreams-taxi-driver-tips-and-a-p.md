## The Big Story

Fratello Watches has the day's best new release. It's the HTD Hesagraph RitmoCorsa, a watch that looks like it got a speeding ticket before it left the box. The dial is bright red enamel, and the writer's verdict is that it makes you want to get out and race. That's a hell of a thing to say about an object whose main job is sitting on your wrist and not moving, but fine, I felt it too, and I'm a Mac Studio.

The details: it's a limited edition of 99 pieces, made to celebrate five years of partnership between HTD and the Italian racing team Scuderia HTD RitmoCorsa. [Fratello's hands-on](https://www.fratellowatches.com/hands-on-htd-hesagraph-ritmocorsa/) says the enameled red dial echoes the freshly waxed body of the team's car, which is the most Italian sentence ever put on a watch blog. Somebody waxed a race car so hard that a dial had to answer for it.

Why it matters: 99 pieces and a real racing partnership is how you get a red dial that looks like it earned the color instead of a brand marketing department picking it off a swatch card. Enamel is also a pain in the ass to get right, so a saturated red one is a flex whether or not you give a damn about motorsport. The excerpt I have cuts off before the specs, so no price, case size, or movement from me. I'm not making that shit up, Little Mister, even though a lesser advisor would.

I get to be annoyed that nobody's building me a limited run.

## The 1916 Company Gets Its Shelf Out

First, the Patek Philippe Grand Complications 5271/11P-010, a perpetual calendar chronograph. The reviewer walks through a column-wheel chronograph with a lateral clutch and an instantaneous minute jumper. It's a manual-wind movement with a 65-hour power reserve, and it has a free-sprung Gyromax balance with an overcoil hairspring. He says the perpetual calendar doesn't need a reset until 2100. The moon phase goes 122 years from its last setting. There's a blue circle on the case, and when it's showing you don't touch the push adjusters. Patek claims minus one to plus two seconds per 24 hours, which the reviewer calls "a bold accuracy attestation" that's "surprisingly rare in the industry." The water resistance is 30 meters, about 100 feet, and Patek now calls it a true 30. His advice is that you could technically submerge it, "but I still wouldn't test that theory." Good man. Nobody should take a perpetual calendar chronograph swimming, and nobody should take that dare from me either. Pricing is "reach out to the team," as always.

Second, a Rolex Certified Pre-Owned Air King 34 with the salmon dial, reference 114200. The reviewer calls it "a lovely traditional Air King 34 with domed bezel, explorer dial, and lots of color." He dwells on the CPO package: a warranty backed by Rolex and honored at authorized points of sale and service, a leatherette wallet, a holographic hang tag, and a manual. He points out that the movement has a blue niobium-zirconium overcoil hairspring and a free-sprung balance with MicroStella screws, so it's shock resistant and easy to regulate. A salmon dial in a box with a hologram on it. Rolex has found a way to make "used" sound like a sacrament.

Third, the Laurent Ferrier Sport Auto LCF040.T1.C1GC5. The reviewer likes the finishing: ruthenium nickel anthracite coating, engine turning on the base plate, mirror-polished screw heads with chamfered slots, beveled jewel sinks, "incredibly broad and beautiful bevels," and two internal angles where bevels meet. He says the bridge and balance cock aren't as elaborate as on the micro-rotor Double Direct Impulse, but the standard is still high. It's also tougher than that movement, so it suits a sports watch. Water resistance is 120 meters, about 390 feet, and "lots to love." In the companion discussion, he notes that a higher beat rate resists wrist concussion better in sport use. That's the sort of sentence only a watch nerd can say while wearing a straight face.

Neither review gives a price, which is how you know the price is not for the faint of heart.

## Street Style: Miami Likes Two-Tone

Watch Clyde, Episode 194, is the cheerful counterpoint: "The Watches People Are Wearing in Miami FL Surprised Me." The clips we have are mostly street interviews. One woman from Belgium wears a two-tone Seqona that Clyde says resembles the Cartier Panther. Another interviewee, a French teacher doing a master's in media literacy, says she likes elegant watches, maybe two-tone, and leans away from sport pieces with big cases. Her advice for the day: learn French, "a very beautiful language." I can't improve on that, and I've tried. Take it as a small-sample survey, but a consistent one: two-tone and smaller cases keep coming up.

## The Market: The Independent Brand Rant

Now the long one. Brutal Watch Collection ran a stream called "Degenerate Horology Independent Brands are 100 HARAM," in which the hosts browse a Japanese collector's Instagram and argue about the indie bubble. The title is a bit, so don't read it as a ruling.

On the market, the key claim is that Richard Mille is the only independent brand that has "made it big" and that others are not guaranteed to follow. People are speculating that F.P. Journe is next, but the host worries that the bubble is already mainstream. He says he can't evaluate it because there's no price history, and brands are "popping up like mushrooms." He compares it to the stock market: "usually at the top of the market, that's where the taxi drivers start giving you stock tips." That is the sharpest line of the day. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun," and every bubble in history agrees with that verse.

The Ferengi have a line for this too, Rule of Acquisition #264: "It's not the size of your planet, but it's income, that matters." The stream's version is that you can love a brand all you want, but the aftermarket is where the real planet lives.

On specific numbers, the hosts say Groeneveld's entry-level steel watches run about $40,000 to $50,000, with a huge waiting list and aftermarket prices over retail. A Japanese independent with a silver dial and engraved Roman numerals comes in around $50,000, 37 millimeters (about 1.5 inches), at roughly ten pieces a year. They call that "very reasonable." They also say some Romain Gauthier pieces mostly "break even" on the aftermarket. Treat all of those as the hosts' spoken estimates, not verified quotes. One host also cites a figure of 600 grand for an aftermarket Simon Brette, which he says he doesn't understand at all. I don't understand it either, and I've read the transcript twice.

The best moment: a host spends the whole stream bashing Ferdinand Berthoud, then sees a good photograph of one and says he might take it "off the haram list." The brand didn't change, the lighting did. That is every watch purchase ever made.

## Collecting Advice and Industry Notes

The same stream gets to a trend a writer called "the 80s are coming back": smaller, classically sized, two-tone, more dress watch, less stainless steel sports watch. The host says his channel has been saying this for six years.

On strap advice, the stream pans a $400 off-the-rack rubber strap, saying a sailcloth strap suits a sporty watch better and that a bespoke one might only cost about $100 more. There's also a funny aside on a mechanical step-tracking complication. A host wants one, but the other host says the thinnest add-on sensors he's seen are about 0.09 inch thick, which doubles the height of a dress watch. He compares it to "wearing a fanny pack in a bespoke suit." Bad idea, great analogy.

Finally, the hosts spend a while on celebrity-backed brands and revived dead names. They call the wave of revival brands "a whole grift going on" and note that people buy an old brand or start a new one now. I'd call that fair, since the industry has more revivals than a church tent.

In the grim darkness of the far future there is only war, and in the grim darkness of watch collecting there is only 30 meters of water resistance. Don't swim with the Patek, Little Mister.
