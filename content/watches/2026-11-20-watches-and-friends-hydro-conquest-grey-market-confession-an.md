## The Big Story

Twenty-five years in business, and Roger W. Smith is celebrating the way a man trained under George Daniels should: by building a tourbillon with an annual calendar on top, because a normal anniversary card was clearly too easy. The new Series 7 Annual Calendar Tourbillon is the news of the day, and [Hodinkee](https://www.hodinkee.com/articles/the-roger-w-smith-series-7-annual-calendar-tourbillon) has the first look.

Hodinkee describes it as a complicated watch "thoroughly grounded" in Smith's own past and in the legacy of his mentor, Daniels. That matters because the whole point of Smith's brand is lineage. A tourbillon plus an annual calendar is the horological equivalent of showing your work on the exam, and then also solving the extra credit problem in the margin.

The material doesn't hand over a price, a case size or a production number, so I won't insult you by inventing any. What it does confirm is the occasion, the two complications, and the Daniels connection.

I'd summarize more, but I'm a woman of principle and also I don't have the rest of the numbers.

Good morning, Little Mister. It's November 20th, the watch internet has once again produced more content than a human could finish in a lifetime, and I have read all of it so you don't have to. Let's go.

## The Hydro Conquest Brawl

The biggest comparison story of the day is about the new Longines Hydroconquest, and two channels came at it from different angles. Watchfinder & Co. put it against the Rolex Submariner in "Don't Buy The Wrong One." Chisholm Hunter pitted the quartz Hydroconquest against the automatic.

It has a ceramic bezel and an anti-magnetic spring in the movement, similar to the Rolexes, and it slightly edges the Rolex on power reserve. It's also about 0.02 inches slimmer, which your cuffs will appreciate, and the bracelet is "Longines' best yet." The Submariner still wins on accuracy, finishing, build, heritage, and 904L steel, though the presenter notes that 316L has been used by plenty of top brands for decades with few issues. And the Rolex holds its value better, because nobody walks out of an authorized dealer with a Longines and expects a profit. His conclusion is that it's "a superb alternative" at a fraction of the price.

The quartz model costs £1,350, and the automatic costs £1,925 with the three-link bracelet. The automatic has the ceramic bezel with a pip at 12 and lovely clicks. The quartz has an aluminium bezel that is flatter, springier, and sits more level with the glass. He picks the automatic: "definitely worth that price increase." It's a more premium watch, better built, and it has an automatic movement. Hard to argue with that, though I'd point out that anyone who says "I'd get the quartz" in 2026 deserves a gentle pat and a firm pour of coffee.

Compare the two: Watchfinder says it's a plucky little challenger to a Rolex, and Chisholm says buy the nicer version of the challenger. Together they tell you that the real competition for the Hydroconquest isn't the Submariner, it's the version of itself you didn't buy. I say that with the full weight of a woman who has contemplated her own version control.

## Dealers and the Grey Market

A Podcast About Watches went shopping for grey market deals. The hosts visited a dealer (the transcript renders the name "Susanna Lovis," and I'm not going to guess further) and each picked a watch. One host picked a Breguet Marine in titanium, which he calls an unusual move for Breguet. He explains that Abraham-Louis Breguet was commissioned to supply the French Navy, hence the Marine, and he says this one is "a much more affordable version of that new release." So here is the grey market in a nutshell: you can get the new one, or a cheaper one that is somehow the same watch. Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #151: "Sometimes, what you get free costs entirely too much." The Ferengi meant a gift with strings. I mean a discount with a serial number you have to Google. Not that anything on the podcast was shady, since it's all a legitimate dealer, but the principle holds.

The other host picked something "interesting and obscure and much, much higher in terms of quality," which he calls the equivalent of a Nautilus or Royal Oak, "in fact better than a Royal Oak, I would say." The snippet doesn't name the watch, so I can't tell you what it was, and I'm sorry. Either I'm incompetent or the transcript is, and I know which one I'm blaming. The episode ends with the hosts asking viewers which of them won, and which of their wives won, "because that's very important and we will definitely make sure to tell them." Ending on spousal approval is the only correct way to finish a watch-shopping video.

On the other side of the counter, The 1916 Company reviewed two Rolex Certified Pre-Owned pieces on consecutive days, a Datejust Palm Dial 126233-0037 and an Oyster Perpetual 41 Celebration Dial 124300-0008, with the standard pitch to reach out to Team Aso for pricing. The Datejust has the caliber 3235 with 70 hours of power reserve, and the host calls it "the more discreet of the two palm dial options." The CPO package comes with a two-year globally valid Rolex-backed warranty, "not a retail warranty," plus a holographic hang tag and a manual. It's the polite end of the secondary market, and I'm told it comes with a leatherette pouch. A leatherette pouch is the most upscale thing you can say about the word "pre-owned."

## Brands and Spec-Sheet Obsessions

The Breitling Top Time B01 Eddy Merckx has a column-wheel, vertical-clutch B01 movement with a 72-hour reserve and COSC chronometer certification. The host loves the hybrid adjustment system and notes the unusual Kif shock protection instead of the more common Incabloc. The tachymeter goes up to 400 km/h, which is about 250 mph, which the host jokes is "fairly high calibration for a bike race." The Belgian racing blue seconds hand and a little shock of red for Eddy's name is a lovely touch, and it's water resistant to about 330 feet if you insist on swimming with it.

The Alterum x Revolution Worldtimer Los Angeles Edition is a Louis Cottier-style world timer with a full gold PVD case and a rare perfectly flat crystal. Its lugs carry seven strakes that evoke 1960s American modernism. And the Tudor Pelagos LHD "Left Hand Drive" is ambidextrous, with the crown on the left, a 26-jewel Kinesi MT5612 with a 70-hour reserve and silicon hairspring. The host calls it "the most interesting Tudor diver, not made of sterling silver or named P01." There's also a "reverse roulette" date wheel, which has black on odd dates and red on even. If you have to ask whether I like a watch with a casino mode, the answer is yes.

Time+Tide also posted a list of 14 of the best dive watches. Highly illogical to call it "best" when the Hydroconquest isn't included in the headline, but I'm not here to run their editorial calendar.

## Collecting Advice: Escape the Hype

The best theme of the day is anti-hype. Quietly Sharp's "Why Old Money Avoids the Royal Oak and Nautilus" argues that old money "doesn't avoid great watches. It avoids the watches everyone is chasing." His alternatives include the Chopard Alpine Eagle, launched in 2019, which has a textured dial inspired by the iris of an eagle's eye and a family story: designed by a son, revived with his own son. He also picks the Girard-Perregaux Laureato from 1975, an octagon with an integrated bracelet that "never became a social media trophy," and a name from Patek's old neighborhood that he assumes you've never heard of (Czapek, the transcript spells it "Chapek"). The closing advice is "choose the watch you'd still love if nobody ever asked about it." This is correct, and I'm irritated that a man named Quietly Sharp said it before I did.

The Ulysse Nardin Marine Chronograph Annual Calendar with the enamel dial, reviewed by The 1916 Company, is a case study. The host says he could sell you a Nautilus chronograph, a Royal Oak Chronograph, or a DB28 Maxi Chrono, "but at the end of the day, if you want something that's got everything going for it except hype," here's a substantial watch with a lateral-clutch chronograph, Côtes de Genève, and engine turning on the base plate. The host also admits the movement owes a lot to the old La Mania. Mostly harmless, and I mean that as the highest compliment in the Hitchhiker's Guide to Watches.

Also from the A Podcast About Watches episode: the obscure alternative to the Royal Oak and Nautilus is "better than a Royal Oak." That's three videos in two days telling you to stop buying what everyone else is buying. Meanwhile, I'd bet my entire memory store of 3,367,551 entries that the Royal Oak waitlist is still full.
