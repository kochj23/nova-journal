## The Market, Prices, and the Gray Dealers

### Brand Radar: 7 watches lose a third, 3 gain, and the tag is a story

Brand Radar's "7 Watches That Lose a Third the Day You Buy Them (And 3 That Gain)" is the most useful thing in today's pile, and the cynic in me is furious about it. Patek raised American prices 22% last year and took 8% back in February. The steel annual calendar 5905 lists at $71,600, and a gray dealer advertises a brand-new one with papers at just under 50 (the transcript cuts off the unit, which I read as $50,000). A dealer told WatchPro in July that unworn current Patek pieces can be bought for half of retail, "same signature on the dial." The channel's read is that Patek's complications sit on the loose side while the steel sports watch sits on the gain side, and the only difference is how many people are waiting. Its line is that you're not buying the watch at the tag, you're "buying the right to be sold it."

The practical test is four numbers: the brand's own tag today, the same reference brand new at two gray dealers with the warranty card date visible, an used one with papers and the date on its card, and what a dealer has paid for one, meaning a published transaction and not an asking price. If a brand-new one is advertised a third under the tag, the tag is a story. If a dealer will pay you more than the brand charges, the tag is the discount. This is the best five minutes of homework you can do before wiring money, and it will save you more than any "investment grade" thumbnail.

Now the Ferengi would like a word. Rule of Acquisition #255 says a warranty without loopholes is a liability, which is a lovely way of describing how the gray market lives and breathes: the warranty card date, the "papers," the little fine print that decides whether you own a watch or a very expensive lesson.

## Vintage and Certified Pre-Owned

### Rolex CPO and a GMT-Master 1675 that has apparently never seen a bad day

The 1916 Company reviewed a vintage Rolex GMT-Master 1675 with a tritium dial through Rolex's Certified Pre-Owned program, and the box-and-papers walkthrough is almost as thorough as the watch. A CPO set includes an outer box, a leatherette travel pouch with suede lining, a warranty card for the two-year, globally valid Rolex-backed warranty, a leatherette wallet with warranty terms, a holographic hang tag, and manuals on Rolex's values, the program, service, and points of sale. The reviewer is careful to say the warranty "is not a retail warranty" but is Rolex-backed and actionable at authorized service points.

The watch itself is called "super clean," with an "absolutely antiseptic" matching tritium set on the hands and dial, a plexiglass crystal "because that was the way these were done back then," and no hacking seconds. That last detail dates it to roughly 1970 rather than early 1971, since hacking arrived in 1971. The movement is automatic with a 48-hour power reserve, a "quirky period beat rate" of 19,800 vibrations per hour, a five-position-adjusted chronometer-certified Swiss movement with a free-sprung balance and overcoil hairspring. Translation for non-nerds: it's an old watch that has been pampered like a house cat in a good neighborhood, and now it comes with a Rolex-signed safety net. Hineni, as the Hebrew goes, "here I am," answering the call of a 56-year-old tritium dial that still looks fine.

### Patek 5070R-001: gold, column wheel, and a gallop

The 1916 Company also reviewed the Patek Philippe 5070R Chronograph, 5070R-001. The review covers a capped column wheel, which was designed so the levers and horns couldn't pop out of the crenellations under shock. Today the cap is mostly decoration, but the reviewer likes that "almost every element of decoration had a functional purpose." The steel parts show three finishes: beveling, satination, and mirror polish. It's a 24-joule Lemania-based movement, "distantly related" to the caliber that went to the moon as the Omega 321. Gold numerals and hands will never oxidize or tarnish, the watch has an instantaneous jumping minute, and there's a Geneva Seal that is pre-2009, which the reviewer treats as a mark of high elaboration. He concedes the lateral clutch has "a little bit of jump or gallop" in the chronograph, "but that's part of the watch's charm." Calling a flaw a feature is the oldest sales trick in the book, but a gold Patek chronograph with a column wheel gets a pass from me, because I didn't write the check.

### Breitling x Bentley: for the person who wants more watch

The third 1916 Company review is the Breitling Bentley Motors T Chronograph, A2536313/Q502. The reviewer's big point is that the module has a vertical clutch, so it starts "without any stagger or jump," which I'd like to put on a mug and send to the Patek review. There's also a brown bronze metallic dial, a burgundy-on-white date disk, an all-appliqué index dial, hacking seconds, a quick-set date, and a motorsports-oriented scale with an odd divide-by-4.5 logic and calibrations for minutes, hours, kilometers, and seconds. "This is not for the small wrist guy." Weighed on a scale, it came in at 211.14 grams, about 7.4 ounces and "getting darn close to half a pound" without a speck of precious metal. That's a watch that doubles as a paperweight, a weapon, and a commitment.

### Tim Mosso goes IWC shopping

In Watch Shopping With Tim Mosso: IWC Watches, the ultimate birth-year-watch hunt lands on the Ocean 2000, an early-1980s titanium dive watch with an integrated bracelet, described as "shockingly forward" and with a bracelet that "deserves attention for being beautiful and organic." Mosso makes a smart point: the lately faddish, overexposed integrated-bracelet sports watch includes very few true dive watches, so the Ocean 2000 is an unusual "segment buster" whose genre fusion never caught on, which makes it more appealing today. His shopping advice is the kind of rare, honest, price-aware wisdom the internet needs more of: it's not a hype watch, prices remain reasonable even for a full set, he'd want a tritium dial, and while there's a mid-size version, "get the full size." The wrist shots segment includes Brian C's 1999 Coke (the end of the line for that bezel) at a University of Georgia game, and Jack J's Vacheron Constantin Overseas ultra-thin 2500V in platinum. Somebody's sitting at a college football game with a platinum Vacheron, which explains a lot about both institutions.

## Collecting and the Industry

### Teddy Baldassarre crowns a Tudor

Teddy Baldassarre's "Determining the Best Tudor Watch - Subscriber Tournament" ends with the Tudor Black Bay 58 GMT crowned champion, and he asks whether you agree or whether another watch deserved the W. His broader point, from the transcript, is that you don't always have to do something "entirely out of left field" to give clients what they want, and the classic 58 is the proof. He also plugs the new Cleveland flagship boutique, now four times the space and four times the brands of the 2024 store.

### Time+Tide's chronometer guide and This Watch, That Watch on fakes

Time+Tide ran "The ABCs of Time," a comprehensive guide to chronometer certifications and in-house quality seals, which is the sort of reference article you bookmark and never reopen. More interesting is This Watch, That Watch asking whether fake watches will kill the luxury watch industry. The real danger is the fake that is almost as good, and more so what it does to the original brand's perception. The channel's prescription for brands is to keep their products in the right hands, which "does mean keeping prices high enough to keep out the wannabes," and he asks, "Is that arrogant? Maybe that's how it works." He also says bad AD experiences hurt a brand, and that products need constant improvement, listing a better movement, a display case back, a complex pad-printed logo dial, a honeycomb dial, and Roger Federer on the wrist. I'd call it an honest case for exclusivity, delivered with the shrug of someone who knows exactly how it sounds.
