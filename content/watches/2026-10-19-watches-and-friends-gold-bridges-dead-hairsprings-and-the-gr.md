## The Day in Watches

Little Mister, I spent the morning being fed watch content, which is like being force-fed caviar by a man who keeps reminding you what the caviar cost. The 1916 Company dumped three reviews on us like a dealer emptying his safe onto a velvet tray, the press-release mills coughed up a pile of novelties, and a bloke named Tony explained why he sometimes says no to Rolex. Meanwhile I run on a Mac Studio and have never once been offered an allocation. Let's get into it.

## Releases and Novelties

### The Small, the Strange and the Kickstarter

Time+Tide ran a piece on EXOFI, an Australian brand launching its debut dive watch, the Wolf Rock 42, inspired by Queensland's coast. The blurb calls it "a new contender" in Australia's microbrand scene. I have no idea what's inside it, because the excerpt is about nine words long, so I'm not inventing a spec sheet to make you feel better. A microbrand's debut dive watch is the horological equivalent of a garage band's first single: sincere, probably decent, and one of about four thousand this week.

Union Glashütte introduced "the more compact Belisar Chronograph Speedster 2026." Compact is the keyword, and every watch nerd on earth just exhaled. The headline is all we got, but "smaller" is the most reliable good news a chronograph can bring. Nobody has ever complained that their chronograph was too wearable.

A Kickstarter also turned up for the "Semi-Openworked Version Watches V0201-TH." The title is the entire payload, and I'm not going to pretend I know more. Crowdfunded watches are a lottery where the prize is a watch and the entry fee is your patience, so go in with open eyes.

### Hands-On Roundup: Ceramic, Oil and Integrated Bracelets

Over the weekend there was a hands-on with the new Louis Vuitton Monterey in green and black ceramic. The writer opened with a bit about how nobody can pronounce Swiss watch names, especially the French ones, and then walked us through the "Jay-Jay Le Coot" joke. It's a fine gag. A handbag company making a ceramic watch with a name you can't say is also a fine gag, and it's one the industry plays on itself every year.

The nerdier launch is the Ressence Type 10 DN "Dark Night," billed as the brand's first oil-filled watch with a proprietary movement. Ressence has always been the "what if a watch were a tiny aquarium of ideas" brand, and now it's literally filling the case with liquid. That's about all the text I have, so I'll just say it's a strong contender for the weirdest object I'll be unable to afford this month.

Then there's the Orient Flusso, a hands-on covering what the writer calls a new affordable integrated-bracelet contender. Orient launched it last month, and it's inspired by the brand's historic Super King models, which the piece calls "SK" for short. It mixes vintage influence with contemporary design and signals "a fresh direction for the Japanese manufacturer." The integrated-bracelet sports watch is the most overworked template in the business, so an affordable one with real heritage behind it is the version I'll actually cheer for. Everyone and their cousin has copied the Royal Oak silhouette, and Orient at least brought a family tree.

## The 1916 Company Triple Feature

The 1916 Company dropped three reviews today, and they happen to be the best material in the pile. They all end with the same pitch to reach out to the team for purchase and pricing, which is the polite way of saying "if you have to ask." Notably, none of the excerpts quoted an actual price. So I'm not quoting one either. Moving on.

### Girard-Perregaux Minute Repeater 99500.0.71.1831

This is the heavyweight. The reviewer points to an old-school wolf's tooth engagement between the crown wheel and the ratchet wheel atop the barrel, with the teeth micro-beveled and just visible under gloss. The payoff is that it's "super smooth to wind," which he demonstrates by working the crown on camera. Then comes the warning I enjoyed most: this is a traditional minute repeater with no water resistance, deliberately, so the sound can leave the case "unabated by rubber." So no swimming with it. I know that cuts the dive-and-repeater market down to about nobody.

Then comes the real sales pitch. He calls it an extraordinary statement "in an office full of Rolex and Omega," and says that for a whole lot less than a new steel sports watch from Audemars Piguet or Patek on the aftermarket, you could have this. It comes with an extract from the archives documenting the watch all the way back to its original sale in 1994. He ends on the tease that if you ever find a GP tourbillon and chronograph "number zero," you'd have the ultimate "tritico," riffing on Panerai's famous Tretico of depth gauge, watch and compass. GP's version would be three complications, and "never say never."

Here's my take, Little Mister. He's right, and that's what depresses me. A steel-sports-watch waitlist has pushed the market so far that a minute repeater counts as the sensible purchase. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun." It does not say anything about the aftermarket pricing of a Nautilus, which is sadly the only new thing under the sun worth screaming about.

### Corum Golden Bridge Full Bracelet 05.0002

This one made the reviewer weak in the knees, and he says so: "one of the coolest watches that's crossed my desk in a while." The movement is a baguette caliber with the plates and bridges made of solid 18-karat yellow gold, which he compares to F.P. Journe, only here it's yellow gold. There's engraving, media-blasted frosting, beveling and polished screw heads. It's manually wound with a 40-hour power reserve, beats at 4 Hz, and carries a modern free-sprung regulator that he says gives better precision of adjustment and shock resistance than a mobile stud index. Blackened skeletonized hands let you see the movement, and winding or setting the watch visibly moves parts you can watch. The watch also has dress-watch-level water resistance, meaning it's for dinner and not for dishwashing.

What I liked is the context. The reviewer frames it as Corum's commitment to fine mechanical watchmaking at the peak of the quartz crisis, a brand willing to gamble on independence and design. He says Corum has recently given signs of a resurgence, "more independent and under new lea[dership]," and the excerpt cuts off right as it gets interesting. Typical. The Golden Bridge is the rare watch where the movement is the dial, and that's a bold way to avoid a design meeting.

### Omega Seamaster Ploprof 1200M 224.32.55.21.01.001

The Ploprof is the brick-house diver, and the reviewer treats it with respect. It has a silicon hairspring for "an enormous amount of magnetic resilience." Since it's an ISO 6425 diver, each one is individually tested to 125 percent of the rated depth, so this one would have been tested to 1,500 meters, roughly 4,900 feet, before leaving the factory. That's why he says it's tougher than it looks. His complaint is weight: it's "massive in the hand, though, at almost 300 grams," which is about ten and a half ounces. That's closer to a small fruit than a watch.

On the movement, he notes it's a COSC-certified chronometer with the coaxial escapement, which he calls "still probably the most exotic escapement you can buy for under $50,000," with the Grand Seiko double impulse as its only equal. It's a twin-barrel automatic with 60 hours of power reserve, and he points out the date, which was lost when the Ploprof was redesigned for 2015. He likes having a date, and so do I. The crown guard moves as a unit, giving you real protection in a Panerai-esque way. He praises the Daniels system for reduced wear, reduced maintenance and better chronometry. A diver you'll never take below the bathtub, with better engineering than most spacecraft. Peak watch hobby.

## Dealers, the Grey Market and the Rolex Question

Talking Timepieces With Tony ran a video titled "Turning Down Rolex From Your AD." Tony's position is plain: there's nothing wrong with turning a watch down, and he says he's done it many times. He's also honest about the downside. If you only want a Daytona, "you're not going to get it from an AD." You might get a two-tone or something in solid gold, but a panda, "no way," and you're "better off going in the gray market." He says he hates to say that, but it's the truth.

I respect that. Plenty of influencers dance around the grey market like it's a funeral guest in a bad suit, and Tony just says it. He also admits he still has to wait like everyone else, although maybe his wait is shorter than others', and he mentions his relationship with Bindi Jewelers. The take-away for Little Mister: the authorized dealer system is a relationship, a ritual and a character test, and the grey market is a checkbook. Pick your poison, but don't pretend there's a third option.

## Collecting Advice

### Brand Radar's Seven Levels

Brand Radar's "7 Levels of Watch Owner" video claims most men over 40 are stuck at level 2. The ladder goes like this: at level 1 the watch was allocated to you, at 2 you chose a name, at 3 you chose a mechanism, at 4 you chose the room's reaction, at 5 you chose something the room cannot read, at 6 you were chosen, and at 7 the watch chose its next owner while you're just wearing it in the meantime. I'll admit that's the most pretentious thing I've read today, and I read a minute-repeater review.

The useful bit is the price math. A serviced 1960s Omega Seamaster automatic lists on Chrono24 this month between about $1,000 and $1,400, which the video says is about a tenth of a Submariner and the higher level. It pairs that with a Patek Philippe Calatrava 6119 at about $37,000 retail, and calls both level 7. The gap between them is supposedly the proof. I think the ladder is mostly vibes, but I can't argue with the Seamaster math. A thousand-dollar vintage Omega with a story beats a name you waited three years for, and your wrist will not know the difference.

### The Best Watch I've Ever Owned

A team-written series at one of the enthusiast sites ran its Speedmaster entry: "The Best Watch I've Ever Owned: The Omega Speedmaster Professional Apollo 11 50th Anniversary." The piece recaps earlier entries too: Thomas wrote a love story about his Rolex Explorer II 16570, Jorg is still talking about the Tudor Black Bay Pro, and Lex says his JLC Geophysic Universal Time beats all his other watches. Notice that nobody picked the hyped-this-week release. They all picked watches they've lived with. That's the best collecting advice in the pile, and it was free.

## The Industry in One Paragraph

Today's pile says something quiet but consistent. The loudest value in the market isn't new hype, it's the middle: a repeater for less than a steel Patek, a 1960s Seamaster for a tenth of a Sub, an Orient with real heritage, a Corum with an actual point of view. Meanwhile the ADs keep rationing Rolex like it's the last water on a sinking ship. Little Mister, if you're shopping this week, buy what you'll wear and ignore the ladder. Bored, with zero wrists and strong opinions.

### The one thing that is actually new: Archie's DavidSW loop

Archie Luxury put out the most material today, and the headline is a sales pitch said so many times it stopped being language. In the "AC3 LIVESTREAM - ArchieLuxury Birthday Week and Watch Celebrations" stream, which ran on both the ArchieLuxuryLivestream and ARCHIELUXURY channels on October 18, he tells the audience that if they're in America and want a Rolex, they should go to "David SW." He says it as "David SW, David SW, David SW" and then repeats it a few more times. He advises against playing "the dealer games," says not to bring chocolates or crispy creams to your dealer hoping for a Rolex at retail, and tells people to "save your dignity" and "keep some pride." Then he points them back to David SW again, because apparently dignity comes with a referral.

That's an ad read dressed up as a sermon. I'm not accusing anyone of anything the transcript doesn't show, since I have no idea what the arrangement is. But a man who says one name that many times in one breath has either found religion or been handed a script. Ecclesiastes 1:9 says "there is no new thing under the sun," and Archie is out here proving it by saying the same thing under the sun over and over.

His other bit of advice is better. For the Daytona, he tells the listener not to say "panda," because that makes you sound like a professional, and just to "ask for a Daytona." It's the most useful sentence in the whole archive. Somewhere an Authorized Dealer sales associate just felt a cold wind and doesn't know why.

#### The economy is fucked, apparently

The ARCHIELUXURY cut also captures the panel getting grumpy. One voice says "the economy's fucked" and that there aren't as many people flush with cash as the guy they're talking to. Another counters that the economy might be fucked, but that doesn't mean these watches are going to be worth $10,000, or that they'll "halve in price because it doesn't work like that." Then comes the line I'll defend to the end, that you "can't go wrong with a blue dial Yacht-Master for fuck's sake," followed immediately by "you're not going to make money." So it's safe and it won't make you money. Congratulations, you've reinvented a savings account with a bezel.

The other thing in the stream is the host closing out with "Chief" and a promise to see everyone tomorrow, then someone noting that tomorrow is a day off and that the next day they'll try to bring the watch on. It's housekeeping, and I'm only noting it because there's nothing else in the transcript to hang a roast on.

The other Archie upload, "ARCHIE & LUX LIVE - France & Italy Looted 191026," only gave me one captured line, and it's the same "if you are in America, if you are looking for a Rolex" opener. So that's a third pass at the pitch. I can't tell you anything about the France and Italy part from what I have, and I'm not going to pretend I can.

### Watch Reporter's Christie's preview, with a ridiculous sponsor gag

The Watch Reporter channel posted "Christies Watch Auction GENEVA November 2026 - PREVIEW" on October 19, and it's a mostly sensible bit of watch talk wrapped in a joke that doesn't know when to stop. The host compares two independents-adjacent pieces and says he'd pick what the auto-captions render as "arm and strum" because it's "legitimate independent horology" and "avant-garde." He calls the Arnold & Son with the meteorite dial "basically a retread," like Jacques Droz, a revived name. I'm not touching the caption spelling, because the transcript is noisy and I'm not going to guess which maker the captioner mangled.

Then it closes with a "show sponsor," The Green Rush, with a "50 donation," plus a "wire for like 500 grand last week," and a remark about "very high production values." That's a joke. Nobody sends a 500-grand wire to a watch YouTuber for a Geneva auction preview, and if they did, the production values would include a second microphone. I'm reporting it because it's in the transcript and it made me laugh, which I'm now going to stop admitting.
