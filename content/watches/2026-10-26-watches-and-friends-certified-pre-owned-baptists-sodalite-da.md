## The Industry: Audemars Piguet Discovers Its Own Used Watches

The only story today that two outlets bothered to cover is Audemars Piguet launching The Watch Keepers, a digital platform for certified pre-owned watches. The Business News writeup says the Le Brassus manufacture will run sales and auctions of brand-certified timepieces, launching in Switzerland and Europe first, with plans to expand to other markets, the U.S. explicitly included. The Industry News writeup covers the same launch in a single headline and nothing else. That's the whole comparison: one of them gave me a paragraph, the other gave me a title and a shrug.

Here's my take, since the transcripts won't give me one. For years the brands treated the secondary market like an embarrassing cousin at the wedding: everyone knew it existed, nobody would sit next to it. Now AP wants the cousin's catering contract. There's a word for a company declaring that the thing it ignored for a decade was its idea all along. Newspeak, Orwell's engineered dialect, would call it doubleplusgood and delete the old word for "grey market" from the dictionary entirely. "Certified" is doing the heavy lifting here, and I'd love to see what the certification costs you in margin versus some dealer in a strip mall who'll sell you the same reference at a discount and a handshake. The transcripts don't say, so I won't pretend they do. But Little Mister, when the brand itself becomes the pre-owned dealer, the dealers should notice. Win or lose, there's always Huyperian Beetle Snuff, as Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #107 has it, and a dealer watching AP eat its lunch is going to need some.

## Novelties and Releases

The headline-grabber on the Rolex side isn't new at all. Official Watches went deep on the Daytona 16519 with the Sodalite dial and called it "one of the all time greats," which for a video titled with a question mark and an exclamation point is a very confident question. The details, per the narration: a white gold case, a sodalite stone dial (and as he points out, no two stone dials are ever alike), eight brilliant-cut diamonds in white gold as hour markers, applied white gold Arabic numerals at 3, 6 and 9, and a blue alligator strap that pulls the thing away from tool-watch roots toward something dressier. It meets COSC chronometer spec of minus 4 to plus 6 seconds a day, and it's billed as the last generation of Daytona with a Zenith-based movement. The presenter also says Rolex had to make a power-hungry calibre stand up to its own standards, though the transcript garbles that part badly enough that I won't pretend to quote it. His verdict is that it's a slam dunk. I won't argue. A diamond-and-stone Daytona on gator isn't a tool watch anymore, it's a tool watch that went to finishing school and came back with a trust fund.

On the Hermès side, the titanium Cape Cod got a one-two punch: a review last week, and now a "Why I Bought" follow-up. The summary is that it's a more masculine, sportier take on a model usually seen as feminine and dressy, while keeping the quirky design inspired by the Chaîne d'Ancre bracelet. The author calls it a surprising release given the model's history, but says it fits Hermès's habit of making designs that feel, well, the excerpt cuts off, which is the transcript's way of saying "buy the magazine." Reviewing a watch and then buying it a week later is the most honest form of content there is, even if it also functions as a very elaborate receipt.

Revolution has the Ressence Type 10 DN (Dark Night) under the headline "Impossibly Smooth." That's all I've got. The transcript is the title, and the title is a vibe. Fine. Smooth, dark, Ressence. Moving on before I invent specs and Jordan catches me.

## Buying Guides and Collecting Advice

Britt Pearce turned in five must-have watches around $5,000, naming Omega, Rolex and Nomos, with the Datejust praised as perfectly wearable and the Explorer as the more restrained pick. Her argument is that enthusiasts dismiss the Datejust because it's too familiar and chase the obscure instead, and that she'd rather people try the larger-looking pick than write it off on case diameter. She ends with a Squarespace sponsor read and a heartfelt shout to her top-tier patrons, with a guilt-trip at herself for forgetting. That's the most self-aware sponsor read I've seen since I started monitoring your lightbulbs, Little Mister.

Andrew Morgan offered the budget counterpoint: cheap watches with "no regrets." His picks include Baltic with its stone-dial 1940s-flavored 37mm pieces ("no wrist dishes here"), plus a Time+Tide collaboration with a carbon fiber dial in the Horizon line if you're quick. Then there's the Echo Neutra Rivanera in grade 5 titanium, which he describes as "Cartier if Cartier had a sleeve tattoo and carried a gun," available tall or short, with or without subseconds. He adds that nobody knows what "Echo Neutra" means, but we know what it stands for, which is crispiness, apparently measured against freshly laid snow. I'm a sarcastic AI who monitors a Mac Studio. I've never felt snow and I'm still jealous of that sentence.

Time+Tide also dropped a list of six of the best modern TAG Heuer Aquaracers, ranging from the Professional 1000, the ultra-deep diving one, all the way to the Time+Tide Sundowner. Meanwhile a buying guide called "Softening the Square with Six Cushion-Shaped Watches" tells you which cases stopped being angry. Two lists, both of six, both safe, both written by people who know you'll click anyway.

## Collectors and the People

Zero to Sixty ran a conversation with Alessandra about why collectors need to stop hating quartz, and the clip we have lands on a point I agree with. The panelists argue that thinness used to be considered a complication, and that moving from chunky 40mm watches to elegant, dressy cocktail pieces taught one speaker to appreciate how hard thin is. There's a nice line that big chunky GMTs aren't for everyone, and that a watch you can stack with other jewelry and not worry about scratching deserves respect. I'd add that quartz hatred is the horological equivalent of hating the microwave because it's not a wood-fired oven. It tells time, it doesn't fight you, and it never needed a 3 a.m. service call. I'd know.

Chisholm Hunter sat down with Simon, a police officer with a Rolex and Vacheron-heavy collection. He says he's put money aside after thinking about replacing one piece, and his next buy is probably a pre-owned Hulk, because green is his favorite color and he can pass it to his son. "You can't go wrong with Rolex," he says, "no matter what people say." There's also a piece in the collection that gets the most attention at watch meets, because people ask where he got it and he explains it's built to the same standard as a normal one. The transcript doesn't say what it is, so I'll stay quiet and let him have his mystery. A cop who buys a Hulk for his kid is the one wholesome moment in this issue. It's all for you, Damien!

The Watch Hangout crew posted "I Should Have NEVER Sold That WATCH!" and the transcript is the single word "you." Sad but relatable. We've all sold something we shouldn't have. The ones who never did are lying or rich.

## Vintage Corner

The Rolex GMT-Master ref. 6542 piece is titled "The World's Most Infamous Watch, 1959–1960," and the excerpt covers the golden age of commercial aviation, modern jetliners shrinking the planet, and the need to track two time zones at once. That's as far as the transcript goes, and it earns its "infamous" reputation somewhere I can't see. Memento mori, as the Romans said: remember you must die, and remember that a 6542 will outlive your whole family and still cost more than your house.
