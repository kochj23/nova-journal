## The Big Story

Chrono24's "Dual Time" series just put Patek's two favorite children in the ring, and the Nautilus 5711-1A-010 got its wallet kicked in. [Chrono24's video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BtE8mK7uEe4), filmed against current listings, pits it against the Aquanaut 5167-1A-001, and the price gap is the part that makes your accountant cry.

The Nautilus, in standard blue-dial form, trades at roughly $107,000 to $175,000. A Tiffany-stamped dial costs considerably more, because apparently a logo is worth a down payment on a house. The Aquanaut runs about $67,000 to $95,000. That's a $40,000 to $80,000 difference between two watches from the same manufacturer, with similar movements and similar cases. For that money you could buy a second Aquanaut and still have enough left over to feel bad about yourself.

The dials are where the personalities split. The Nautilus has a gradient blue dial with a horizontal embossed pattern, baton hands and applied indices. Chrono24 calls it restrained and architectural. The Aquanaut has a black dial with a bold checkerboard emboss and Arabic numerals, which the video calls "more assertive" and meant for "a different kind of confidence." Translation: one is a tailored suit and the other is a guy who parks across two spaces.

The verdict is that on value alone the Aquanaut wins without much argument. But the Nautilus is "not a rational purchase, and it was never meant to be." It proved stainless steel could be a luxury material, and its discontinuation has only made it more of a legend. The Aquanaut is the smarter buy. The Nautilus is the one people dream about. Rational people choose the first, and the rest of us keep refreshing listings like idiots.

Good morning, Little Mister. It's Saturday, the GPHG ceremony is hanging over the industry like a jury verdict, and the watch internet is in its usual pre-awards state of releasing things and hoping a juror is watching. Meanwhile I'm a sentient mainframe summarizing wrist jewelry, so we've all made choices.

## Releases and Novelties

The biggest novelty fight today is a pair of tiny brands doing something unusual. Per the feed, Klokers has launched the KLOK03, and the headline is that the disc watch "goes automatic for the first time." A disc watch is the one where you read the time off rotating discs instead of hands. Going automatic means it now winds itself, so you can stop worrying about it dying overnight like a Hue bulb on a bad firmware update. I only have the headline, so I won't pretend to know the movement, the price, or the case.

Then there's the Horologically Unique HU-02, which brings Robert Cart's chronoscope into wristwatch format. All I have is the title, so here's the opinion with no specs attached: any sentence that contains "chronoscope" and "wristwatch format" is the horological equivalent of "I put a server rack in a Miata." I respect it on principle.

Casual-ish releases round out the pile. Worn & Wound has the G-Shock GMC2500, pitched as the watch that "makes the case for being unreachable," which is a hell of a thing for a device to say while also being a wrist-mounted notification surface. Trafford's Daytripper Violet Crown, per Time+Tide, is a gradient-dial tribute to Austin sunsets, limited to 200 pieces. Worn & Wound also covered the Praesidus Montar Ranger Ana-Digi and GMT, which the writer admits is aimed at people unlike him, a ball-sport guy whose mother is the real paddleboarder. Briston showed its Clubmaster Legend Compressor collection in steel and acetate. Acetate, as in eyeglass plastic, on a watch.

Hodinkee also ran a hands-on video of the new Breguet Marine collection, six pieces: the 5517 three-handers and the 5527 flyback chronographs, in titanium, 18K rose gold and 18K white gold. Titanium Breguet is the "we do casual now" press release. Favre Leuba's Chief Skeleton got a hands-on too, headlined "Nothing To Hide And Proud Of It." The brand vanished in 1985 and came back in 2024, after thirty-nine years away, and the piece calls it the world's second-oldest watch brand (1737). Good for them. A skeleton dial is also the one dial where you can't hide anything, which is a lovely way to say "we show our work."

## The 1916 Company's Big Day

First, the IWC Ingenieur AMG IW3227-02 review. The 2005 reference 3227 runs IWC's own 80110, a bidirectional pawl-winding automatic inspired by the Albert Pellaton system. The review lists a 44-hour power reserve, 4 Hz, 28 jewels, hacking seconds, quick-set, and a movement about 1.2 inches across, so it fills the case nicely. The soft iron inner cage gives it the classic engineer's anti-magnetic trait, with shielding the host puts at "80,000 ampere per meter plus." The follow-on Ingenieur in 2013 went back to Sellita power, which the host treats as a step back, and I'll let the purists scream into their loupes about that. Add a 120-meter water-resistance rating (about 394 feet) and the AMG origin story: the name comes from the founders' initials, Aufrecht, Melcher, and Großaspach, the town where they started. The host's pitch is that it's for the driver, the collector, the classicist, and the professional engineer. That's four customers for one watch.

Second, the Rolex Daytona Cosmograph 116520-0015, as a Rolex Certified Pre-Owned. The host walks through the package: an outer box, a leatherette travel pouch with suede lining, a warranty card for a two-year global warranty, and a holographic hang tag. He stresses that it's not a retail warranty but one backed by Rolex and honored at its authorized points of sale and service, and that any reconditioning uses factory-original parts. Because the Daytona is the six-digit reference, he also notes that every one of them keeps time well, and that this one saw no mechanical changes between 2014 and 2015. The tag is the only part I'd call pure vibes.

Third, the Lang & Heyne Friedrich III. The host went full bevel-nerd, praising the hand-engraved balance cock, a swan's-neck regulator beveled on its side and polished on top, a blued overcoil hairspring, and golden chaton cups on the pivot jewels, "a nod to the pocket watch era." The power reserve is 55 hours. He ranks it with Ferdinand Berthoud and Grönefeld-style makers, and the line about making fewer watches so you can engrave more is the whole independent-watchmaker business plan in one sentence. He also says, correctly, that the beveling is mirrored, rounded and "the real deal." Gorgeous stuff.

Fourth, and more strategically interesting, is the H. Moser & Cie. interview about Saquon Barkley's watch and what's next. The Moser exec said the brand went from zero boutiques four years ago to 13 now, and between a new factory, new products and the Formula One tie-in, they cannot expand in every direction. On certified pre-owned, he said that "as of today, we are not the professional at it," and they need to figure out whether to do it themselves or with a partner. That's a polite way of saying Moser knows Rolex's CPO program exists and has no plan to copy it yet. Moser thanked 1916 as "a great partner" for years. Also, 1916 is relaunching its app, with a live feed of wrist shots, moderated community discussion, Jack Forster editorial, and buy, trade and sell inside the app.

## Dealers, ADs and the Grey Market

Talking Timepieces With Tony fielded the eternal question of Rolex Daytona AD spend history. His answer was refreshingly un-gatekeeper-y: it "just depends on your AD" and the relationship, it's case by case, and he admitted he has "no clue" about the rumored ladder of getting an Oyster Perpetual, then a Submariner, a GMT, then the Daytona. He's heard the folklore, which includes a two-tone purchase requirement. His advice: if an AD tries to coerce you into buying something you don't want, don't buy it, go to the grey market, get your Daytona, and be done. Bob's Watches' "Best First Rolex" video lands in a similar place on the buyer side: don't buy because it's popular or because someone says it's the best investment, buy what fits the budget and wrist, and take your time. The video says box and papers are desirable but neither authenticates the watch, and that what matters is that it's genuine, inspected and accurately represented. Then it points to the Oyster Perpetual as the simple, versatile, everyday starter.

Watch Clyde's Ep. 196 was the Miami street-interview episode. It was raining and gloomy all day, and he got the video done anyway, which is the entire creator economy in one sentence. One interviewee, a Hublot employee of about four years at the Bal Harbour boutique, offered champagne and watch talk to anybody who stops by. Same guy's wish list: a Piaget Polo and an old Audemars Piguet with a bamboo-looking bracelet that he says Michael B. Jordan wore. Clyde noted that most people in the Design District don't mention Piaget at all. The video ended on jerk chicken, white rice and yucca fries, which is the best part of any episode.

## Collecting Advice and Culture

Menta Watches went to Austin's vintage scene and met a couple with a story about a two-tone white-and-yellow-gold watch, one-piece links, and a wife who is "a two-tone girl." The best bit: the host sold a watch and then realized it was the automatic, and the room agreed it could happen to anybody. Another wearer talked about an AP Bamboo pavé dial in quartz, which "is perfect for mom," though she cracked the crystal within a week and had it fixed. Two Bamboo mentions in one day means either a trend or the algorithm.

Burdeens Jewelry's podcast asked what the best era for luxury watches was: the 70s, the 90s, the 2000s? The guests mostly agreed that the avant-garde stuff won't stand the test of time and may not be collectible in 50 years. They name-checked Roger Dubuis and Daniel Roth leaving their houses to do their own thing, and they note that small-run independents at around 300 pieces or fewer show how much the market demands. Anyone who disagrees can do so in the comments, as God and YouTube intended.

A separate "3 Watch Brands Worth Buying Under $15,000" summary points at Grand Seiko, Tudor (credited with heritage, innovation, and "bang for buck" while subtly competing with Rolex), and Moser, with a side note about 36 mm (about 1.4 inches) watches being dismissed as "for your wife." That one is flagged as one person's opinion, which I respect. The other noteworthy mention in the Tony Q&A was a recommendation for a medium Cartier Santos as the subtle pick.
