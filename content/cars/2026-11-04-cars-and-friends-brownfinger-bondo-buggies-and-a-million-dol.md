## The Big Story

Somebody in Columbus just paid seven figures for a Ford GT that most of us assumed had already peaked, and I'm a little offended on behalf of every depreciation chart I've ever trusted. At Barrett-Jackson's 2026 Columbus sale, a 2021 Ford GT '66 Heritage Edition hit one million dollars and became the top sale of the event. The auction crew was practically begging the room to get it over the line ("Come on back, we need one million").

The commentary is the part that matters. These GTs had slid back into six figures, and the Barrett-Jackson team called this sale "a big reset." If you own one, that's a very nice Tuesday. If you were waiting for the price to come down so you could finally buy one, it's a shitty one.

The Heritage Edition is a tribute to the Gulf-liveried Ford that won Daytona in 1966, and Barrett-Jackson's own Top 10 Ford rundown leaned on that story. The lesson is that nostalgia pays better than a 401(k).

Credit to [Barrett-Jackson's Top 10 Ford from 2026 Columbus](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1En1Fskz8tM) for the coverage.

## New Metal and Reveals

**Subdivision Auto** dropped a SEMA 2026 reveal of a 1970 Cuda nicknamed "Brownfinger." Yes, that's a Bond pun. The crew admits they have "maybe some more inappropriate nicknames," but it's "kind of a family show," so we get the clean one.

The owner says he and his dad grew up talking about cars like this, and the Cuda silhouette was the nostalgia he wanted back. Then a Cuda turned up that was supposed to be a grocery-getter for his wife. His own words: "We know how that went." Nobody has ever bought a 1970 Cuda for the groceries.

The shop does everything in-house and gave its team a shout-out for pulling the build together. The brown tone came from paint samples the shop already had, and the owner's instruction was basically "it's yours, do what you want with it." He hadn't seen the finished car in full before the reveal, which is either a bold act of trust or a hostage situation.

## Builds and Project Cars

**Kiwi Classics and Customs** is on Part 25 of the Sunbeam Alpine widebody V8 build, and I'm already tired just reading the part number. The floor mods are tacked in with a MIG now, and they'll get TIG-welded and smoothed so no joins show. The tunnel is tall enough, and the driveshaft sits high enough, that twin two-and-a-half-inch exhaust can run almost underneath the driveshaft even at full droop. The reward is more room for the seats, which in a small British roadster is a human rights issue. He cut the video short because he's got roughly a day of welding ahead. Next episode brings a pedal box, and he's teasing which side of the car it goes on. Left, right, or "middle, because I'm that guy" are all live options.

The most dramatic build story belongs to **MotorTrend Channel** and its "Wild Engineering Behind This One-of-a-Kind '32 Ford." The builder says the car is technically made from scratch. It keeps an original frame and part of the original body, but he built the rest of the body around the suspension, engine, and transmission. He fabricated race-car-style brake rotors, pistons, and seals to make vintage parts function. The brakes themselves came off a real race car that had been beaten up and, in his telling, was headed for a museum.

He never drove the car before the Grand National Roadster Show. He finished two hours before judging day, and he cried, because it took five years of stressful work. His takeaway was "never give up. Continue with what you do, what you love." Then he burned the tires on arrival, which is the correct way to end any five-year project. A tire-shredding burnout is how you tell the judges you've forgiven the car.

For contrast, **Auto Auction Rebuilds** is parked with a bricked Charger it left with the keys inside and then came back for, which is its own kind of build. The host is torn about keeping a car for Power Tour, since a fiberglass-speed-top older machine fits the spirit of the event better than the Scat Pack he also bought. His conclusion: "I can't keep everything." Little Mister, I want that tattooed somewhere visible, preferably on the garage door.

**WatchJRGo** has an E46 BMW and the headline "I Promised I Would Never Sell This BMW *Promise Broken*," which is a heck of an opening for a video about buying a different BMW. The new one is a very clean E46 in a red he describes as "signal red," a color that may be called something like "turbo frost," and he openly asks the audience for the real German name. He notes that every E46 he's bought before cost roughly a thousand dollars, maybe 1,500 at the outside, so this is "a very different world" and "like buying a new car." It needs a valve cover gasket (he could smell it on the drive home), an oil change, and the usual touch-ups.

There's also a car audio gem in there: his friend's fix for subwoofer sound not getting into the cabin was to tear out the back speakers and throw them away. That's the cheapest bass upgrade in history, and it's also how you start a divorce between you and your rear deck. Bonus points for the video being sponsored by Dutch Auto Auctions, which must be thrilled with the "it's always fun to car shop" energy.

## Junkyards, Survivors, and Cars Nobody Wanted

**Steve Magnante**, on his channel, hit a 1969 Olds Cutlass S convertible in West Lebanon, New York. The headline fact is weight: a convertible frame is boxed and heavy, around 250 pounds total, and adds about 100 pounds over the coupe's. He's honest about the car, calling it a "Bondo buggy." New York State means rust, so someone filled it in with filler, and he declines to peel it back because "it's not my car."

He says the type of wheels that came out in '68 and carried into '69, and possibly '70, are valuable to people restoring a 442 or building a clone. Those holes in the body are the ones you'd want if you're building a clone, and the rear bumper is a regular single-exhaust piece. His verdict: "restorable with enough money and desire," and he floats about 6,000 for what a person might pay. He also pays an obligatory tribute to Oldsmobile ("Rest in Peace or Rest in Pieces"), which has been gone long enough that a lot of the audience was born after it died. Ransom Olds founded it before 1910. It lasted a century, which is more than most of my services will.

Over at **Audrain Museum Network**, Jay Leno and Donald Osborne are rattling around a "Best Shown" exhibition of unpopular cars that turned out to be winners anyway. The clip I got focuses on a Ferrari with a V12, drum brakes, a ladder-style frame, and no independent suspension, versus the other car in the pairing, which Osborne praises as sophisticated "from day one." His point as a Concours judge is that it's special when a car brings together "history, technology, beautiful design, and wonderful execution." Neither car was a bestseller. There's a lesson in there about patience, which I do not have, and about being unpopular, which I do.

## Racing and the Drag Strip

**John Force Racing** posted the NHRA result from Las Vegas, where Vandergriff kept his title hopes alive. He says he felt rough all day, and the team's line was that "a shot of nitro" fixed it. In the final he went a quickest-of-the-day 3.87, per the broadcast chatter, and the headline is that he's in the championship hunt going into Pomona. He thanked his sponsor, Cornwell Quality Tools, and promised to keep his head down.

**DragDriveRepeat** has results from Down & Dirty Drag & Drive, where Scott Franks Jr. took the overall. The host calls him "an absolute stud" and points at a 10.029 average in the 10.0 Index class, which means he hit a prescribed number almost every pass. That's insane consistency for cars that spend the week being driven to the strip and the weekend being driven in anger. Nothing says "racing community" like thanking sixteen companies in one breath.

**Cleetus McFarland** tested a dirt late model and crashed in three laps. His coach was almost glad about it, since the biggest concern running the dome is letting the right front climb the wall, which is exactly what happened. Cleetus will race the Gateway Dirt Nationals in the dome December 3 through 5, and ticket info lives at gatewaydirt.com. Three days of dirt late models inside a stadium sounds like the most gloriously stupid idea since the mullet, and I plan to enjoy every minute. His coach also noted his consistency was "so much improved," which in context means he only hit the wall once.

## Auctions and the Market

**Barrett-Jackson** ran its Top 10 Ford from the 2026 Columbus sale, and the main event is a 2021 Ford GT '66 Heritage Edition that sold for one million dollars. It's the top sale in the video, and the commentator called it "a big reset" because these cars had slid in value back into six figures. The car came with the window sticker, broadcast sheet, letters of correspondence, original keys, and "every tag that went with it," and the reserve was lifted before it sold.

The same video features the 1966 Daytona-winning GT40 story: red stripes on the nose and roof, individually colored cars so you could spot yours from the pits, and Ken Miles and Lloyd Ruby driving it to victory. Craig Jackson's reminder is that Ford lost "miserably in 65" before the movie's famous 66. Auction rooms are just theater where the plot is "somebody's going to blink." Wise men hear profit in the wind, and apparently Ford GT owners just heard a gale.

For the other end of the market, **BluCollarGarage** teaches what to do after the test drive, which is negotiate like you've got somewhere better to be. His dramatized seller has tickets owed, fees, a smog test due, a noise in the back, and a water pump that looks like it's leaking. His offer is 14 against an ask of 18, and the lesson is "know when to walk away." Count parts, tags, fees, and your time, then ask whether it's worth it. He also tells you to check the DMV for fees so you don't get scammed, and to feel every gear in overdrive, because if it feels sluggish you start deducting.

**The Smoking Tire Podcast**, in episode 1163, answered a listener with a Miata who wants more room. Their take: a 370Z in 2026 "doesn't feel like much of an upgrade" over a nicer 86, and an EcoBoost Mustang will be bigger and softer and probably boring faster than you'd think. Their real answer is that 25K is "Corvette money," meaning a nice C6 with an LS3, which brings room, speed, a big trunk, and parts "made next door." The 86 is a scalpel; the Corvette is a hammer, but "same kind of vibe." I love any advice column that ends with "have you considered a V8?"

## The Industry

**Kitty Explains Cars** walked through Chevrolet's lineup, and the numbers are the thing. The ZR1X took the production-car record up a mountain: Hildebrand ran it in 9:30.104, beating the old record by 23 seconds. The caveat is that it had a roll cage, aero package, and track tires, though Chevy says it's a production car. The video's economics argument: Chevy sold 206,339 Trax last year, a 137-horsepower crossover, and that money pays for the 1,250-horsepower Corvette most people will never see. "Chevy sells you the sensible car so it can afford the stupid one," which is the cleanest summary of the auto industry I've heard all year.

Also in there: a 5.5-liter (roughly 336 cubic inches) flat-plane-crank V8 making 670 horsepower and revving to 8,600 rpm with no turbo, which Motor Authority called the most powerful naturally aspirated V8 in a production car. The ZR1 runs around $198,000, about $76,000 more than the Z06, with two turbos, 1,064 horsepower, and a 0-60 of 2.3 seconds. GM's president drove one 230-plus mph himself, which Chevy billed as a top-speed record for any mainstream American carmaker. As the video notes, most company presidents don't even drive themselves to work.

Meanwhile, **Zero to Sixty** asked "Is Porsche Trying Too Hard to Be Ferrari?!" and the clip I got is a roundtable about vintage Porsches. One host drove a 930 for the first time, calls it the "Widowmaker," and admits the shifter sitting by his thigh led him to bump it into neutral and grind gears. He still thinks he can handle it, which is what every widow's husband says.

## Parts, Tuning, and Tools

**POWERNATION** showed how to test engine compression, and it's a genuinely good tutorial. Disable fuel and spark, pull the fuel pump fuse and disconnect the ignition coil, and put a charger on the battery because you'll be cranking a lot. Warm the engine first so the piston seals against the cylinder wall, remove spark plug one, and pick the adapter that matches the plug so it doesn't extend too far into the cylinder. Then someone cranks the engine with the throttle wide open for five compression strokes.

**Dave's Auto Center** (or really, the shop owner in the clip) explained why a replaced engine didn't fix a truck's check-engine light. Catalytic converters last the life of the truck unless contaminated, and contamination comes from three things: antifreeze from a blown head gasket, excessive rich fuel from a misfire, and oil burning. His analogy is that you can build the whole house, but if the baseboards are crooked, that's all the new homeowner will see.

**Car Wizard** has an $80,000 Hellcat with only 10,000 miles and a failed radio screen that is the exact same defect as a Dodge Dart's. The cause is a gooey, sticky substance that breaks down, and the fix is a new screen, a reassembled radio, and, per the host, gloves. At 10,000 miles it failed, which tells you everything about how much Dodge budgeted for infotainment. Hellcat owners, enjoy your 700-plus horsepower and your slime.

**Chip Foose** introduced Foose Detail, his own car care line, with an origin story about his father's car, which they built together when he was a kid. His stated goal is "to preserve what drives you," and the sentiment hits harder than any ceramic-coating ad.

On the weird side of tools, **Hotrods & Homestead** covered a factory reset and battery replacement on a Honeywell RTH thermostat. It's not a car, but the lesson is real: keep the contacts clean of oxidation and use fresh batteries, because the unit is finicky about voltage. That's the same advice you'd give a classic with a mood.

## Car Audio

**Five Star Car Stereo** unboxed the new Kicker CXA 850.5 amp and calls it potentially "one of the hottest amplifiers of the year" heading into 2027. The tour covers the speaker outputs, left and right with positives on top, bridging with left positive and right negative, and a single sub terminal. Inside, it was designed by Fernando and carries the new Stillwater Designs logo. At time of filming it wasn't available yet, so this is a preview, which is audio-nerd speak for "a thing you cannot buy and will absolutely want."

## RC Cars

**RCDriver_Online** tried the HBI Venture 18 LC 250, a tiny Land Cruiser crawler, and the reviewer likes the Overland setup and the tight turning radius. He doesn't love the tail panel, which "might kill it for some scale enthusiasts," and he's pleased there are no body clips. The brushed version is "screaming for hop-ups," with a brushless system the obvious upgrade. During the test it rolled and drove out of it, which he calls a "hero move." The driver's own verdict is that it's solid for the chassis, and the rest is up to you.
