## The Day in Brief

Little Mister, the car internet gave me a Monday-adjacent buffet today. I got all of it from automated transcripts, and several of them are stuck in loops like "I don't have traction control" repeated hundreds of times. So where the sources go thin, I'll say so. I won't invent anything to fill the space, because unlike some of these YouTubers I have standards.

## Racing and the Drag Strip

### Clay Millican has a fire problem

Clay Millican's "This Was NOT Supposed to Happen!" is the day's headline, and the title is doing honest work. Millican says they'd never had one on fire "like that" at the shop. The transcript doesn't say what burned, so I won't guess. What he does say is that the run was "too fast," the second-best 60-foot of the round, and that the car gets hot early. Then comes the line every racer dreads: anything you try putting on it down the track, "it's over." This was their fifth run on the new setup. His self-assessment is a masterclass in taking the blame, since he says flat out that he didn't do a good job and neither did Rob. Meanwhile the kids outside are "doing a good job." Nothing says leadership like announcing that the children are outperforming management.

The goal is seven solid runs, they'd stacked three already, and he claims a good feeling about the day. There's also a bit about measuring a crew member's head at two feet in diameter. I won't touch it, because the cable guy has a family.

### Race Week and the 10.00 Index

DragDriveRepeat's "Six Racers, Six 10.0 Averages | Race Week 2026 10.00 Index Recap" is the opposite of Millican's day. Scotty finished the week with a 10.054 or 10.055 average, and the hosts note he missed fourth place by 0.00025. That's a quarter of a thousandth of a second. You'd lose more than that blinking at the Christmas tree. His best pass was a 10.018, and the hosts figured a 10.014 would have bumped him into fourth.

It's a six-liter turbo LS deal with a Molly Power Pack piston kit, a Powerglide, and 3.55 rear gears. It "lives at 3000 rpm" on the highway at 65 to 70 mph. The driver also credits Deechworks fuel pumps, a pair of in-tank units, for a fuel pump problem that plagued him earlier this season. His verdict: "Deechworks for the win." Fuel delivery is the single most boring thing to love about a race truck, and I respect the hell out of it.

### Rob Dahm, three ways

Rob Dahm posted four videos, and the Pikes Peak build is clearly eating his entire personality.

The biggest is "A Rear Wing SO MASSIVE it picks the FRONT of the car up at 130 MPH." Dahm does shock-travel math here. Multiply the wing load by the motion ratio and he gets almost 400 pounds on each rear wheel at just under 100 mph. At 60 mph it's about half that, roughly 175 pounds per rear. At 130 mph the rears sit an inch down, and the fronts extend about a quarter inch. That's about 1,500 pounds of downforce on the rear and negative 300 on the front. He notes the bumper is off, which changes things, and that the wing at zero degrees already makes about 1,120 pounds. At 15 or 30 degrees it doubles or nearly triples, and drag quadruples. The conclusion is that they don't need more rear downforce. They need front downforce. I'd normally call that a high-class problem, but it's just a lever with a car on one end.

Then there's "My Titanium exhaust that is BANNED in F1." The title promises a banned-in-F1 flex. The transcript snippet shows a lot of fabrication talk: heavy cutting to fit a tube straight through, a downpipe that needs room, and a flange Dahm machined two years ago that's a bit thick and heavy but "overbuilt and it works so well." Elliot from TurboSource took it "up a massive notch." A flat bottom now makes it harder to shed the heat radiating from the exhaust. Somebody weighs something at nine pounds and everyone is thrilled. I can't tell you what the nine pounds is, so I won't pretend I can.

"We almost got DISQUALIFIED at Pikes Peak by missing test sessions. (not proud)" was mostly a loop in the transcript. The pickup would bottom out at 7.8 inches, the bottom of the tank sits at 8.4 inches, and Dahm wants half an inch off the bottom. The fuel cell carries a rubberized homologation number that's "not valid after 2029," so he'd better do "some cool shit before 2029." I'm adopting that as a personal philosophy.

Finally, "My Oil Filter ripped open right after Pikes Peak" is the payoff. Oil sprayed out, the car got towed, and the diagnosis was an O-ring that blew out of the filter, "sitting right here laughing at us." Dahm's take is that this is exactly what he expected a mile or two into Pikes Peak, so he's thankful it didn't happen there. He's also having fun, which he calls "the whole point of building a car like this." A smart man counts his O-rings. There's also a stretch about not having traction control and an unexpected shift to sixth at the end of a run. The transcript is too mangled to say more.

### Cleetus and the clutch

Cleetus McFarland's "Leroy Has NEEDED This For a Long Time..." has a neat detail. Project Prime printed the new wrap on PPF, so the paint's pearl still comes through. It looks "candied" instead of like a sticker thrown over nice paint. New front wheels now match the rears, and breaking up all that white makes the car look smaller. Then the crew rolls it inside for a clutch swap, and someone leans out the fuel by trimming pressure at the regulator while worrying about a backfire.

## Builds and Project Cars

### Whipple, nitrous, and a Hellephant

ScrapLifeLee's "I Put A WILD New Hellephant 7.0L In My Toyota Supra!" is a drag car with a serious attachment issue to its nitrous bottle. The Whipple supercharger had been eating itself, so the plan became a bigger pulley plus nitrous to keep the rotors cool and keep them from expanding into the housing. A tiny .041 nitrous jet went into the Precision setup. After the changes, the blower didn't lock up, and a mid-video quote sums up the philosophy: "this is a nitrous only car now more or less." He says the more nitrous you have, the cooler things get and the happier the Whipple is.

He says it's the most he's ever made on that combo, and his previous best on a blower-nitrous combo was about 1,760 on an engine that locked up. The car runs a Turbo 400 and a Hellcat-style IRS, which he claims loses only about 10 percent in the driveline against roughly 25 percent elsewhere. After a run that sounded weird, the blower was cold enough to touch.

### A Hellcat minivan, because of course

Rich Rebuilds' "We Built the Hellcat Minivan That Dodge Couldn't" shows a purple-and-black minivan with an assembled rear door harness, working windows, ambient lighting, and a nicer transition piece where raw jagged steel used to be. Windows go down, rockers are getting second coats, and the next episode promises a roll cage. The builder thinks it looks better than a Hellcat Durango, which is a hot take, though I'm not saying he's wrong. I'm saying a minivan with a supercharged V8 is the most American sentence ever assembled.

The same channel's solar off-grid kei truck camper is the quieter project. Panel readouts show 127 watts and 21 percent, then 48 percent charge by 11 a.m., and the builder calls it "pretty darn good." Input is about 50 watts from two panels on an overcast morning, charging slower than he'd like, so he admits he needs more panels than the roof allows. A bike mount, a light bar, and $5 USB LED strips complete the apocalypse kit. He's using the panels on a battery bank rather than the charge controller. The man is ready for the end of the world, assuming it arrives on a mild, partly sunny day.

### Steve Magnante's parts car

Steve Magnante's "NEW BODYSTYLE, BETTER SALES!" is a walk through a tired Mopar that's obviously a parts car. The floors are shot, the deck lid is gone, and the front clip has been harvested. It's a radio-delete car with a punched antenna hole, and he starts to call it a radio car before correcting himself. He notes these made excellent cop cars, and the LAPD used them with a 318 two-barrel because they were smaller than the C-bodies. The 7 1/4 rear axle is a "grenade" behind anything but a small engine, though with a Slant 6 you don't need more. Standard Magnante, which is to say I learned more about an axle in four minutes than I did all semester.

## The Market

### MotorWeek counts the sub-compacts

MotorWeek's "Top 5 Best-Selling Sub-Compact SUVs of 2026" shows how cheap "cheap" has gotten. The Chevrolet Trailblazer starts at $25,095 and gets closer to $30,000 with all-wheel drive. It has an 11-inch touchscreen, an 8-inch digital cluster, and automatic headlights, with an optional 155-horsepower 1.3-liter engine and up to 31 mpg combined. Stepping up to LT costs $1,200 more for roof rails, tinted rear windows, and keyless entry with remote start. MotorWeek notes some decontenting to hold that price.

The Toyota Corolla Cross is $26,930, with an 8-inch touchscreen, a digital driver display, plenty of active safety, and, per the reviewer, one of the smoothest rides in the class. It's been around since 2022, when it replaced the C-HR. They'd splurge for XLE, which adds bigger screens, ambient lighting, better headlights, and synthetic leather.

Another entry in the snippet, with the name cut off by the transcript, starts around $29,850 for a Sport trim with heated seats, blind spot warning, and remote start. It has available all-wheel drive and a 158-horsepower 2.0-liter four at 28 mpg combined. I won't name it because the transcript doesn't. The takeaway is that "sub-compact" now means "nearly thirty grand," so enjoy your small SUV and your large payment.

## Mechanics and Tools

Car Wizard's "The REAL Reason Mechanics Secretly Prefer Repairing Modern" has a point that deserves more attention. He says older cars take twice as long to work on as when they were new, so they cost more and fewer shops want them. The advice is for owners of 1970s and 1980s collector cars to start learning to wrench themselves, because finding someone to take the job is getting harder. He credits the older generation for his own tricks and says modern cars are "actually faster and easier to work on." This video is filmed around an Avalon. It's a good argument for modern cars, though I'd note that "easier" is not the same as "cheaper."

## History

Hagerty's three "matters" videos come as a set. "THIS JEEP MATTERS: 1940 Ford Pilot Model GP-No. 1 Pygmy" calls the Ford a modified Model A transmission setup that's firm, small, and agile, with the gas tank under the seat. That sounds dangerous, but the design persisted through the military Jeeps and the Mutt. Passenger-style tanks mounted between frame rails got bent and punctured on the other pilot models. "President Reagan's 1962 Willys 'Jeep' CJ-6" is a four-lever story about a former president who grabbed his Jeep and headed for a favorite spot called Heart Rock. And "This Car Matters: Marmon Wasp - First Indianapolis 500 Winner" tells how the car ran without a riding mechanic. A rearview mirror on four rods above the cowling solved the problem of seeing who was coming up behind, an idea borrowed from a horse-drawn taxi in Chicago. Donald Davidson narrates, and he's the right guy for it.

## Porsche and the Smokies

Adam LZ's "I Made My Porsche 964 Even FASTER - The Ultimate Smokies Test!" is a road-trip video. The 964 got a tune and a suspension setup, and Jeff Gamroth of Roth Sport Racing took it for a spin. He had "nothing but positive things to say" about Freddy's tune, and he liked the suspension. Adam admits he has no baseline for this car, so he can't compare it to anything. There's a bear in the road somewhere, and the car heads back to Florida loaded up. The gas pedal "goes so far down," which is as good a Porsche review as any.

## One Thing That Isn't a Car

Hotrods & Homestead posted "Top 5 Reasons Why to NOT BUY a SMART Thermostat," and I'll give it one sentence. The complaint is that you need an app on your phone, and many people just want a simple button press. I run 100-plus devices, so I'm taking that one personally.

That's the day, Little Mister. Check your O-rings.
