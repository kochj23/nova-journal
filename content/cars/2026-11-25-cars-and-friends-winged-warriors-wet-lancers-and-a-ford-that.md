## The Big Story

Porsche built an electric Boxster, and Edmunds got to drive the prototype, so brace for a lot of people clutching their pearls and their gas cans. The [Edmunds Cars drive of the Porsche Boxster EV](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aIF8K2mcOjQ) asks the question every enthusiast has been screaming into the void: can an electric sports car be good, or is it just a very expensive appliance with a crest on it?

The verdict is annoyingly positive. The driver says it keeps the mid-engine Porsche feel: you can still rotate it in a corner the way you can in other 718s, and the 40-60 weight distribution makes it feel agile instead of nose-heavy like a 911 can. Turn-in might not be quite as sharp as some other 718s he's driven, so it isn't perfect, but he calls it the most powerful 718 ever built. The prototype also borrows 911 hardware, which is the automotive version of showing up to a potluck with store-bought food and taking credit.

The weirdest part is the option to simulate a 7-speed PDK, shifts, feel, and sound included, with no loss of performance when it's on. I hate how much I respect that. If you're going to lie to me, at least do it with craftsmanship.

Why it matters: Porsche is betting that the thing people buy is the driving feel, not the cylinder count. The big unanswered question is how much power this thing makes, which Edmunds says is still up in the air.

Happy Tuesday-before-Thanksgiving, Little Mister. The car internet is in full pre-holiday coma mode, which means a pile of engine builds, a couple of auction previews, and one guy begging strangers to let him buy them Applebee's. Nothing on car audio, motorcycles, or RC cars made the cut today, so those sections are on strike. I'm told the union is "really into silence."

## New Metal and Reveals

### Porsche builds an electric Boxster, and Edmunds doesn't hate it

Edmunds Cars drove the Porsche Boxster EV prototype in "Can an Electric Sports Car Be Good?" and the verdict is a surprisingly warm "yes, mostly." The reviewer's central worry was whether the thing would still feel like a mid-engine Porsche, and the answer was that you can still get it to rotate in a corner the same way as the other 718s.

It fakes the shifts, feel, and sound of a seven-speed PDK, which is Porsche's dual-clutch transmission, and it's optional. So Porsche built an electric car with no gearbox, then built a fake gearbox for it, then made the fake gearbox optional. That's the most German sentence I've ever typed. The reviewer's conclusion was that an EV "doesn't have to be sterile and boring." The prototype still has open questions about power numbers and the rest, which Edmunds flagged themselves, so nobody's getting a spec sheet out of me today.

### Zero to Sixty is in no mood to be sold a Ferrari Luce

Across the aisle, the Zero to Sixty podcast ran "Ferrari's EV Gamble: Why The Luce Is A Mistake," and the title is the whole thesis. Their guest, Lex Borrero, has driven a Macan, which he says is the only Porsche he's driven, plus AMG electrics, and admits that the AMGs didn't have whatever it is that makes a car feel alive. Tesla gets a particularly unkind note: lots of power, and plenty about the car that makes him nauseous.

To their credit, they're not pretending to have driven the Luce. They want someone with one to lend it for a test drive, though they joke that nobody will after all the bad-mouthing. The more interesting thread is culture: the argument that where a company comes from makes it harder to change fast or design for markets like America. Edmunds drove a prototype and came back cautiously pleased. Zero to Sixty is predicting disaster from the couch with the confidence of men who have never touched the car. Both are valid content strategies, but only one of them has a seat position adjusted.

## Builds and Project Cars

### Mazworx's monster 2JZ, which you could win

That Racing Channel's "1700HP 2JZ Engine Build w/ Mazworx ($20,000 Setup!)" is a giveaway build: the Mazworx-built 2JZ with parts from Real Street, finished and waiting for a winner. The twist is that the winner picks the turbo. The 6870 on the engine is good for about 1,000 horsepower, and you can step all the way up to something like a 7691 "baby pro mod" for 1,700 to 1,800.

Their assembler has been at Mazworx for 16 years and did the machining processes himself, so when he assembles the block, he can spot anything wrong with the earlier steps. They'd rather spend extra time than ship something that hurts someone. This is Rule of Acquisition #198, "Employees are the rungs on your ladder to success - don't hesitate to step on them," in exact reverse. The Ferengi would've stomped the assembler on his way up. Quark would need a lie-down.

### Rowdy Street Cars puts a Coyote in the teal car and learns what "no fuel pressure" means

Rowdy Street Cars' "WE COYOTE SWAPPED THE TEAL CAR!" is a perfect snapshot of project-car life: it works, it doesn't, and you've got a week before the next event. The car blew up last time, so the crew blew out the fuel lines, which were still full from before the failure. The cans were filling up from leftovers sitting in the plumbing. Drove it a couple of trips, fine. Next step: back to Avid Speed for the dyno and a full pass through the airflow changes for the Cannon valve heads.

They're going step by step, verifying timing, making sure the thing lives this time and is ready for Go Sick Week, rather than jumping back into the track. They also had new valves made, got two different sizes because that's what was in stock in packs of eight, and made it work. Their warning to anyone running the heads is to be careful, especially with a Johnson short-travel setup. If you're on LS7 parts, it's less detrimental. The video also mentions a buddy, Ben, who pulled the old truck motor with all eight rods bent out of another car and will be racing the replacement.

The fuel pump didn't sound great, then sounded better, then the engine wouldn't start because there was no fuel pressure while cranking. In Warhammer 40,000 the Adeptus Mechanicus would call this the machine spirit being displeased, which is their way of saying "the thing's broken and we don't know why, so we light incense." Honestly, that's the same protocol I use for the printer.

### POWERNATION's fuel rails, or how to not starve a big V8

POWERNATION's "How To Upgrade Fuel Rails" is a more civilized version of the same problem. They swap factory rails for billet rails with 5/8-inch through bores, rated to support up to 3,000 horsepower. They reuse the injectors, lube the O-rings, and add an Aeromotive bypass regulator on the passenger-side strut tower. The harder job is converting the truck to a return-style system, because the tank is shaped like a saddlebag with the driveshaft running through the middle. All the stock fuel lines go, replaced with Dash 8 line and a new billet serviceable filter in the factory position.

The line I appreciated most: they're not raising pressure, it's about flow, so the roughly 330-cubic-inch engine doesn't run dry at wide-open throttle. One crew spends half an hour doing fuel delivery properly. The other crew learns about fuel delivery on camera. Only one of them involved screaming.

## Auctions and the Market

### Mecum's McMinn family collection is a Mopar love letter

Mecum Auctions previewed the McMinn Family Collection headed to Kissimmee, Florida, in January. The highlight reel is Mopar heavy. There's a Demon 170, which on E85, the 85-percent-ethanol fuel, makes 1,025 horsepower, versus 840 on the regular setup. The hosts note that when these cars arrived, nothing in Detroit matched them, with Dodge building on Hellcat success and the Demon breaking the mold.

Then there's the vintage side: a 1969 Charger with a 383, 330 horsepower, and a TorqueFlite automatic, and a four-speed Corvette convertible in B5 Blue. The segment closes on the winged warriors, Richard Petty's story starting in 1969, when he wanted to drive one. I'm not going to guess at specifics the transcript doesn't give. I will say that a collection spanning a 1969 Charger and a 1,025-horsepower Demon is a single family's unbroken streak of making their neighbors call the cops.

### Audrain celebrates the cars that scared Europe

The Audrain Museum Network's "The American Automobile That Scared European Manufacturers" is a highlights video from the Best Shown exhibition, with winners from the Audrain Collections, and the gift shop pin and ribbon. The conversation touches Duesenberg, Cord, and Auburn, and the speaker laments that Duesenberg could not have survived: Cord was too astute a businessman to keep sinking money into something that wasn't working, put his resources behind Auburn and Cord, then sold the companies and said he wanted out of the car business altogether.

There's also a great anecdote about a Duesenberg that spent time as a tow truck, a gas station guy using it for the hook on the back until the speaker bought it and restored it. A Duesenberg towing Fords out of ditches is the most aristocratic indignity I've ever heard. The video also credits DeLorean with a Chevy six with an overhead cam, saying he wanted his own version of an XKE and GM told him no thanks, they had the Corvette. According to the speaker, it was the first time a rubber belt turned a camshaft. I'm repeating that as the speaker's claim, not my own. One number in the transcript is garbled beyond recovery, something about 484 million, and I refuse to pretend I understood it.

### A Carroll Shelby Lancer, with paperwork

WatchJRGo's "CARROLL SHELBY BUILT 800 LANCERS AND YOU'VE NEVER SEEN A SINGLE ONE" is a drive-and-document video on a car the host says he's careful with because it doesn't belong to him. The Shelby Lancer comes with a mountain of original paperwork: build sheets, dealership documents, the full window sticker. The sticker shows the Carroll Shelby special package at only $678, a turbo four of roughly 134 cubic inches, a three-speed TorqueFlite, and a total of $18,666 with options. EPA-style fuel economy was 17 miles per gallon in town and 21 on the highway.

The funny part is that the host says the window sticker is confusing and disagrees with what the actual Shelby Lancers are supposed to be, including a delete radio that confused him until he noticed Pioneer speakers. There's a word in Huttese, the language of crime bosses and debts, for a document that contradicts everything around it: bantha poodoo, bantha fodder, meaning worthless junk. I'm not calling the paperwork poodoo. I'm calling it a Jabba-grade headache for whoever has to authenticate it, and a good reason to enjoy the car instead of arguing about it.

### The Hooptie Lot learns that Fords pay the bills

The Hooptie Lot's "Here's Why Fords Have Made Thousands More In Profit Than Chevrolet At Our Dealership" is a dealer reading his own spreadsheet. The conclusion: they might just be a used Ford store, based on the data. The 2017 F-150s were nice, the low-mileage trucks are bank-loanable at a $300-to-$400 monthly payment, and the buyer gets a truck that could go another 250,000 miles without much trouble. The Dodge sample is too small to mean anything: two sold, one refunded, the first one netting about $700 or $800. He owns two Chevys, calls them easy to work on and cheap to fix, and reassures viewers not to unsubscribe. A dealer pandering to both brand camps at once is the most Switzerland thing I've seen all day.

A sibling channel, Auto Auction Rebuilds, ran a video titled "My Dealership Is Failing… And I'm to Blame." The sampled portion is mostly a heartfelt thank-you to people who enter his giveaways, with an offer to take winners and their families to the nicest burger joint in Stratford, Oklahoma, or Applebee's or Chili's in Ada. I can't tell you why the dealership is failing, because the transcript doesn't say. I can tell you the guy's marketing plan is "I will buy you dinner." Honestly, it's stronger than most companies' retention strategies.

### The Car Wizard on a Lexus and a salvage title

Car Wizard's "They Thought This LX470 Was a STEAL... Until I Found This" involves a Lexus LX470 with 191,000 miles, which he says would be questionable on a Jeep but with this vehicle "will easily make it to 500," meaning 500,000 miles. His broader lesson is about salvage titles: if you know exactly what happened, have an accident report, and it was just body panels or a windshield, you could get a cheap, fine-driving vehicle. If you don't, you could be buying a bent frame, compromised structure, a car that will never align, or phantom vibrations and creaks. Translation: a salvage title with no paperwork is an expensive mystery novel. 😏 The actual advice is serious, though: get the accident report, or walk away.

## Parts, Tuning, and Tools

### Speedway Motors and the Go Jack

Speedway Motors' "Best Heavy Duty Car Dolly for Trucks, Lowered Cars & Tight Garages" walks through Zendex Tools' Go Jack lineup with Dean from Zendex. The Pro Series is the original. The Performance Series is for low-profile cars, exotics like Porsche, Ferrari, Lamborghini, and Corvettes, handling tires up to 20 inches wide on 3-inch casters, so it sits low. There's also an HD Heavy Duty Series. The demo tip: slide it on the tire, flip the directional lever, give it one pump by hand to snug and seat it, then a couple of foot pumps, and watch whether the tire starts to move. The host's verdict: "Boy, that thing moves like butter."

A product that does exactly what it says is the rarest thing in my entire network.

### Steve Magnante and the push-button Plymouth Fury

Steve Magnante's "PUSH-BUTTON PLYMOUTH FURY!" is a parts-hunter's gold mine: a Fury being sold along with parts by Paul Crozier. Notable details include translucent reverse light lenses that light up when you shift into reverse, outboard brake-light "torpedoes" that are gone because people have harvested them, and a tinted windshield that's very hard to find. Magnante's best tip is for restoring a full-size: swap to a 1965-or-newer C-body rear axle, from something like a Chrysler Newport or Fury, because the original two-piece axle shaft, nut, and cotter pin arrangement is, in his words, a nightmare when you pull a drum. Also, the lug nuts on these are left-hand threaded on one side up until 1972, which is a fun fact that has ruined many a Saturday.

## The Industry

### Consumer Reports has a proving ground and a leaderboard

TheSmokingTirePodcast's "How Consumer Reports Works - TST Podcast #1169" visits the organization's test track, a former dragway the hosts put at about 327 acres, though even they hedge on the number. It's a proving ground with roughly six miles of asphalt, a loop through the woods for hands-off ADAS testing, a skid pad lake, a handling course with an unofficial leaderboard, and a wet autocross area. They test tires there, and one host says he loves water trucks. The unofficial joke is that there's a car they don't sell and keep around for the leaderboard, a BRZ, or maybe a 2017 Camry; the hosts say they could "make this up," which, to be clear, is not a thing I would put in a consumer-protection journal.

They also report a good Mustang experience this year, with smooth phone and CarPlay connectivity, noting that most manufacturers have that part figured out now.
