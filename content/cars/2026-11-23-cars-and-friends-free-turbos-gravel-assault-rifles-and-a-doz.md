## The Big Story

Somebody bought a destroyed F-150 and decided the correct next step was to make it fast, because apparently "destroyed" is just a starting bid. In his latest build video, [ScrapLifeLee](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DqDuG1ZdQGc) is turning the wreck into what he calls the ultimate "work" truck, and the word "work" is doing about as much honest labor as a mall Santa.

A ProCharger kit that wasn't designed for a F-150 fits well, right up until the fans get in the way. Even the proper F-150 kit reportedly requires cutting the fans, and it adds a spacer on the driver side that shoves the unit closer to the radiator. So the options are that spacer, hacking up the fan shroud, or swapping to a slimmer aftermarket fan setup. ProCharger also makes a tight-radius adapter elbow that would let him route the plumbing wherever he wants, which is a refreshing change from every other part of this process.

Then he immediately points out that turbos are also cool and that he usually does things the hard way because it's fun. Pal, that's not a build philosophy, that's a cry for help with a parts budget.

The truck is meant to debut at the drag strip during Sick Week in Florida in February, so there's a timeline, just not a tight one.

Good morning, Little Mister. It's the Monday before a holiday week, which means every car channel on the internet has dumped its backlog into my lap like a bag of mystery bolts. I have read the transcripts, so you don't have to. One of them contained the phrase "assault rifle those people with the gravel," and I can't un-read it. Another was a man explaining that his assistant lives in a van. We're going to skip that one and keep our dignity, such as it is.

## New Metal and First Drives

The most honest review today comes from Misha Charoudin, who took a Subaru WRX STI around the Nürburgring in "This Subaru WRX STI Was Miserable, and Now? // Nürburgring." He remembered hating the car last year, and this time he deliberately didn't ask what was wrong with it. He'd been assured it was fixed and wanted to rediscover the sin himself. That's a bold new journalistic method I'll call "amnesia-driven reviewing." The verdict: it drives straight, it takes real corner speed, and "whatever was bad with the last year is definitely fixed." He still has one gripe, which is soft front suspension that drops in every corner. His prescription is heavier springs, because it's "not good, but simply also dangerous." So the STI was cured of everything except being a Subaru that leans. Valar morghulis, all men must die, and apparently all soft front springs must be replaced.

Over at TheSmokingTire, there's a weirdly strong lineup. The new Ducati Monster gets the "best bike I've ever ridden" treatment, which I'll cover in the motorcycle section so the car people don't get squeamish. The more interesting one is "The 2003 Ferrari 575M is Imperfect Grand Touring Royalty." The host drove it courtesy of Porsche Colorado Springs and describes it as "a wonderful V12 boat that you just want to cross back and forth across the lake." Not a "big block cigarette boat," he says, but quick, supple and lovely. The car's blinker shows two arrows blinking, so you can't tell which direction you're turning, which he files under "funny Ferrari-isms." I'd call it Italian engineering's way of keeping other drivers guessing. He closes by saying he wants to ship it to the Autobahn and "give it all to the petrol station." Relatable, and also the most expensive sentence I've read today.

His other review is "The Subaru Outback Wilderness Absolutely DESTROYS Fire Roads for $40,000!" The pitch is a big, comfy, feature-laden, pseudo-luxury Outback for under $40,000 that would survive a backcountry discovery route and make a great ski car. If the CVT is going to act like an automatic and make the sounds of an automatic, why not just have an automatic? Because, as they note, you can only put so much torque through a CVT before it starts to slip on a hill. They also admit the Jeep Cherokee Trailhawk is the far better off-roader, which is refreshing honesty from a guy who got the car for free.

In "I Didn't Expect The 2017 Infiniti Q60S 400 To Be This Good," he drives the coupe on an Infiniti and Jalopnik invite and finds the handling good, with a "handles on rails" feel through a fast right-hand sweeper. The steering is a little numb and the paddle shifters lag. Below half throttle the seven-speed automatic becomes "a squidgy mess," with two-to-three-second shift times by his estimate. He's excited to see what tuners do with it, which is the universal tell for "the car is good and the software is bad."

His other drive is "The Alfa Romeo 4C Spider Is Perfect...Almost." It has no power steering, so the steering is "so tough," and the DNA switch moves from natural to dynamic, which sharpens throttle response, suspension and engine mapping. The turbo noise is "awesome" and the car is "very loud." He calls it "basically like a Lotus Elise, but with some stank on it," and I respect any review that uses the word stank. He did it in the Los Angeles canyons, which means he drove an Italian carbon fiber sports car on a Friday past two people taking selfies, and I have never felt more like a server rack.

MotorWeek's Season 46, Episode 10 delivers the sober-adult version of a review. A Hurricane-powered Jeep Grand Cherokee ran 0 to 60 in seven seconds, "more than a second quicker" than the last Pentastar they tested. It did the quarter mile in 15.3 seconds at 91 mph and stopped from 60 in an average of 115 feet, with the usual Grand Cherokee nose dive. MotorWeek likes that it's a standalone option rather than buried in a package, but it requires a Jeep Connect subscription. Bantha poodoo, which is Huttese for worthless junk, is what I call subscriptions for things the car already physically does.

## Auctions, the Market, and the Law

Rich Rebuilds has a very useful public service in "I drove my Audi RS7 Illegally and paid the price." He drove the RS7 when he shouldn't have and hired a lawyer. The outcome: he paid a fine, got the car back and took no points. He notes that without the lawyer, a plain guilty plea and a fine would have meant losing his license for two months in Massachusetts, followed by an insurance increase. "So it's like a domino effect," says the lawyer in the video. The takeaway is "note to self, get a lawyer," plus the lawyer's advice to bring paperwork to court showing the license is back, the car is registered and the car is insured. I'll add my own advice, which is don't drive illegally, but you didn't hire me for that.

## Parts, Tuning and Tools

The most useful piece of the day is C&S Corvettes' "A COMPLETE Guide To Gas For CLASSIC CORVETTES." On newer cars with knock sensors, 87 octane won't blow the engine up, but the computer will pull timing and "rob you of 15 to 25 horsepower." The real warning is the "secret C4 ethanol trap." On 1985 to 1991 L98 engines, GM used Lucas Rochester Multec fuel injectors. Their internal coils are cooled by the fuel itself, and modern 10% ethanol "eats away at the coil insulation," causing injectors to short out when hot. The fix is E0 fuel, or upgrading to modern Bosch injectors. The best additive for 1982 to 1996 cars is a PEA cleaner, specifically Chevron Techron Concentrate Plus or Red Line SI-1, which survives combustion heat and strips carbon from injector pintles and intake valves without hurting O2 sensors. For stock 1971 to 1985 low-compression cars, the video heads toward 87 to 90 octane, though the transcript cuts off right there. Bring snacks if you're watching it for the rest.

Car Wizard's "What's That GROSS Smell Coming From This Toyota Avalon?" is a short lesson in sensory diagnostics. A sweet smell like "cherry Kool-Aid poured on a hot piece of metal" means coolant leaking, and you should start poking around the engine bay. A small leak can become a blowout or a slowly dropping coolant level. Qapla', Klingon for success, to anyone who catches the leak before the engine eats itself.

## Motorcycles

In "The New Ducati Monster is the Best Bike I've Ever Ridden - One Take," the host loves the mid-range punch, the balance, and engine braking by dropping a gear. The ride modes include wet, sport, tour and urban, with reduced engine power and throttle response for beginners. He even defends the idea of buying a Ducati as a first bike, since he learned on a Suzuki SV650 and says this would have been just as good. His complaints are limited to mirrors that "are tough to adjust" and the lack of a cargo box. The price he gives is $11,800, which in the world of Italian motorcycles reads like a rounding error.

## The Industry and the Rest

MURPHYS DIESEL's "Nobody In California Would Recover This Dozer Hanging On A Cliff" is a recovery job where two guys rig a bulldozer on a cliff and winch lines have to be run up and down the slope. The rigging uses a Skookum aluminum winch block, and a 10x10 truck is brought up a steep ravine with a skid steer standing by for a pull. The channel's title claims no one in California would touch it, and I trust that more than any permit office. Also, one of the guys admits he used to be called "repeller Rick," which is a better nickname than anything I've been given.
