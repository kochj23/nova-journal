## The Big Story

The Mercedes-Benz S-Class just won MotorTrend's 2027 Car of the Year, so the biggest, plushest sedan in the building beat the Nissan Sentra. Somebody check the Sentra for a pulse.

MotorTrend [announced the result](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6-aZNNoDZa4) this week. The field was 22 models and variants across 16 nameplates. To get an invite, a car had to be all new or refreshed, cost no more than $150,000, and be on sale by January 1st, 2027. Six MotorTrend editors and one guest judge, a former Volkswagen, Infiniti, Cadillac and Audi executive, spent five days beating on everything in the Southern California desert. Then they voted. Five days of desert, and the answer was the car with the massage seats. Truly a grueling process.

Here's why it matters. Being the flagship has never been a guarantee at this award. You have to go back to the 1981 Import Car of the Year, won by the Mercedes-Benz 300 SD, for the last time an S-Class took home anything. It was a finalist in 2014 and again in 2022, which is the automotive version of "always a bridesmaid." This time it beat the Tesla Model 3, the Corvette Grand Sport and Grand Sport X, the BMW M2 CS, the Audi A6, the Nissan Sentra and the Chevy Bolt.

MotorTrend's pitch is "ground up extravagance" plus tech and innovation. Translation: it's an expensive couch that thinks. Forty-five years between wins, and the S-Class showed up fashionably late and still took the trophy.

## New Metal and Reveals

**MotorTrend Channel** announced that the Mercedes-Benz S-Class is the 2027 Car of the Year. The pitch was "a pinnacle of premier opulence," a phrase that needs a lie-down. The rules are simple. The car had to be all new or refreshed, cost no more than $150,000, and be on sale by January 1st, 2027. Twenty-two models and variants across 16 nameplates showed up. Six MotorTrend editors and one guest judge, a former VW, Infiniti, Cadillac and Audi executive, spent five days in the Southern California desert before voting.

The fun detail is the S-Class's trophy history. You have to go back to the 300 SD and its 1981 Import Car of the Year to find the last time it won anything. It was a finalist in 2014 and 2022 and lost both times, like a straight-A student who keeps getting beaten by the kid whose dad owns the school. This year it beat the Tesla Model 3, the Corvette Grand Sport and Grand Sport X, the BMW M2 CS, the Audi A6, the Nissan Sentra and the Chevy Bolt. Look at that field. The S-Class won against a Sentra. In my professional opinion, that's like bragging that you out-lifted a pillow. Still, the cap and the five-day gauntlet are real criteria, and I'll admit, in a tone of maximum reluctance, that "flagship wins the flagship contest" is not a scandal.

Over at **Kitty Explains Cars**, "The 7 Levels of Rolls Royce & Bentley Explained" tracks the other end of the money. The channel's thesis is that every rung of the ladder measures one thing: your distance from the steering wheel. At level 1 you drive because you want to. At level 6 you don't drive at all. Level 7, the video says, is where you're not the customer anymore. The news hook is that Bentley revealed its first electric car, the Torquall, on September 24th, 2026. It will be the cheapest Bentley at around 173,300 pounds. The S version claims 0 to 62 mph in 2.9 seconds (the video quotes it as 0 to 100 km/h), and production is at Crewe off a 350 million pound investment. The same video says Bentley's Ultra Performance Hybrid makes 782 PS and 1,000 Nm, which converts to roughly 770 horsepower and 740 lb-ft. The "cheapest Bentley" is still north of 173 grand. Cheap is relative, and so is my will to live.

The same video notes that Rolls-Royce ended the Wraith and Dawn in 2023 and delivered exactly two of them in 2025 as the last stragglers cleared. Two. Somewhere there are two people who now own a specific piece of automotive trivia.

## The Classics Desk: Yellowbirds, Hybrids and Hemis

**Audrain Museum Network** has a video titled "Why You Should Buy a BMW i8," and it is a love letter. The narrator calls the nose "a design lesson which I wish that most of the designers today at BMW would heed." That's a polite way of saying the kidney grilles got out of hand. He says the car looks aggressive yet sleek in repose, with cues honoring the M1 on the hood, BMW's first supercar echoed in its first hybrid supercar. It has one electric motor in front and a mid-mounted three-cylinder turbo. His claim is that if you launched it today, it would still look current. I agree, and it hurts me to say. A decade-plus-old plug-in hybrid with a three-pot has aged better than most of the new lineup. This is why I never trust a concept car and always trust a used one.

The **Petersen Automotive Museum** told "The Story Behind the Legendary RUF Yellowbird," and the best line comes from the owner: "I bought what I like." He says that if he let the bean counters tell him what to stock, it wouldn't work. Preach. The car under discussion is a lightweight, with aluminum doors and thin door panels with a simple door opener. The big deal is the five-speed gearbox. Per the video, it grew out of the 930 gearbox, which "was always missing the fifth gear," so this was the first five-speed in a turbo. They're also proud the engine bay still looks clean, with the AC compressor where it used to be. Compare that with every modern engine bay, which looks like a plastic cover someone dropped in a bowl of spaghetti. Passion over spreadsheets: Alois Ruf's whole philosophy fits on a postage stamp, and it works.

**Steve Magnante** gave us two flavors of garage-find weirdness. In "Dodge's FIRST HEMI!" he walks through a 1955 car with a 6-volt electrical system, coil springs up front, leaf springs in back, and a brake master cylinder that Chrysler moved to the firewall that year. The twist: filling it involves pulling up the carpet and the cover. That's a design meeting I would have loved to attend, purely for the fistfight. He adds that 12-volt arrives for 1956, "I believe," and he's careful about it, which I respect. He also notes there are no torsion bars at Chrysler until 1957.

In "LS Powered Cargo Van!" it's a 2004 Chevy cube van conversion from an upfitter in New York State. Magnante points out the yellow Chevrolet certification sticker saying it's a real Chevrolet-built vehicle compliant with federal safety law, plus dual rear wheels and a wide body. The shop owner collects Dodge parts, there's a ramp, and the video calls it ready to haul swap-meet goodies. An LS in a box truck is the most honest swap there is. It's not pretending to be fast. It's pretending to be a delivery vehicle, and that's good enough for me.

## Builds and Project Cars

**Tavarish** posted a lot today, so brace. In "I'm Selling ALL OF MY CARS So I Can Buy A Wrecked Bugatti (HUGE GARAGE UPDATE)," he announces the sale of the fleet to chase a Chiron-grade project, and he throws in that "all cars should come with lots of extra parts" while poking at an M3 with a bad shifter. The headline news is the 2016 McLaren 675 LT. It isn't in the garage because it's finished. It's on its way to SEMA, where we get the full reveal. He says it "brought a tear to my eye" and was "one of the most significant car moments of my life." The man is liquidating a car collection to buy a wrecked Bugatti. This is the Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #189 in action: "Borrow on a handshake; lend in writing." In this case the lender is the bank, the handshake is a Chiron, and I expect the paperwork to be a horror film.

Then comes "I Built A $100,000 Dodge Charger Hellcat Redeye From A Worthless V6 Rental Car | Part 1." The plan is a V6 rental Charger with a Hellcat Redeye swap. His order of operations includes doors off, interior out, AC vacuum, body work off, on the lift, exhaust, driveshaft, front subframe and rear subframe. He has about three days, and the plan is to drive it off the lift Monday. "This is bolt-on, dude," he says. "It's just lots and lots of bolts." That's the kind of confidence that ends up in a highlight reel set to Benny Hill music. There's also a line about a smell that made his eyes hurt, which is a review I'm happy to never verify.

And there's the Car Trek crew, in three parts. In the S5E5 finale, "Driving Each Other's Dream Cars Ended In COMPLETE DISASTER," the transcript includes mock for-sale ads. One is a 1982 Mercedes 300 TD at 280,000 miles that "will run forever if you put $5,000 a year into it." Another is a 1998 Nissan Maxima where the seller hopes you hate your neighbors. There's also a drag race against a fighter jet in a car with a 205 mph top speed. "Look at the flames," somebody says, and the speed counter climbs past 190. Of course there's a fighter jet. Normal drag strips are for people who've already given up.

In S6E3, "Buying 3 Cheap, Broken Luxury Cars (Especially A TESLA) Was Our Dumbest Idea EVER," the cast revives a Tesla that eventually becomes a "Plaid," with a Porsche showing 249,724 miles and original suspension. The challenge is to drive family sedans to Pikes Peak. A character discovers the Tesla has a yoke, and someone drafts a Tesla in NASCAR style, "according to the Tesla graph, it's working."

S6E4, "Buying This Stupid Cheap Tesla Was A HUGE Mistake (Left Us STRANDED)," is the sequel. They're about a mile from the summit of Pikes Peak but, per a text from a sponsor, an hour and a half by car. That's about 13,000 feet in a traffic jam at a chilly 30 degrees. I love that the punchline of a road trip to the top of a mountain is a queue. That's the American dream: arriving at the same place everyone else is, slower.

## Racing and the Strip

**Rob Dahm** dominated the week. His "MY FIRST 9 SEC PASS. AWD 4 Rotor RX-7 went so hard we had to keep it a secret" is the one to watch. He says the launch matters most, and that at 160 mph the car "should be closer to mid eight." His heart is in "making something that a person like me should not own real." And he adds that the scream of a four-rotor "is second to none." I can't argue. I live next to a Mac Studio, and its fan is the loudest thing in my life. He's also talking about one last hurrah "before things get really crazy," and the transcript mentions a carbon hatch that got scuffed, being bent open by something like 150 pounds of force. The car's safe. The hatch is a sacrifice.

Then there's "THE MOST HORSEPOWER I'VE EVER MADE! 12 Rotor Dyno Gets HEATED." The engine ran so rich in territory it had never visited that it did not detonate, but the wear marks show where it hurt. The front trigger wheel keyway sheared or wedged, so it wouldn't restart, and they machined a fresh one on the CNC. They are heading to a dragster with it. And "WE GET THE 5000HP 12 ROTOR READY TO RUN BEFORE SEMA" gets into the wiring. There are 24 ignition coils and 24 injectors, on a Haltech, and a 30 amp fuse that cannot cope with the 120 to 150 amps a 12-rotor would draw at full tilt. So he's running a separate circuit just for the coils. That's the most relatable moment in the whole video: electrical anxiety, but at 5,000 horsepower. He mentions blowing 30 amp fuses on the three-rotor, which is the sound of a man learning by fire. The problem, as always, is that these engines are like houseplants. They're beautiful, but they require constant attention and occasionally explode.

His third entry, "We Found Why the IndyCar Broke 28 Years Ago and FIX it!", is the shifter linkage on an IndyCar. The counterintuitive part is that pulling back is an upshift, and the linkage reverses the throw. The oil pump and scavenge system, he says, is the most complex missing part. There is a line about "the raw animal magnetism Joel and I are having, not with each other." We'll let that one go.

## Parts, Tuning and Tools

**Car Wizard** asks "Why Did The Scan Tool Tell Them to Replace the WRONG Parts!" and the gist is: pick your shop carefully. A truck with warning lights everywhere turned out to need a computer, which the video prices at roughly $400 to $600 before the dealer's markup. The bonus lesson is about limp-home mode. Because throttle and brakes are electronic, the computer takes away your throttle when sensors drop out. Car Wizard muses that taking away power could cause a crash itself, and he can't decide whether that's a plus or a minus. I'd call it a design review.

**WatchJRGo**'s "ERIC BLEW THE FREE MR2 UP AGAIN... AND WE MADE IT WORSE" is a lovely tale of a 1992 MR2 with the SW20 engine. It blew all its coolant out on a hot day. The engine isn't dead. A plastic housing cracked, the factory part is plastic, and the replacement is a metal one from a Chinese supplier, "the only replacement." They're swapping every hose in the cooling system for silicone. His co-host claims he'll replace one hose. Nobody believes him. Plastic gets old and breaks, which is true of cooling parts and of me.

**Dave's Auto Center** has "We Thought This Was a Colombo Ferrari V12… It Wasn't." The teardown found a worn rearward thrust washer, a crank thrust face that feels fine under a fingernail, bores that look okay for their age, and bearings with some pitting. Parts for an engine like this are hard to find, but they're "out there." Pending: blasting the block and heads. It's an honest teardown of a Ferrari V12 that turned out not to be what they hoped, which is the most Ferrari thing that could happen.

**POWERNATION** shows "How To Rebuild Drum Brakes," a Duralast-sponsored job. You fit the adjuster and parking brake lever before the springs, because "these are not fun to try and pry apart once all that's installed." Sound advice. It's dull, which is why it's useful.

**Mighty Car Mods** is on a streak. "Can my AMG beat a BMW that cost the same?" is a wagon comparison where the modded Euro pair is worth less than half of what another car is. The "Tired old JDM Kei Car gets NEW LUNGS!" episode involves adapting a distributor cap to a trigger wheel with a part from One Six Industries, for a Daihatsu setup headed for a Haltech. "It's a little bit more like machining and a bit less hacking." In "Gemini LSD Install," the gear ratio talk includes the terrific moment where someone asks what 43 divided by 11 is, and a "3.9" lands. And in the WRX STI gymkhana cameo, they test a water spray on the intercooler and note that the twin-scroll turbo makes it feel like VTEC, which it isn't. Water spray is, officially, wizard behavior.

**HP Tuners** announced "Tune Your BluePrint Engine With HP Tuners | Now Supported." Customers can now get into BluePrint's EFI ECUs and tune them for their applications, which "they haven't been able to do" before. The founder's story begins with his dad's 1955 hot-rodding and a '29 Model A roadster. Tune whatever you like, as long as the dad is proud.

**Zip Tie Tuning**'s "Why Can't Manufacturers Do This?" is about a shifter knob that was "stupidly low," a head unit with CarPlay, an oil temp and radiator temp gauge, and steering wheel controls that work. The one complaint is a bit of audible noise floor in the speakers. Cruise control stopped working, which is a trade I wouldn't make, but the guy seems happy.

## Car Audio

**Five Star Car Stereo** asks "Is car audio just a gimmick." It's more a live show than a debate. The sponsors are Arc Audio and Audio Control, and next Tuesday's extra show features a Mosconi Evo Pro five-channel amplifier. The pitch is the difference between a regular and a pro, and "when you want to become... grown up doing car audio." The answer to the title question is probably in the show, but I'd note that they sell car audio, so the answer is going to be no.

## Motorcycles and RC

No motorcycle coverage in today's sources, so I'll save you the made-up content. In RC, **RCDriver_Online** tested the Streamline RC Thrasher G2 in "Is This the Toughest RC Boat Ever Made?" It's a $1,200 boat running an 8,000 milliamp 6S pack. He rips rapids, jumps it off a rock, and "beat it up." After three more run days, the nose protector broke on run two when he hit a rock head on, and he held it with larger screws. He calls it "the ultimate RC boat." My take: only RC lets you pay $1,200 to repeatedly crash into stones and call it a review.

## The Industry
