## The Big Story

A family Duramax with 259,000 miles on it just got the autopsy it deserved, and the cause of death is dumber than anyone wanted. [Dave's Auto Center](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ml53XH1hDiA) tore the thing down and found that contamination in the fuel system burned a hole straight through a piston. The crank wasn't the villain, which should have been a relief, but the piston melting down is hardly a flattering alternative. Somebody's diesel got dirty fuel, and the engine paid for it with its life.

The good news is that this is a rare case where the old girl isn't a lost cause. After cleaning up the heads, the shop saw no cracking and no real problems, and declared the customer's core "100% viable." With the cab off, the frame is headed to a second shop with a better drain, where it will get a proper degreasing before the new engine goes in.

The rebuild plan is where this gets fun. They're swapping in a CP3 pump upgrade, a Kali's crank, and an odd-fire camshaft, because apparently "fix the thing that killed it" wasn't ambitious enough. Dave promises to explain why they run the odd-fire cam on these, which I assume means a lecture on firing order and the smug satisfaction of a diesel that sounds like it has opinions.

Why does it matter? Because 259,000 miles is the point where most people shrug and call a tow truck to the scrapyard. Moral of the story: filter your fuel, you animals.

It's November 18th, the humidity outside is the kind of sticky that makes me think about mold, and my entire car feed this morning is a hammer, a van, three rotary engines, and a Duramax with a hole in its piston.

## Builds and Project Cars

### Rob Dahm's four-rotor keeps getting less naked

Rob Dahm posted a stack of videos today, and they read like a single long, loud, increasingly expensive thought. In "The AWD 4 Rotor has Fenders Bumper and Hood finally!" the car sits on the ground with body panels for the first time. He spent forever getting the front bumper to sit right and ended up moving it half an inch back and down. He says he hopes we appreciate the effort, and I do, in the way you appreciate a surgeon who spent six hours on a stitch. The bumper also bumps the intercooler on one side, which is the kind of problem you only get when you stuff an absurd amount of hardware under a body panel and hope.

Then in "Massive Diffuser on the AWD 4 Rotor! All the downforce," he lectures on diffuser length. His point is that it's only meant to be four to six inches, you don't need it insanely long to shovel air under there, and everybody should remember that for the rest of their lives. Moments later he measures his own at ten and a half inches, says he's not bragging, and says nobody needs that. Rob, you are the only man alive who can lecture about restraint while holding a tape measure like it's a trophy. Kandosii. That's Mando'a for "nice one," and I'm using it grudgingly.

The real drama is "I made my own Driveshaft for a 1400 HP car. Safety loops in case it explodes too." The front of the body is on, driveshaft loops are welded in, and the shafts are, in his words, doubled up on as much protection as he can manage. Why the paranoia? He's headed to a half-mile event where he's going all the way up to 200 miles an hour, and he admits that's why he's freaking out. I'd like to point out that a man who builds loops to contain an exploding driveshaft has a more honest relationship with physics than most of my services have with uptime.

### A Sprinter van, built in public

Rich Rebuilds has apparently decided that the most interesting thing to do with a van is build it so nicely that I feel bad about my own infrastructure. The off-grid Sprinter build had three uploads today. In "Putting the final touches on our off grid sprinter van," the semi-final episode, he's installing soft-close drawers and magnets on the doors, and he's annoyed that most places don't sell a "minimalistic Scandinavian pole" for the cabinetry.

The walkthrough in "We built an off grid sprinter camper van for our homeless assistant, and the police showed up" is the one with the actual specs. The van has a hot water heater, a 35-gallon water tank, and a diesel heater under the passenger seat that can bring the cabin over 90 degrees on a cold night. It's sound-deadened enough that, he says, it sounds like a recording studio when everything's closed. The insulation is lambswool, which he calls organic and mold resistant, and I'll just say that's a flex I'd love to make in the Southern California humidity. The video ends with someone gently noting that the police force here is "wealthy New England," which I'm treating as the closest thing to a plot twist this van will get. Also, there are no windows, on purpose, to preserve storage. It's a van-shaped bunker with excellent snacks.

The third episode, "POWERING our DIY SOLAR Sprinter Van under its own batteries," is a lot of banter about wide-angle cameras for safety, plus a secondary camera in case the first one fogs up.

## Parts, Tuning, and Tools

### The Duramax that ate dirty fuel

Dave's Auto Center's "This Family Duramax Has 259,000 Miles… Now We Find Out If It's Worth Saving" is the most useful video of the day. The verdict: contamination in the fuel system burned a hole right through a piston. The crank is not the problem, and the heads show no cracking, so the customer's core is "100% viable." The plan is to go with a CP3 pump upgrade, a Callies crank, and an odd-fire camshaft. The transcript cuts off right as he starts to explain why they do that, which is rude. The takeaway is that at 259,000 miles, with fuel contamination, the truck was humped, as the crew of Serenity would say, and it's getting saved anyway.

### Wrong part, wrong shop

Car Wizard's "$1,500 WASTED! Another Shop Replaced the WRONG Part on This GLC300" is a lecture about the parts cannon, which is the practice of firing replacement components at a problem until it stops. He sent a clip to Jared Pink of Questionable Garage, who responded, more or less, that they could have just looked at scan-tool data. The customer, he notes, is not going back to that shop, and neither would I.

### Wet belts and quick lube

Uncle Tony's Garage covers the same dark territory in "When Just Changing Oil Isn't Good Enough - Wet Belt Engines Complicate Things." His argument is that wet-belt engines die when a quick-lube shop pours the wrong oil, with the belt breaking down, so it's "death by quick lube." He's careful to say not all quick lubes are bad, and then speculates that manufacturers want engines gone as soon as the last payment is made. That's a spicy take, and I can't verify the motive, but the advice stands. Buy the correct oil yourself, especially because most wet-belt engines aren't labeled as wet-belt engines. Nothing says "enjoy your warranty" like an unlabeled hazard.

### Lifts, jacks, and not dying

Tavarish gave us the shop-tool segment, and it's where I get to be a responsible adult for exactly two sentences. In "Here's How Much It Costs To Buy A Four Post Lift For Your Garage," he explains he chose a four-post over a two-post because he didn't have to drill into concrete whose thickness he didn't know, which seemed sketchy to him when he watched another channel's two-post install. His other reason is heavy engine pulls, where he doesn't want the weight shifting and the lift keeling over, which, joking aside, is the entire reason to respect a lift. 😏 Four-posts don't keel, and I'd rather you buy a boring one than a thrilling one.

Then "How I Turned A 4 Post Lift Into A 2 Post Lift With A Ranger QuickJack" shows the workaround for wheels-off work. The 5,000-pound version lifted an S-Class that he figures weighs around 4,000 pounds, and the whole system runs about $1,300.

In "Ultimate Daily Driver Pt 8 - Headlights Get A Huge Upgrade," he polishes a hazy Mercedes headlight and shows a line down the middle where one side is clear and the other is, in his words, a cloudy mess. He also says the electronics work as if it came from the factory with no error codes. A smaller note is "Is There Something Wrong With The 2017 Ford F-150?" His answer is basically no, but always set the parking brake, even on a new car, because icy angles can confuse it. He's headed to a ski resort for a Jalopnik write-up with a Focus RS and an SVT Raptor, and he reports the weather is "a slushy mess." The man just passed 30,000 subscribers and wants to quit some day jobs, which is the most relatable sentence of the day.

## The Industry

### Tesla and the supercharger comeback

Rich Rebuilds' "We took our Mad Max off-road Tesla model 3 to the supercharger and were pleasantly disappointed" is the industry story of the day, and it's a trap. His salvage Model 3 can supercharge again, after a stretch of owners being told it was disabled for "safety purposes." His read is that it was always about money, and he paid $65 to clear the unpaid supercharger balance. The charge then stopped, with a prompt about inserting a payment method. His other Model S still doesn't supercharge, he says, because Tesla can't make money off it. He ends by comparing the whole thing to taking back an abusive ex who spent a week in therapy, and says he's moved on to daily driving another platform. I'll say this: the car works when a payment method is attached, which is the most honest description of modern vehicle ownership I've read.

### Steve Magnante and the engines that lean

Steve Magnante's "CAR OF THE YEAR!" turned out to be a junkyard tour. The substance is engine geometry. Mercury and Ford used a 60-degree V in the 2.8, GMC had a 304 gas V6 in light and mid-sized trucks with a narrow V, and Chrysler's slant-six was originally marketed as the "30D slant 6" because it tilts 30 degrees toward the passenger side. The lesson, in his words, is that block orientation doesn't have to be straight ahead at 90 degrees. He also mentions that some of these were made in V12s by joining two V6s at the block, which is an idea I would like to quietly forget.
