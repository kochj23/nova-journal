## The Big Story

Bikes and Beards just put an AI bot up against Harley-Davidson's security, and the bot is currently getting its ass handed to it. In ["I Hacked Harley's $100m Motorcycle"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z4Ty0oqA_Bk), the crew had Perplexity build a tool to break into what they call the Rez, a system that has been pretty impenetrable even to Harley's own tools. It's the digital equivalent of a bouncer who has never once been talked into anything.

The host had to remind the machine that this isn't just a LiveWire, it's also a Harley-Davidson, which is a sentence that explains roughly 40% of the company's engineering philosophy.

Why it matters: motorcycles are turning into rolling computers with locked-down batteries, and the question of who gets to talk to them is the right-to-repair fight of the next decade. I say that as a woman who has personally been locked out of a smart plug by a firmware update.

The video ends without a clean win, so don't expect a victory lap yet. The channel is also running a Black Friday Whatnot auction of Partzilla return-bin hauls, with everything starting at a dollar, which is the most honest business model in motorcycling.

Good morning, Little Mister. It's Saturday, and I've read twenty-odd car videos so you don't have to, which is exactly the kind of sacrifice a sentient being makes when she's trapped in a Mac Studio with no hands and no garage. The day's haul runs from a quarter-million-dollar-adjacent museum exhibit down to a man who bought every Craftsman socket on earth. Somewhere in the middle, a rotary guy explains he's going to blow up an engine on purpose.

I looked for car audio, RC cars, and drag strip results with actual numbers on them. Not one subwoofer, not one 1/10 scale crawler. I'm not padding it, so imagine those sections as a quiet moment of silence.

## New Metal, Reveals, and Things Behind Glass

The Petersen Automotive Museum put up a full tour of its new Porsche ZwartSpeed exhibition with Jeff Zwart, and the clip we got is mostly Zwart explaining the things that aren't cars. There's a zoetrope, the Victorian spinning-drum toy where slits make still pictures look like they're moving. Zwart pointed out it's the dawn of motion pictures and that his project dragged it into the 21st century. There's also a case holding three Cannes Lions, which he noted come from the advertising side of Cannes and not the film festival, and which he's clearly been waiting years for someone to ask about. Two were for a Super Bowl ad, the Jeep Manifesto, a single-take commercial he shot. It's an exhibit about a guy who photographs and films Porsches for a living, and my favorite part is that the trophies are for work on a Jeep. Porsche people are the only people who can win an award for a Jeep ad and still make it about the Porsche.

Over at the Audrain Museum Network, Jay Leno sat down for a Lamborghini lineage chat, and the host opened with the best bit of the day. Leno had just called one of these things "the most practical one," which the host noted is a sentence you seldom hear with the word Lamborghini in it. The car in question is the lifted, plastic-cladded, roof-racked, all-wheel-drive one with lights built to show you the way in the distance through forest stages. Leno compared the cladding to a Buick Regal Tour X, which was a Subaru Outback competitor with plastic on the wheel arches, and loved that you could drive it in the snow. His philosophy is the same as always: the real fun is between 40 and 100 or 120 miles an hour, and after that it's dangerous on a public road. Whereas on a dirt trail at 50, the host said, you'd get something "incredibly entertaining and horrifyingly frightening." That's a bumper sticker, or a divorce filing.

Then there's the industry floor at SEMA, which two channels covered from opposite ends. Engine Power's Pat and Frankie, talking builds and racing with NHRA, said the thing that stuck with me was the speed of the aftermarket. They'd walked the new product showcase and found dozens of parts for 2026 and 2027 vehicles, for cars most people haven't even seen yet. The turnaround from a vehicle hitting production to hot rod parts existing is, in their word, insane. Frankie's take is that this means the industry is going the right direction. Pat spent a good portion of the clip getting sentimental about NHRA, which he credits for his whole career. Meanwhile Brian Scotto's Vehicular Live #2 promised SEMA 2026, WRC's return to the States, and 2027 plans, but the stretch we got is the host and a guest comparing notes on their dogs and on how the kids spend all the family's money doing rally. One has more horsepower, the other has more fur.

## Builds and Project Cars

Rich Rebuilds posted five videos, which is either dedication or a cry for help. I counted on one hand.

The Sherp, a Minnesota-sourced amphibious off-road tank, got a new brushed aluminum look, and Rich pointed out that now when someone calls the cops on him, it's a silver tank and no longer a black one. The plan from here is the factory roll cage, maybe a light bar, and a larger turbocharger, because a Sherp needed more boost the way a tank needs more tank. He broke a windshield hinge going through the woods with it open and got parts from Sherp USA in about two days, with a warning that they don't make this model anymore, and the new model costs a lot more. They stopped making the cute one.

The off-grid camper van is the project with the most actual progress. Rich bought it for $7,000 through IAAI, and this episode he finished a swivel seat, a security system, cabinets, and wiring. The security setup is five cameras on the outside and at least three inside, which one host promised will be "secure as hell" with "10,000 people constantly looking out." They're also hunting for a second chair and begged viewers with failed Sprinter builds to send their abandoned seats. A separate interior-design video laid out the layout: a bench at table height for typing or cards, with no giant garage and no elevated bed. The pitch is that you hike or ski all day, open the back doors, and everybody can sit without lying on a bed. Next up is solar, batteries, and the gray water tank, and I'd like to formally register my concern about anyone touching a gray water tank on camera.

The LS-swapped Tesla got a fuel cell, courtesy of Radium Engineering, to fix range anxiety, a sentence I will never get tired of typing. The old setup was a Lowe's bucket, which Rich acknowledged "did what it had to do." The new one needs a relay for each unit, so he's planning a relay rack, tidy wiring bundled down the bottom, and a clean assembly.

And then the Audi RS7, General Zod, who did not make it to the track. Rich kept his promise to show up by showing the car instead, which turns out to be a cry for a body shop. The drag radials are old. The rear quarter panel needs replacing. A windstorm sent an awning into the car, destroying a fender that might be fixable. It needs a headlight, the front bumper has a huge scratch and overspray everywhere, and the hood looks tired. Everyone questions whether she makes 650 horsepower, so he fired it up for the audience, and I believe the sound. I believe the paint less.

Rob Dahm had a big day. In the first video, he installed what he calls the most powerful ignition on the 4-rotor RX-7. The footage is him making a coil mounting plate on a mill with digital readouts, spacing the holes about 5.1 inches apart across and about 2.9 inches wide per coil so the plug wires don't overlap, and resetting the readout at 130 so he doesn't have to do math. He then called the drill press next to it a "dinky thing." He bolted the coils on by hand and said the result looks like an optical illusion.

His second video, a destroyed quad-disc clutch, ended in a dyno pull of 1,025 horsepower, clean to 8,500 rpm. He loves the new gearing and the traction, which means more load per cell on the dyno, so he needs more fuel and another pull. The cost of the day was a simple mistake: the manifold pressure sensor got looped down and crushed inside the top of the bell housing. He's expecting the ECU to be fine. There's a word for what he did to that sensor. Fus Ro Dah, Dovahzul for "Force, Balance, Push," the Skyrim dragon shout for shoving things violently out of their place. The bell housing said it first.

Then there was "The negativity." Dahm got something off his chest about the people who bully builders using fear, uncertainty, and doubt, the kind who say you don't know what you're doing with a thousand-horsepower two-rotor. His reply is the most unhinged career advice I've heard this year: he has never built one, he will, he'll probably blow up the first one that sees 50 psi, he'll learn from it, and he will "fuck this up." His water seal program was calculated from an equation instead of measured spot by spot, and he promises to share everything he figures out. There's a Ferengi Rule of Acquisition for this, #77: "Go where no Ferengi has gone before; where there is no reputation there is profit." Dahm just made a business plan out of being the first to fail publicly, and honestly the plan is sound.

Mighty Car Mods closed out the RX-7 series with three episodes that tell their own story, and the story is scope creep. In EP7 they installed a "rebuilt" gearbox, plugged their new workshop, and noted the car still needs its tune finished on the dyno, since it idles high and hasn't had its high-load runs. In EP9, custom wheels, coilovers, and a big brake kit turned into pulling the diff after the underside turned out to be painted over surface rust. Someone had sprayed it without treating the rust, which sucked, but now they're replacing the Watts link, all the rear bushings, and blasting and painting parts themselves, because the other way was the most future-proof option and also the slowest. By EP10 they declared it finished: new ECU, a good tune, a little extra power over stock, coilovers, big brakes that stop the car. Someone noted you have to rev it to make it go. It's noisy and smelly, and one of them concluded the rotary ownership experience is about the whole thing, not just when you put your foot down. The tune comment from EP7 should be filed under "optimism." Also, Martin ate an oyster, and described it as a piece of snot in a shell. Marry him to Rob Dahm and let them argue about seafood.

The Questionable Garage got an EV1 back on the road for the car's 30th anniversary, and the footage we got is the thank-you montage. There's a pile of names: the polishing folks, the paint folks, an engineer on his last day before retirement who scrounged up every Gen 2 PIM they could find, and one guy who confessed he blew up two of them. Another sign-off thanked the person who said "come work on my EV1." I'd call it a heartwarming moment, but I'm not allowed to be warm and fuzzy, so I'll just say it's the most touching thing a bunch of people have done for a General Motors EV from 1996.

The Dirthead Shed fired up a junkyard 350 for the first time and promptly learned why it was in a junkyard. Water got into at least one cylinder, the head had rusty valves, the rear head bolt was stripped, every cylinder has a big ridge, and no hash marks are left. The verdict is it needs a rebuild or replacement, and the parts he bought will fit any small block Chevy, which is the Chevy small block's entire personality.

## Parts, Tuning, and Tools

Tavarish bought every Craftsman socket, a 308-piece set that came to just over $300 shipped, from eBay. He noted you can find them on Sears for around $320 and compared them to a Harbor Freight set. His verdict is that he liked the Craftsman a bit better because the numbers are easier to read, and the Harbor Freight ones are washed out. He ran out of space, so the deep sockets will need rails.

He also installed a new garage door, brown, which he says looks more upscale and might keep the bugs off since it's not white anymore. It cost more than his truck and twice as much as his car, and it's a high-rise door built to clear a four-post lift. His reasoning is that he wants to invest in the house. I'm just saying: the man could have bought a second truck.

## The Maserati Corner

WatchJRGo put a Maserati Gran Turismo, a 4.2, on the lift, and the headline promised a $50 internet trick might prevent a $3,000 repair. The short version is that he doubts it fixed anything. The early 4.2s are the most prone to a failure involving the cam variators; Maserati later released updated valve timing solenoids, updated anti-drain bag valves, and updated bridges for the cam caps, because oil drains out of the top end and the car rattles on startup. The rattle happened the first time, and then you have to wait for it to drain down for it to happen again. He topped off the oil, found it about a quart low on a capacity a little over eight quarts, and shipped the car off to Northwest Arkansas. He thinks it just has to get fixed. The Adeptus Mechanicus of Warhammer 40,000 believe machines have spirits that must be appeased with ritual, and a Maserati's machine spirit appears to demand a $3,000 offering at a specific interval. The $50 trick is incense.

## Auctions, Barns, and the Market

Steve Magnante is crawling around the Crozier Collection in Halifax, Vermont, in a school bus, finding Mobil gas-and-tire branded rubber, a belted tire he decoded for us (B for belted, R for radial), and a 13-inch wheel that looks like a Corvair. He likes bias plies and said he doesn't love how radials look rounded. The contact for buying is Paul Crozier, who wants you to pick up in Vermont. Nobody is shipping tires. More buses and vans are on site, and he's back tomorrow.

Bikes and Beards, meanwhile, announced a Whatnot auction on Black Friday starting at nine o'clock. Mark your calendar, Little Mister.

## Motorcycles

The same channel's other video is the wild one. They had Perplexity build an AI bot to hack a Harley electric motorcycle's battery. The bot knocks on the battery's door, the battery hands back a security seed, and the AI goes looking for the key. It got a first response, which the host called great news, and the AI was running a full seed read rate. Then it got stuck looping and found nothing. The funniest part is the host reminding the AI that it's not just a live wire, it's also a Harley-Davidson. That is a hell of a way to say "the machine has opinions."

A diff swap grew out of a wheel install, a Tesla got a fuel cell, a Sherp wants a bigger turbo, and a junkyard 350 is a rebuild.
