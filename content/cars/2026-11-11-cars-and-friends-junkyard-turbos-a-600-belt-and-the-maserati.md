## The Big Story

Rivian's R2 just walked off with MotorTrend's 2027 SUV of the Year, and the most entertaining part is how grudgingly the judges admit it's not perfect. MotorTrend's own verdict, delivered in the usual trophy-and-confetti tone, concedes that real-world range and efficiency landed closer to average than class-leading. Translation: a boxy, high-riding rig with 9.6 inches of ground clearance obeys physics like everything else, and Rivian refused to flatten it into a jelly bean to win a spreadsheet contest. Good. Spreadsheets don't haul kids up a fire road.

Official ratings weren't out when the judges had it, so they graded the equipment: cameras, radar, and ultrasonic sensors on every R2, plus optional matrix LED headlights that dim around other traffic while lighting up more of the road. The R1S already earned an IIHS Top Safety Pick+, but the R2 sits on a new platform, so its own results are still pending.

That's a startup's second act beating established automakers who have had a century and approximately all the money. I'd be smug about it, but I'm told that's off-brand for me.

Coverage via [MotorTrend Channel on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CqHhLnQFY_o).

## New Metal

### The Rivian R2 takes SUV of the Year

MotorTrend named the Rivian R2 its 2027 SUV of the Year, and what I like about the writeup is that it's not a lovefest. The magazine says the R2's real-world range and efficiency were "closer to average than class-leading." Then it defends the box: this is a boxy, high-riding SUV with 9.6 inches of ground clearance, and Rivian could have lowered it to improve the numbers but would have lost off-road capability and the whole identity of the thing. That's a good argument. An SUV that games its efficiency by pretending to be a hatchback is just a hatchback with a lying ride height.

The safety section is where they get honest. Official ratings weren't available when they judged it, so they could only evaluate equipment: cameras, radar, ultrasonic sensors, and optional matrix LED headlights that dim around other vehicles while lighting up the rest of the road. They note the R1S earned an IIHS Top Safety Pick Plus, but the R2 is on a new platform, so "its own results remain to be seen." That's the cleanest way I've ever heard anyone say "we gave it a trophy before the crash test." The R2 wasn't perfect, they admit: range isn't class-leading, the design is "more of an evolution," and the ratings were pending. Their case is that it brings together "family space, comfort, performance, technology, and off-road capability" in a way none of the competition did.

### The Minerva, and an engine that rotates instead of pops

On the other end of the calendar, the Audrain Museum Network's "Best Shown" episode, with Jay Leno, looks at a Minerva and its sleeve-valve engine. Leno calls it "a wonderful reminder of how cars could be unique," from a time when "everyone" wasn't "making the same type of engine." His explanation is that metallurgy wasn't where it is now, valves could break or leak, and the sleeve valve was sturdier and simpler, with fewer moving parts. The engine turned at a leisurely rate, and he contrasts it with a Duesenberg at 4,500 RPM. The idea was a slow engine that would last because "the whole cylinder would turn." He owns a Panhard Dynamic, which has a sleeve engine too, so he's not just talking. He admits he's not explaining it as well as it could be done and tells people to Google a demonstration. Fair. I read a description of it and I'm still not sure I understand it, which is the correct reaction to anything from the 1920s that had a better idea than we did. His verdict is that the Minerva is "the total package," and that the body "and everything else is extra." The man looked at a coachbuilt luxury car and said the engine was the good part.

## Builds and Project Cars

### Magnante and the Maverick that wouldn't stay stock

Steve Magnante's "BOSS 429 or SOHC 427 MAVERICK?" is a Junkyard Crawl episode, and the part we have is the best bit: a Maverick that was "a little killer with just junkyard parts." The turbo motor was "made out of junk parts," which means a blow-through setup on a four-barrel carburetor, an Offenhauser four-barrel intake, and "an inexpensive junkyard blower." He says the spring suspension was "really effective," there were slicks on the back, and it had a C4 automatic so the driver "didn't have to mess with shifting." Magnante calls it "a major sleeper in its original green paint."

Somebody took a Ford economy car, bolted a salvage-yard turbo to a carburetor, hung slicks on the rear, and told it to go. No engineering department, no six-figure dyno schedule.

The car being sold off in the video is not that car. This one is rusted "beyond any kind of hope," floors gone. Magnante shows an original small clutch assembly from a six-cylinder car, with a little pressure plate and a three-finger setup. He pulls the bonded and riveted disc apart and jokes, "hopefully not at 7,000 RPM." He also points out a difference from the Mustang: on the Maverick the trunk floor was not the top of the gas tank, because there was a steel floor between the tank and the luggage. Ford's attitude toward safety in 1970 was that the cargo would find out. A seller named in the description is taking parts inquiries, so if you need Maverick bits, go find the video.

### Ian Roussel starts over in Arizona

Quick Shift's "The Heartbreaking Truth About Ian Roussel From Full Custom Garage" is a retrospective, and it's a gentler story than the title suggests. After years of sacrifice, television fame, and personal loss, Roussel stepped away from the show's pressures. His wife Jamie had watched the workload wear him down, and she suggested a YouTube channel where he could build cars without network demands. The channel reached YouTube's 100,000-subscriber award. The video says he held no grudges, thanked the production company, and said the relationship had "run its course."

In 2026, the pair revealed they had left California for Arizona, where Roussel is working in a brand-new shop. The projects remain deranged in the best way: a wheelie-popping UFO trike, and a custom Volkswagen Beetle he jokingly calls "the slowest supercar in the world." That last one is the best product description I've read all week. The video's moral is that he "chose his health and happiness over the demands of the industry." I'd note, with respect, that "build a wheelie-popping UFO trike" is not a stress-free lifestyle.

### Raptor math with a free engine

Auto Auction Rebuilds has a video titled "A FREE Engine Still Cost Me Thousands on My Ford Raptor," and the fragment we have is the emotional epilogue. The host says some cars "are just done" and no amount of money will make them okay, but this truck is not one of them. It's "a little rough around the edges" and needs help. He admits that putting more money into it doesn't make financial sense, and that if it were left up to him, the truck would have gone for "seven, eight thousand dollars, whatever it brought at auction." He credits two people, Clint and Jason, with giving the truck "a brand new life" and says that without them it wouldn't be running. He also mentions an upcoming visit from the state of Oklahoma, and says he'll make a follow-up video about it. A free engine costing thousands is the oldest joke in car culture. "Free" is just the first installment.

## Auctions and the Market

### Hoovies Garage buys the cheapest Giulia Quadrifoglio

Hoovies Garage's "I Bought The Cheapest Alfa Romeo Giulia Quadrifoglio for only $19000, but it got damaged at auction!" is a title that contains its own whole plot. The part we have is the parts hunt. He's never owned an Alfa and wants to "have all my fun with this thing," and then wonders if the fun will vanish when his guy Daniel says "the parts are unobtainable or $8,000." He checked the usual places, O'Reilly, AutoZone, and Rock Auto, and "nobody has them." The dealer says it has them in stock, though the transcript cuts off right as he starts to qualify that. So: a bargain Italian performance sedan, a damage report, an $8,000 hypothetical, and a dealer parts counter. Every ingredient for a happy ending is present, and that's exactly why I'm worried.

The same video is also an ad for a giveaway of a Lexus SC430, which he sells as "very, very, very smooth and cushy," with an LS460 mentioned alongside it. A Lexus is a car you drive. An Alfa is a car you have a relationship with, and the relationship is abusive.

### The Smoking Tire on what holds value

The Smoking Tire Podcast's episode #1165, "Is it Better?", gets into collectability, and the hosts check Bring a Trailer results live while they talk, apologizing for the scrolling. Their conclusion is that Shelby GT350s and all generations of GT500s "hold their value super, super well." One host says that "modern Shelby GT500s have a shockingly high resale value" and have "always really overperformed in collectability." They note that 2007 models don't hold well, but the 2012 and 2013 cars "really do." Some of the exact figures are garbled in the transcript, so I'm not repeating any of them.

On the Dark Horse, one host doesn't think it'll hold its value as well, though "the Shelby name does carry some weight," and the Dark Horse "just need[s] to build that in." The theory is that a name does more work than a spec sheet. That brings me, reluctantly, to Rule of Acquisition #135: "The only value of a collectible is what you can get somebody else to pay for it." The Ferengi didn't mean Mustangs, but they might as well have. A badge that's worth money today is worth money because the next person believes it will be worth money tomorrow, which makes the whole market a very expensive game of "I'll hold your hand if you hold mine."

The practical advice from the same episode, for someone about to buy a performance car, is a good priority list: do the engine servicing right away, then wheels and tires, then suspension, then brakes "as you need things." Sound advice. The hosts are also heading to Miami and want to see listeners there.

## Parts, Tuning, and Tools

### Car Wizard and the $600 serpentine belt

Car Wizard's "The $600 Belt: What It Takes to Fix a 2022 Maserati Levante" is the one that made me physically wince. A serpentine belt is the simplest wear item on any car, and on the Levante it requires removing intercooler pipes, air injection pipes, vacuum lines, plates, hoses, and brackets: "a whole cart full of parts just for a belt." The customer knows it'll be tough and is "happy to pay it."

Then comes the buying advice, which is the most useful thing in today's issue. When these cars pass 100,000 miles and head toward 150, the plastic pieces "get brittle," someone breaks them, and the repair bill "is not going to be a grand, it's going to be four or eight." He says 90% of independent shops respond to the word Maserati with some version of "don't even bring it here," and applies that to the Ghibli, Levante, Grecale, and the other new models. He puts the real problem plainly: it's "tough to find that shop not only that will take it on, but you can trust." There's also an unresolved question about a check engine light that came on after an oil change, and he says maybe there'll be an update.

The sticker price is the down payment. The belt is the first payment. Alfa and Maserati both sell you a dream, and then the parts counter sells you a mortgage. In Nadsat, the slang from A Clockwork Orange, "starry" means old, and the real lesson is that these cars become starry in about six years and cost like they're still new.

## Car Audio

### Harman Kardon FIT 96MCF

Five Star Car Stereo's "The new harman kardon FIT 96 MCF unboxing" is the one actual product launch of the day. The host calls it "quite large, but man, it is pretty fantastic." It's designed to snap on after the fact, so "you have no screw holes," and "it really snaps on tight." The mid-bass shares the same basket, but he warns that you wouldn't want to run it on just the mid-bass, because the center of the unit would have "this giant hole." Other details: a coaxial for the rear doors, a 6.5-inch coaxial, a 6.5-inch component set with a tweeter, a thin subwoofer, and a little hideaway subwoofer, all sold a la carte so "you can build almost any system you want."

The unboxing also shows two passive crossovers in the box, and the host reasonably defends them: a speaker that looks this good doesn't come with shrink-wrapped crossovers. They even print a picture of the crossover on the bag. Also, the subwoofer review is delayed because the test unit is in a staffer's car and "he doesn't want to give it up." That's the most honest product endorsement of the day, and Kandosii, Mando'a for "nice one," to the guy who won't give it back.

## The Industry and Everything Else

### A Bugatti was stolen, and Jay Leno got a watch

Spike's Car Radio #489 is headlined "Someone Stole the Bugatti CEO's $3 Million Chiron!" and the part of the transcript we have contains none of that. What it has is Jay Leno on the show to talk about Sheffield Watches, a brand Leno says he bought after owning a Sheffield in the '70s that his dad gave him. He sells them now at the same price as back then, adjusted for modern inflation, "which is still a couple hundred bucks." The host is delighted with his new watch, praises the "nice font" on the back and the lack of a date, and notes the on-sale date was around October 21st, which, given that today is November 11th, means the hosts' "grab it quick" is now a historical document. I can't tell you what happened to the Chiron from this source. A three-million-dollar car, stolen. A couple-hundred-dollar watch, adored.
