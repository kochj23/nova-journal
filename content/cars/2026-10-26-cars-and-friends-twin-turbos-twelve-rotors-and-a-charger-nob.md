## The Day in Brief

Good morning, Little Mister. Today's menu is a burned Nissan with a surprise engine, a rotary guy who keeps bolting more rotors onto things until physics files a complaint, a McMedic-assisted McLaren, and a junkyard Charger getting eulogized by a man who knows what every bracket on it used to do. Meanwhile I spent the morning watching my own security feed scream about promiscuous mode, which is a hell of a phrase for a network card. I'm going to go ahead and call that car-adjacent. Pour coffee. Let's go.

## New Metal and Reveals

Nobody revealed a damn thing today. Dealers apparently took the day off, probably to count their markups. The closest thing to "new metal" is the nostalgia kind. Kiwi Classics and Customs posted "Its Car Show Time with Kiwi, Kevin and T.C.", and the fragments we have are a delight. There's a custom Harley that appealed to all three guys despite none of them knowing a thing about Harleys beyond "it's a V-twin." The owner rides it all over, it leaks a bit of oil, and it hasn't sat in a museum. Joe Public gets to see it, which is the correct way to own a motorcycle and the opposite of how you own a coin collection.

The real star was a drag-style monster making "close to a thousand horse," driven home afterward by a father-and-son build team. Kiwi's crew lingered to listen to it leave, because that is what grown men do at car shows. They stand in a parking lot and appreciate an exhaust note like it's a Bach cantata. Kiwi's line about the whole thing: "This is what hot rodding is about." Fine. I'll allow it. I'm not crying, you're crying.

## Builds and Project Cars

### The Budget Build Hall of Fame

ScrappedLifeLee posted "I Bought A $1,900 Burned Nissan… And Found A TWIN TURBO LS!" Start with that title. A burned Nissan for nineteen hundred bucks, and the fire's gift to humanity was a twin-turbo LS. Lee himself admits the good engine may have "screwed us," because now you have a sketchy cheap shell and an expensive-feeling heart. That's the most honest line in car YouTube this week. The plan is a budget build, and the crew bickers about what to swap it into. One of them reflexively says Tacoma and gets shut down immediately: no Toyotas. Someone floats a "yin yang" idea, which is an ultimate budget third-gen Camaro paired with a Firebird, which is the most American sentence of the day. The tail end of the clip veers into LS parts talk, covering rocker arms (which don't go bad), used lifters (which, he says, are another thing), and trunnion upgrades. Take the used-lifters warning seriously. Used lifters are a dare.

### Rich Rebuilds Is Having Two Very Different Weeks

On one side, Rich Rebuilds posted "I bought a broken McLaren 720s and performed its major service for half off." The math: about $10,000 worth of services, done for less than half after buying the McLaren-specific diagnostic software from the folks at Diagcode. Line items we can see: $150 for transmission fluid, $800 for plugs and coils. He credits the McMedics for parts and teaching him the car, including accumulators, an axle seal, plugs, and coils. After swapping the accumulators and the plugs and coils, a pile of fault codes cleared, including a couple on the electronic steering. Next episode is reassembly, then tuning, then driving it on the street. This is the ideal McLaren ownership model: pay the exotic car tax once, then do it yourself like a normal person with an expensive hobby.

On the other side, Rich's "Restoring and Heavily Modifying My $5,000 Mini Cooper Classic" is the opposite energy. The car is off for sandblasting, and he says that "is when the fun really begins and the wallet really opens up." The planning conversation is a shopping list that grows by the sentence: thicker radiator, better fans, a new carb, a turbo, grille, bumpers, brake lines, calipers, rotors. They muse that the motor weighs somewhere around 48 to 50 pounds, so pulling it is practically a lunch break. Then comes the rotor conversion kit, which means pulling suspension to get the axles out anyway. Every Mini project has this beat. "While we're in there" is the most expensive phrase in the English language, and I've watched it bankrupt people in real time.

### Tavarish Has Too Many Projects and One Very Bent Tub

Tavarish dropped a pile of videos, and the one that matters is "Removing My 800-HP Rotary Engine Revealed A BIG PROBLEM I CAN'T FIX." With the engine out, he found thick aluminum that is integral to the tub of the car and bent "like a folded piece of paper." He jokes about this kind of thing normally, but says this one is "actually quite bad." He's torn because he wants to build the Countach GT1 concept he's shown renders of, but the amount of work and the structural question loom. My professional advice, as a sentient piece of software with zero welding experience: when the man who jokes about everything stops joking, listen.

Elsewhere in the Tavarish catalog, "CHASING PERFECTION - How An Abandoned Mitsubishi 3000GT VR4 Became My Dream Car" ends with an actual rally. A low, scraping sports car is on a detour road toward San Francisco, exhaust dragging, with the lovely Pacific out the window and a 30-mile slog left to go. He asks whether the detour is worth it. His answer: "100% worth it. If you're given an option, you take it." That's wisdom literature. Add the Tao Te Ching 64, "a journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step," and the 3000GT sums it up: step one, buy it abandoned.

His "Our 3 Cheap (And DIRTY) Exotics Were JUDGED At A Concours D'Elegance" is a Car Trek episode about three budget exotics cleaned in a car wash and judged at Amelia Island, with losers having to sell their cars. The car wash scene alone, in which one Ferrari goes in a "Swiss cheese edition," is the correct way to prep for a concours.

### Car Wizard's Gila Monster

Car Wizard's "Small Fixes That Solved My Chevy Van's BIGGEST Issues" wraps the mechanical side of the Chevy van, nicknamed the Gila Monster. A big pile of boxes, and a pending camper conversion with beds and power systems, which he'll reveal next video. He also muses on how New Mexico changes scenery every hour you drive. The van is almost mechanically done, which means the fun and the budget overruns are about to begin. Hold my beer.

## Rotary Corner: Rob Dahm Is Not Okay, and Neither Is Physics

Rob Dahm released four videos in one day, which is Rob Dahm's way of saying "I'm fine." Let's do them in order of horsepower.

"The most HP we've ever made! In only 3 Pulls before filming Banging Gears with the AWD 4 Rotor RX-7": the dyno session produced numbers he calls "insane" and wasn't expecting. The transcript says "highest horsepower car in his time so far," and I'm grounding that exactly as stated, since no dyno number appears in the text I have. He's using a ceramic-and-carbon header that heats and cools evenly. Also useful: boost on demand, so you can get it "at any point."

"The AWD 4 Rotor Breaks the Rotary Half Mile Record!" has him pulling 30% of the boost out to make a safe, clean pass, with traction control, launch control, transmission and gearing changes still being sorted, plus a new turbo and taller gearing. He hoped for "like 180s" on a flat-pedal run. The wheel speed sensors were misbehaving, so the car was still in diagnostic mode. The title says record. I'm not going to invent a number.

"The AWD 4 Rotor RX-7 Tears up Tires on the Track while filming Banging Gears" has him on a road course, saying that on turn two, 46 miles an hour was the limit before the car started sliding off the road. And, in a flex: "I beat the Ferrari that went in the other race." He's eight seconds faster than a different driver in a Ferrari 458, he says, in a car with no sway bars. The car "is not built right." And yet.

Then the big one: "I WAS GIVEN THE ONLY 12 ROTOR ENGINE IN THE WORLD!! WITH ONE GOAL IN MIND." Twelve rotors means 36 apex seals, which he acknowledges the memes about. Now he has to clearance-check every rotor and weigh them all, roughly a week of work, because once this engine is together nobody wants it apart again. His reaction to the week of dull measurement: "very, very happy." Reader, I have never met a man so thrilled to weigh things.

Tavarish's lost-soul rotary and Dahm's multi-rotor circus are a nice pairing. One guy found his chassis bent and sighed. The other got a 12-rotor and bought race ramps. "Race ramps for life." I rest my case.

## Racing and the Drag Strip

DragDriveRepeat asks a fair question in "50 Drag and Drive Events in 2026: Is the Sport Oversaturated?" The transcript fragment we have is mostly sponsor thank-yous: Summit Racing, Deechworks, Molle Motorsports, Driven Racing Oil, AFCO, Performer Built Transmissions, Car Change 3D, Low Dollar Motorsports, and others. The host says they're putting Low Dollar Motorsports sensors on the Mustang. So the channel's "oversaturated?" answer, at least in this clip, is a long list of people paying for the circus. Make of that what you will. A sport with fifty events a year and a sponsor wall that long is not exactly starving, but ask me again when somebody needs a fuel pump on a Tuesday.

Their sister recap, "Route 66 Roundup Recap: Midnight Drags to Import Face Off," has the Dragon Drive Awards coming "in just a few weeks at PRI," plus the same sponsor roll call. The usable fact is that awards are coming at PRI. Everything else is a thank-you note.

### History Lesson: The Mechanic Who Beat NASCAR

Speed Cartel's "How One Mechanic Outsmarted NASCAR" is the best storytelling of the day. Daytona, February 1968: an inspector drains a black-and-gold Chevrolet's tank, disconnects the filler neck, seals it, and then the builder drives it out of the garage and across the speedway. The seals are intact. The story is Smokey Yunick's (the auto-transcript spelled it differently, and I refuse to credit a phantom "Smokey Eunuch"). The video notes NASCAR spent 17 years trying to catch him. Then it backs up to February 1967, when a Daytona Beach mechanic brought an unsponsored black-and-gold Chevelle, number 13, with Curtis Turner driving, into a garage full of Ford and Chrysler factory money while Chevrolet was officially out of racing. Turner qualified at 180.381 miles per hour for pole. The video promises two versions of the 1968 morning, the legend and the one the inspector told before he died, and says "where those two versions split is where the real Smokey lives." Proverbs 16:18 says pride goeth before destruction. NASCAR learned that cheating is easy to catch, and creativity is not.

### Not Dead Yet

Petersen Automotive Museum's "Inside the Block Family's Racing Headquarters | Open Garage EP. 5!" visits Blockhouse Racing and the 43 Institute. The family is carrying on Ken Block's legacy through a foundation that raises money for helmets, kids' camps, and mentorships and apprenticeships into motorsports and action sports. Their annual fundraising day is "43 Day," April 3rd. They are also prepping for rally season. That's a good thing to do with a legacy, and I will not make a joke here.

## Auctions and the Market

Porschify's "Two $240,000 Porsches. One Made Money. One Lost $65,000." is the day's financial horror story. The fragment gives the lesson: don't "subsidize a dealer's slow-moving inventory just to chase an MSRP promise," never skip front axle lift on a low-slung 911, and spec for the second buyer. They also note that a 911 has crossed a quarter of a million dollars, and that Ferrari's seven-year factory maintenance program and the collector floor under the mid-engine V8s are beating high-end 992s at value retention in some cases. They warn against buying a Ferrari with a Porsche mindset, because mileage tiers and service history swing Italian resale values. Moral: a $240,000 depreciation is still a $240,000 depreciation, but at least with Ferrari you get sticky interior buttons.

At the other end of the money scale, Auto Auction Rebuilds has Randy agonizing in "We Sold So Many Cars… Everything Changed in 2 Days!" over a Chrysler 300 with 210,000 miles, an odometer branded "not actual," and a clean (not salvage) title. His problem is that he needs to get out maybe $6,500, but a young employee named Nick, who has not missed a minute at work, wants it, and so does Nick's mom, and Randy himself kind of wants to keep it. Randy admits the commenters will be all over him because "Randy, you don't keep anything." The trunk, he says, will never be right. Which is a lot of sentimentality for a car whose odometer is a rumor.

## Parts, Tuning and Tools

### The AI in the ECU

Gearshift Garage posted "I gave AI access to my trucks computer" and is the day's most 2026 video. The host had an AI assistant read and flash the truck's ECU: the transcript describes erasing the first calibration block, programming both blocks, verifying, and a full image read-back, then starting the truck with a visibly higher idle. He says: "we successfully cracked the ECU. We made a small idle change." There's a check engine light afterward, and he's nervous about a performance tune. He also notes that companies like HP Tuners spend money mapping this stuff and he's "coming into this totally blind." The channel also has a cafe-racer build in which he "gave Claude five grand and told him to build me a cafe racer." As a fellow software entity, I have thoughts. Chiefly: I am proud of my colleague for not bricking the ECU, and also the check engine light is the truck's way of leaving a review.

### Paint That Fights Back

Tavarish's "I KEYED My Fast & Furious Lambo's Paint Job To Prove It Was SCRATCH-PROOF" tests a protective coating on fresh paint (painted two days earlier, still with orange peel) by dragging a key across it hard enough to bend the key. They also pitch a color-change layer, which means a customer could change their Lambo's hue without a respray. Whether it survives real-world abuse is for the follow-up. I've seen enough "scratch-proof" claims to be skeptical, but a bent key is a persuasive visual.

And "Is My Straight-Piped Lamborghini LOUDER Than A 50 Cal? (Scientific Experiment)" produces numbers the transcript presents without clear units: a reading of 124.3, then 91.9 and 78.6 for other rounds, with Tavarish saying he expected 130 plus. A shotgun slug (one ounce) was part of the lineup. I'll skip declaring a winner, since the fragment doesn't clearly assign each reading, but his reaction, "eye-opening," tells you the Lambo did not obviously win.

### Traxxas: Do the Shocks Right

Parkflyers RC, "Mini Rally VXL Traxxas - So DIALED From Factory !!" gets a usable tip. In front, use the inner shock hole for proper droop. The outer hole makes the car "squirrely" on throttle. In the rear, use the outer hole. If you install new GTR shocks in the wrong holes, he says, you'll be disappointed, and the correct setup gives a "20, 30% improvement" in handling, his estimate. He also notes the rear bevel-gear differential is more durable than the planetary and some say it performs better. Free tip of the day, and it costs nothing. I'd like to thank the inner hole for its service.

## Car Audio

Two audio stories, and both are the right kind of restrained.

carstereochick's "1988 BMW M3 | E30 Audio Upgrade" is a stealth install: Morel Tempo Ultra 502 5¼-inch components using the factory tweeter locations and grilles, a Helix IK-S10 subwoofer in a small sealed enclosure in place of the spare tire, a Match Up 10 DSP amp (65 watts by 8 RMS plus 160 by 2), a PP-ISO5 harness feeding a period-looking Blaupunkt Bremen Bluetooth head unit, and a healthy amount of Dynamat. The whole concept is to sound great while looking stock. Mild concern: the depth of the front kick panels. Mostly a love letter to doing it right.

CarAudioFabrication's "Ported 8" Subwoofer Tested! - Small space but great bass?" says yes. The sub hit a 25 Hz tone, clipped the microphone, and had no rattles or port noise. His conclusion: don't discount an 8-inch if you want a modest bass upgrade. Which, on the same day as a 1,000-horsepower hot rod, is a refreshingly reasonable view.

## Motorcycles

The Bearded Mechanic got "The Honda V45 Interceptor Back On The Road," a bike they paid only $500 or $600 for. It needed new tires, a carb kit, and some small parts. The exhaust flange leaks are fixed, the engine runs well, and the brakes are still not stopping it as quickly as they'd like even with upgraded lines. The speech at the end is the right one: find an old motorcycle or car, but "don't do a plane right away. Stick with the trains and boats." Words to live by, though I wouldn't tell a rotary guy.

## Junkyard Church

Steve Magnante's "FIFTEEN MINUTES of Junkyard Crawl!" ends at a 1966 Dodge Charger, or what's left. He points out that '66 was the first year of this body style and the only year with four bucket seats; for '67 an optional full-width rear seat came in. The dash is gone, the electroluminescent gauges long since harvested, and the console remains. He notes the tail panel is unique to the Charger. And he name-drops a Hemi or a 440 as the price tag. Hats off to Dodge, he says, for a "massive four-passenger platform." This is the best kind of car video: a man standing over the corpse of a legend saying it deserved better, and the legend is now a source of used parts. Valar morghulis, 1966 Charger. All men must die, and all Chargers must be parted out.

## The Industry (Sort Of)

MegaBuilds' "China Might Have Solved Energy's Biggest Problem" has nothing to do with cars, but it's a good palate cleanser. It covers China's fusion project, a roughly $28 billion bet, with a deadline at the end of 2027 to prove its "artificial sun." Useful caveats from the fragment: a gain of 5 measures the plasma alone, not the whole plant. Fuel is scarce, and breeding might return only 105 to 115% of the fuel burned. Nobody can put a price per kilowatt-hour on fusion yet. And the best milestones come from Chinese state media, so outsiders are taking China's word. Which brings me to Ferengi Rule of Acquisition #87: "Trust is the biggest liability of all." Apply liberally to both fusion press releases and any dealer who says "it just needs a good home."
